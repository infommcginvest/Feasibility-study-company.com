SBA Feasibility Studies

Independent, lender-grade feasibility studies for SBA 7(a) and 504 loans, built to SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, in force for applications received by SBA on or after October 1, 2026. The new edition moved acquisition underwriting from projections to trailing earnings, lifted the coverage floors, fixed the equity rules and reset the fee schedule. This page sets out what changed, what the rules now require, and where the market analysis fits in the credit.

What Changed

Three texts in sixteen months, and only the last one counts.

SBA issued SOP 50 10 8.1 on August 14, 2026, then replaced it on September 25 with a Technical Policy Updates version before either took effect. The August text never governed a loan, yet most summaries still in circulation describe it. The table reads the three editions side by side; the right-hand column is the rule a lender applies today.

Appendix 15

Acquisitions now close on last year's numbers.

Every 7(a) change of ownership falls into one of four categories, and each carries a coverage floor measured on the acquired business's own history rather than the buyer's forecast.

Change-of-ownership categories and their debt service coverage floors under Appendix 15.
CategoryWho qualifiesCoverage floor
Initial AcquisitionA new majority or largest owner who was not an owner before, or an employee of fewer than 24 months. The default category.1.25:1
Business ExpansionAn operating business with two full fiscal years under current ownership buying another in the same four-digit NAICS group.1.15:1
Owner BuyoutExisting owners, or employees of at least 24 months, buying out a departing owner. Outsiders may take less than half and may not become the largest holder.1.25:1
ESOP and CooperativeAn employee trust or cooperative acquiring a controlling interest of 51% or more.1.25:1

Appendix 15, Paragraphs A and C of SOP 50 10 8.1. Source 1.

The coverage ratio is historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two. Add-backs are allowed when the lender justifies each one, including rent where the owner-occupied real estate is part of the deal, and an adjusted ratio still has to clear a 1:1 global test.

The projection rule is the hinge of the whole edition. A lender must still evaluate the buyer's post-closing forecast, but projections are reviewed but cannot meet the floor, except an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports, where the floor may be met on projections within two years. Where a deal has no clear continuity of operations, the lender may underwrite it as a start-up instead, which brings the start-up equity and projection rules with it. Seller debt that is not on full standby counts in the debt service, and an interest-only seller note is tested as if it amortized over ten years. Seller earnouts are prohibited; a rebate the buyer earns must pay down the loan; a working-capital true-up in the purchase agreement is not a rebate.

Unlimited sources are unborrowed cash, a personal loan repaid from outside the business, an unconditional grant, and verified prepaid project expenses. Limited sources are seller debt on full standby for the life of the loan, other full-standby debt, and a non-controlling minority investor holding under 20%; together they may supply no more than half of the injection. education, advisory and Agent fees are not equity, while the out-of-pocket cost of the business valuation and QoE can count toward it. A seller note written into an acquisition can be refinanced only after 36 months in place and current, and in an Initial Acquisition or Business Expansion a seller may stay on as a consultant for at most 24 months in aggregate, extensions included.

Financial Due Diligence

The valuation sets the ceiling; the quality of earnings sets the cash flow.

Every change of ownership needs a business valuation, and the number that drives the thresholds is the the purchase-agreement price less the appraised value of owner-occupied commercial real estate, which the SOP calls the Business Purchase Price. At $350,000 or less the lender may value the business itself, unless buyer and seller are closely related. Above that, an independent Qualified Source prepares the valuation for the lender; a report prepared for the buyer or the seller cannot be used. For a Special Purpose Property the valuation is a going-concern appraisal by a Certified General appraiser with at least four comparable assignments in the last 36 months, allocating value among land, building, equipment and intangibles.

Total acquisition debt, including any seller note not on full standby, is capped at the valuation; a price above it must be bridged with equity. At a Business Purchase Price of $3 million or more, an Initial Acquisition or Business Expansion also needs a Quality of Earnings report with a cash proof covering the trailing twelve months plus the last two fiscal years. Exempt from the QoE: Owner Buyout, ESOP & Cooperative, and any acquisition of an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property. A report the buyer commissioned is usable when one of the lender's approved vendors reviews it and the review is in the file; never a report prepared by or for the seller. The lender must then run the coverage test on the QoE's earnings, not the seller's.

Special Purpose Property is defined as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built. SBA's example list is not closed, and it names car washes, gas stations, hotels and other lodging, marinas, nursing and assisted living, funeral homes with crematoriums, medical facilities and service centers with pits among its entries. Self-storage, restaurants and day care are not on the list, although Appendix 15 uses storage units and hotels as its own examples of special purpose acquisitions that the real estate fully secures.

7(a) Small and SBA Express

Back in the acquisition market, on the same floors.

7(a) Small loans run to $350,000 and SBA Express to $500,000. The August text barred both from changes of ownership; the Technical Policy Updates restored them, subject to Appendix 15. Three things differ for a small acquisition: the lender may do its own valuation when the Business Purchase Price is $350,000 or less, the fully-secured test need not reach other commercial real estate the owners hold, and nothing else is relaxed. The 1.25x Initial Acquisition floor applies at every loan size.

Outside acquisitions, a 7(a) Small loan is underwritten to 1.10:1 coverage, historical or projected, plus the two most recent months of business bank statements; projections must reach 1.10x within one year; does not apply to changes of ownership. The SBSS credit score that once screened these loans is gone from the text; SBA retired it for loans approved on or after March 1, 2026. A Standard 7(a) loan above $350,000 is underwritten to 1.15:1, historical and/or projected, with 1:1 global coverage; start-ups reach 1.15x within two years.

Loan Limits

$10 million across the two programs, in the right order.

Since July 4, 2026, a borrower's outstanding 7(a) balance no longer reduces its 504 capacity. A business may carry up to $5 million of 7(a) debt and, with the 7(a) loan approved first and the 504 project approved second, up to $5 million of 504 debentures, or $5.5 million per project for a small manufacturer or an energy project. Policy Notice 5000-879058, now integrated into 8.1.

For a capital-intensive operator the sequence is the point: working capital, equipment and the business itself on 7(a), the facility on 504, each underwritten to its own coverage floor. A single 504 project may also bundle several fixed assets, a building and a production line financed at the same time, within the debenture limit.

Application File

Ninety days on the personal financial statement.

Each owner of 20% or more and each guarantor signs a personal financial statement dated within 90 days of submission, on both the 7(a) and 504 sides. Business and interim financial statements may be up to 120 days old, and an acquisition file carries the seller's statements for the last three fiscal years, verified against IRS transcripts.

Where the Study Fits

The regulation says "may require." The underwriting says what for.

Under 13 CFR 120.160(b), SBA may require a feasibility study. The authority is discretionary, and nothing in SOP 50 10 8.1 turns it into a mandate. What the new edition changes is the question the study has to answer.

An acquisition now closes on historical EBITDA, so the market analysis in a change-of-ownership file explains trailing performance: whether last year's revenue was the trade area's doing or the seller's, whether the customer base and margins survive the handover, and whether the adjusted earnings the Quality of Earnings report produces are the ones the market will keep paying. A forecast that cannot carry the coverage test is still worth testing, because the lender must evaluate it, but it no longer decides the credit.

Start-ups, ground-up construction and expansions still depend on projections, and so does the owner-occupied special purpose acquisition the SOP carves out, where the appraised value supports the price and the lender may rely on a two-year projection instead of history. For those credits the study is the evidence under the projected coverage ratio, and under 8.1 the lender must file an analysis of the variance between what the business earned and what the buyer expects. That variance analysis is market work.

  • Initial Acquisition on historyMarket analysis of trailing performance, customer and margin durability, and the add-backs the coverage test relies on.
  • Special purpose acquisition on projectionsDemand, capture and a two-year projection reconciled to the business's own history, with the variance explained.
  • Start-up or ground-up buildIndependent demand and capture evidence for a projection that must reach 1.15x within two years.
  • 504 projectHistorical coverage at 1.15x, or two years of justified projections and Year 1 liquidity where history falls short.
SBA Questions

SBA feasibility questions under SOP 50 10 8.1.

Does an SBA loan require a feasibility study?

Not by regulation. 13 CFR 120.160(b) says SBA may require one, and SOP 50 10 8.1 does not add a mandate. Lenders order a study where repayment depends on a projection, which under 8.1 means start-ups, construction, expansions and the special purpose acquisitions the SOP lets close on projections.

Which SOP governs my application?

SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates governs applications received by SBA on or after October 1, 2026. Applications submitted through September 30, 2026 stay under SOP 50 10 8. The August 2026 text of 8.1 never took effect.

Can an acquisition be underwritten on projections?

Only in two cases. An owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports may meet its coverage floor on a two-year projection, and a deal with no continuity of operations may be underwritten as a start-up. Every other change of ownership must clear 1.25x (or 1.15x for a Business Expansion) on the acquired business's trailing EBITDA.

Is a restaurant or a self-storage facility special purpose property?

Neither appears on SBA's example list, which names car washes, gas stations, lodging, marinas, nursing and assisted living, funeral homes with crematoriums, medical facilities and service centers with pits among its entries. The list is not closed, and Appendix 15 itself uses storage units as an example of a special purpose acquisition the real estate fully secures, so the lender's classification decides.

What does the FY2027 fee waiver cover?

A 0% 7(a) upfront fee on loans of $700,000 or less, and both 504 fees waived at any size, for manufacturers, the listed food supply chain codes and businesses in a county at least 30 percent rural, on loans approved through September 30, 2027.
