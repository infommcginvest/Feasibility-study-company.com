Lubbock is the service center of the South Plains: a university town, a regional medical hub and the market town for a wide farm economy. The employer evidence is dated but consistent. HUD's 2022 market analysis, reporting 2018 data, ranks Texas Tech University first with 6,635 employees, with the City of Lubbock at 2,700 and Lubbock County at 1,355 [^2]. The Dallas Fed names Covenant Health and UMC Health System as the major health care employers and describes Texas Tech and agriculture as the two forces that run the plains economy [^3]. The research holds no current ranked list, so a study that leans on any one employer should obtain its present headcount.

The state is adding to the health care base. In June 2024 the Governor announced a 50-bed maximum-security psychiatric unit in Lubbock, budgeted at $121 million, roughly $2.4 million a bed by simple division [^4]. A public project of that kind brings clinical and security jobs, construction spending and visiting-family traffic, but it is one project, and its opening date should be confirmed before a study counts its staff as demand for housing, lodging or medical office space.

Agriculture is the part of the base the research could not quantify. The Dallas Fed profile describes the High Plains around Lubbock as major cotton country [^3], and the research holds no farm income, acreage or price figure for the region. That matters to lenders in two ways. Retail, fuel, equipment dealers and processors in the small towns around Lubbock rise and fall with crop years, so a study for those uses should show results in a weak harvest year as well as a strong one. And agricultural processing projects are the ones most likely to use USDA programs, where the feasibility study rules are stricter.

Lubbock's base differs from its siblings'. A Corpus Christi study rests on a port and two military installations; an El Paso study works in a market being reshaped by one data center campus. Lubbock has neither. Its demand is broad and slow-moving, which lowers the risk of a sudden shock and raises the importance of measuring competition, because there is no new wave of demand to absorb new supply.

FRED's copy of the Census Bureau estimates puts the Lubbock metro at 368,431 residents on July 1, 2025, against 364,664 a year before, a gain of 3,767 [^1]. The Dallas Fed reports that Lubbock employment grew 8% from 2016 through 2023, half the state's 16% over the same years [^3]. That is the profile of a market that adds customers every year but never fast enough to forgive an oversized project.

Three numbers a lender would want are missing. No 2020 Census count for the metro was in hand, so no five-year rate is given. The research's city population was filed against the wrong city's Census page, so it is dropped. And the payroll figure from the Texas Workforce Commission's August 2026 review could not be checked against the report itself. A study should take all three straight from the Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics, with their dates.

Steady growth suits some property types better than others. Uses that serve every household, such as child care, medical office, grocery and everyday retail, can grow with the metro if the study shows the subject's trade area gaining residents. Uses where supply ran ahead of demand, storage first among them, need a share case, not a growth case. A study that projects a Lubbock storage facility to fill at the pace of a fast-growing suburb, or a hotel to ramp as if the metro were adding thousands of households a year, has borrowed its assumptions from the wrong market.

Texas Tech shapes more Lubbock projects than its payroll suggests. As the largest employer on HUD's list [^2] it supplies faculty and staff households, but its students, visitors and athletic events drive demand for apartments, restaurants, hotels and storage in ways a population count does not capture. The research holds no current enrollment, student-bed or game-day figure, so each must be sourced from the university or the city before a study relies on it.

Student demand has three traits a lender should see handled. It is seasonal: leases, storage rentals and restaurant traffic follow the academic year, with a summer trough and an August peak. It is concentrated: purpose-built student housing competes block by block near campus, and a property a mile further out competes on price. And it depends on the university's enrollment rather than on the regional economy, so a study should show what the subject's occupancy does if enrollment flattens. A student housing study needs the university's enrollment history, its on-campus bed count and any planned residence halls; a multifamily study near campus should separate student renters from the rest of the market.

Event demand works the same way for lodging. A hotel near campus may sell out on football weekends and at graduation, then run far below that mid-week in summer. Annual averages hide the pattern. A study should show occupancy and rate by month and name the events behind the peaks, so the lender can see how much of the year depends on a handful of dates and what happens to coverage if one of them moves.

Self-storage is where Lubbock stands out. RentCafe's February 2026 report, built on Yardi Matrix data, counted 17.1 square feet of storage per Lubbock resident, more than twice the report's national benchmark of 7. Street rates fell 7.4% from a year earlier, to $100, the second-largest decline among the cities RentCafe tracks, and another 196,377 square feet were expected to open in 2026, adding 4% to the year-end 2025 stock [^5].

That combination, high stock, falling rates and more space on the way, is what an oversupplied market looks like, and it sets the bar for any new self-storage or RV and boat storage loan. A study cannot rest on the metro's population growth. It has to draw the subject's own trade area, count every facility in it with its unit mix and posted rates, show where the subject is better located or better built, and project lease-up at rates that keep falling for at least the first year. Break-even occupancy matters more here than stabilized occupancy; our note on the break-even occupancy trap explains why, and the note on why the trade area is the study covers how to draw it.

Car washes show consolidation rather than a glut. Mister Car Wash reported buying five Whistle Express sites in Lubbock in its third-quarter 2025 results [^6]. A new express car wash in Lubbock competes against a national operator with membership pricing and marketing scale, so the study should count that operator's sites near the subject and test the subject's membership base against them.

For apartments, lodging, warehouses, shopping centers, senior living, campgrounds and c-stores the research has no Lubbock number at all. HUD's 2022 analysis is the last federal market reading [^2], and its housing figures were not extracted for this page, so every one of those classes starts from a fresh count of competitors.

The research names no Lubbock corridor or interchange and holds no traffic count for the metro. For a retail, fuel, car wash or quick-service site, the study has to supply that evidence itself from TxDOT's public annual average daily traffic dataset, citing the station number, the count year and a three-year trend for the frontage and the nearest major intersection [^11].

Traffic in a university city also has a calendar. Counts taken in summer understate the school-year flow near campus, and counts taken during football weekends overstate it. A study for a site that relies on student or game-day traffic should say when its counts were taken and adjust, rather than presenting a single annual average as the whole story. Farm-to-market traffic has a harvest season of its own for sites on the roads into the surrounding counties.

The research documents none of the local rules a Lubbock study usually needs: no hotel tax rate, impact fee, recent zoning change, short-term rental rule or utility capacity limit. That is a gap in the record, not evidence that no rules apply. A lodging study must take the current combined hotel tax rate from the city; a site study must confirm the zoning district, the permitted use and any specific-use approval in writing; and every project should carry a will-serve letter from the city's water and wastewater utility.

Water deserves particular care. The research holds no Lubbock water figure, no supply plan and no drought rule, so a study for a water-heavy use, such as a car wash, laundromat, food processor or hotel, should document the source of supply, the utility's capacity commitment and any drought-stage rules in force when the study is dated.

Lubbock falls in the SBA's West Texas District, which covers 71 counties including Lubbock, Midland, Ector, Potter, Randall and Taylor [^9]. The district's fiscal 2025 total in the SBA regional report, as El Paso Inc. relayed it, was $127 million, probably a third-quarter year-to-date figure, which placed it fourth among Texas's six districts [^8]. Those dollars span Lubbock, the Permian Basin, Amarillo and Abilene, so they say little about Lubbock lending on its own.

The research could not find a current lender ranking for the district. The latest it retrieved is a 2018 SBA recognition of Southwest Bank of Odessa as a top-volume lender in the region [^10], which is too old to guide a borrower today. One recent sign of who lends in the area is a Ready Capital case summary describing an SBA 7(a) loan for a gas station and convenience store adding a liquor store in Idalou, in Lubbock County [^7]; the summary gives no closing date, so it shows that national non-bank SBA lenders work in the county's small towns, not when.

Program rules shape storage loans in particular. Self-storage is not on the SBA's special purpose list, so a 504 loan for an existing storage business needs 10% equity, rising to 15% for a new business, and a 7(a) start-up needs 10% under SOP 50 10 8.1 [^17]. The rules do not change the market: in a market with falling rates, a lender should size the loan on the study's lease-up, not on the equity minimum. For program mechanics see SBA 7(a) and 504; for a bank loan outside SBA, see conventional lending.

Program eligibility around Lubbock is undocumented in the research: no rural determination, zone tract or HUBZone status was found for any address. The lookups below decide which programs a project can use.

  • USDA Business and Industry. The city of Lubbock exceeds the program's 50,000-person limit [^13]. Towns such as Idalou and the communities of Crosby and Lynn counties may qualify, and USDA's OneRD lookup settles each address [^12]. USDA raised the guarantee to 85% for projects under $5 million in March 2026 [^13], and when a new business borrows more than $1,000,000 under a B&I guarantee, the rule at 7 CFR 5001.306(a)(3)(i) makes an independent feasibility study mandatory [^14]. Agricultural processors and rural retail are the natural users; see USDA Rural Development.
  • Opportunity Zones. Under the federal timetable the Governor's office publishes, new-round designations began in July 2026 and should be certified by late November 2026 [^15]. A study should state whether the tract was a 2018 zone and whether it is in the new round.
  • HUBZone. Status is read from the SBA's map by address; that map's tracts are next revised in July 2028 [^16].
