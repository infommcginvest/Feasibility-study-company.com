A major-branded station sells gasoline under a refiner's flag, bought through a fuel supply agreement, with a convenience store on the same pad. It earns money two ways: gallons multiplied by a cents-per-gallon margin, and inside sales multiplied by their gross margin. No source the research could find counts branded sites nationally, publishes brand fees, or reports revenue per branded site. That gap matters for the loan, because it means the brand's economics live only in the contract the borrower signs. A study for a branded station has to read that contract, not assume a national norm for it.

Fuel carries most of the dollars and less than half of the profit. NACS puts fuel at 65.0% of sales at the average fueling store in 2025 but only 38.8% of gross profit [^15]. What remains after the dealer pays to sell each gallon is smaller again. In its own cost waterfall NACS puts card fees at 8.4 cents a gallon on 2023 data and the net margin after retail expenses at about 13 cents, a pretax figure it also describes as 10 to 15 cents a gallon [^2]. That net figure is the one that services debt, and a pro forma that quotes only gross margin hides how little room there is.

The gross margins public operators report sit far above that line and moved sharply in one year. ARKO reported a retail fuel margin of 42.8 cents a gallon for 2025 against 39.6 cents for 2024 [^1]. Both are real results, and a study can defend either as evidence. It cannot defend choosing the higher one without saying so. EIA attributes the 2026 jump in pump prices to a Middle East supply disruption and expects prices to ease in 2027 [^5], which is the condition under which wide retail margins tend to narrow.

What a lender should ask for

Three documents settle the margin question for a branded site: the supply agreement's pricing formula (rack-based, posted dealer tank wagon or another basis), twelve months of the dealer's fuel invoices if the store operates, and the posted prices of the stations the study names as competitors. With those, the study can show the margin the site actually earns against the margin the public companies report. Without them, the base case should sit at or below the 2024 operator level, and the coverage table should show what happens at the NACS net margin rather than the gross.

The volume side of a branded pro forma rests on driving, and driving is close to flat. FHWA counted vehicle-miles up 0.9% in 2025, an added 29.8 billion miles [^3]. The year before, Americans drove 3.279 trillion miles, up 1.0% [^4]. Growth below one percent a year spread over every station in the country adds almost nothing to any one forecourt. A new branded store therefore gains gallons by taking them from the stations within its trade area, and the study's job is to show which stations lose them and why.

Price moves faster than volume. EIA put the U.S. retail gasoline price at $3.31 a gallon for 2024 and reported $4.35 for September 2026 [^5]. Its October outlook carried Brent crude at $96 a barrel for 2026, $105 in the fourth quarter of 2026 and $84 for 2027, with retail gasoline easing from $3.91 in 2026 to $3.56 in 2027 [^5]. A pro forma dated in a $4 month will show higher sales dollars and higher card costs per gallon than one dated at $3, while the gallons themselves may fall. The study should state the price its revenue line assumes and show the 2027 path beside it, so the reviewer can see whether coverage depends on a price level EIA expects to pass.

Circle K's range excludes land and still runs past $9 million at the top; Holiday's includes land and tops out lower. The difference is a reminder that an Item 7 range describes a whole system, from small-town pads to large highway stores, and says little about one site. A branded dealer under a refiner's program, rather than a convenience franchise, faces image costs for canopy, fascia and signage that no source in the research priced. The research also found no store size, acreage or staffing norm for branded sites. A lender should therefore ask for the specific system's current Item 7, the brand's image specification, and a contractor's bid for the subject, and should treat a national range as a test of reasonableness only.

Two items in these ranges deserve their own line in the sources and uses. Plans and specifications at $85,000 to $180,000 [^6] are soft costs a lender sometimes finds folded into contingency. Opening fuel inventory, put at $100,000 to $300,000 in Holiday's document [^6], is working capital that the brand's credit terms may or may not cover; the supply agreement says which.

Brands are also adding chargers to existing sites with public money. Phillips 66 opened a station near I-70 in Casey, Illinois with four DC fast charging ports rated up to 350 kW, supported by an $854,641 federal NEVI grant and built with Meyer Oil and Electrify America; the ribbon was cut on July 22, 2026 [^7]. Our EV charging page covers how a study treats that grant money, and the unbranded and independent page carries the cleanup cost evidence that applies when a branded store goes onto a former fuel site.

The first rule is about the contract. SOP 50 10 8 states that every agreement covered by the Petroleum Marketing Practices Act, which includes distributor, fuel supply, dealer and jobber agreements at gas stations, falls inside the FTC definition of a franchise [^9]. For a branded station that turns the supply agreement into an eligibility document. The lender will want to know its term against the loan term, whether it carries a minimum volume, what the dealer repays if the brand comes down early, and whether the agreement can be assigned to a lender or a buyer. A study should summarize those terms in plain language, because a coverage ratio built on a contract that expires in year six of a 25-year loan is not the same credit as one built on a contract that runs with it.

The counterparty on the other side of that contract is consolidating. Sunoco closed its $9.1 billion purchase of Parkland on October 31, 2025; Parkland brought 650 retail outlets and 1,830 dealer sites, including 699 U.S. retail fuel locations, and the combination made Sunoco the largest independent fuel distributor in the Americas [^16]. In 2026 Sunoco followed with a $600 million purchase of Offen Petroleum and guided to $500 million of further bolt-on acquisitions [^17]. For a dealer, a merger upstream does not change the contract, but it can change how the supplier enforces it at renewal: image upgrades, volume minimums and the incentive repayment schedule. A study for a branded site should name who supplies the dealer today, who owns that supplier, and when the agreement comes up for renewal.

The second rule is about the property. Gas stations appear on SBA's example list of special purpose properties [^10], defined as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^10]. Under 504 the borrower contribution rises to 15% for special purpose property or a new business and to 20% when both apply [^10]. The program split is Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^10]. A borrower who already has a special purpose debenture outstanding contributes at the same 20% level on the next special purpose project, by a CDC's reading of 13 CFR 120.910 [^12]. That catches multi-site dealers adding a second branded store.

Under 7(a), a new branded station is a start-up: 10% equity and projected coverage that reaches 1.15x within two years [^10]. SOP 50 10 8 also calls for a going-concern appraisal of special purpose property by a certified general appraiser with recent experience on equivalent going-concern properties, with value allocated among land, building, equipment and intangibles [^9]; the research found that text only through a secondary quotation, so the appraiser should confirm it against SOP 50 10 8.1. The allocation is where the flag shows up: brand goodwill is intangible value, and it disappears if the supply agreement ends.

On the study itself, the rule is permissive, not mandatory: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^11]. Lenders ask for one on ground-up branded stations because there is no operating history to underwrite. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice page sets out how we build to the lender's file, and the note on special purpose property risk explains why the collateral class drives equity. Readers weighing a brand decision should also see franchise versus non-franchise and the c-store franchise category.

  1. The pro forma prices fuel at the peak

    A base case at 42.8 cents a gallon uses the better of two adjacent years [^1] in a period EIA describes as a supply shock [^5]. Reviewers increasingly ask for coverage at the lower year and at the net margin NACS publishes. A study that shows both, and still clears the floor, removes the question.

  2. The supply agreement ends before the loan does

    Ownership of the supplier can change mid-loan. Shell agreed on September 1, 2026 to buy the remaining two-thirds of Tri Star Energy, adding 320 retail sites and 552 dealer supply agreements, with closing expected by the end of 2026 [^8]. The dealers in that portfolio did not choose the change. A study should name the supplier, its parent, the contract term and the assignment clause, and flag any gap between contract term and loan term as a lender condition.

  3. The supplier keeps indemnity rights nobody waived

    Gas station loans begin with a Phase I environmental site assessment whatever the loan amount [^14], and an environmental questionnaire will not substitute [^13]. Any party holding environmental indemnification rights must waive and release SBA and the lender through SBA's indemnification agreement [^13]. At a branded site the supplier may hold those rights under the supply contract, which makes the waiver a branded-specific closing item.

  4. The brand is treated as demand

    A national flag does not create driving. With vehicle-miles up 0.9% in 2025 [^3], a new branded site wins volume only from nearby competitors. Files that cite the brand's reputation instead of a competitor census and a traffic count read as unsupported.

  5. The flag is valued as collateral

    The going-concern appraisal separates intangible value from land, building and equipment [^9]. If the loan sizing leans on a value that includes brand goodwill, the lender is relying on something that ends with the supply contract. The study should show coverage and loan-to-value on the tangible assets as well.

The clearest branded case is the Opelika, Alabama acquisition at I-85 Exit 64. The subject is an existing store selling a refiner's brand, bought as a change of ownership, and the exit already has two 24-hour branded competitors on the same frontage road. It shows the two branded questions together: whether the supply agreement transfers with the store, and whether historical cash flow under that agreement covers the new debt.

The Lockhart, Texas new-build case models a hypothetical branded store on US 183 against a field that includes two Valero-branded stores confirmed on the brand's own locator, one of them with a quick-service restaurant, plus Exxon-supplied and several brand-unresolved stations. The Cartersville, Georgia case at I-75 Exit 296 sits among Chevron, CITGO, Exxon and Texaco flags and two national travel centers, so brand alone does not separate the subject from its neighbors. The Lake Wales, Florida case on US 27 sets refiner-branded dealers (Shell, Chevron, CITGO, Sunoco, Marathon) against chains that sell under their own names, such as Love's, RaceTrac, Wawa and Murphy USA, which is the competition a refiner flag now meets on most suburban corridors. The Versailles, Ohio case shows the evidence problem at small scale: one village station appears under six different brands across directories, which is why every census row is checked against the brand's own store locator before it counts.
