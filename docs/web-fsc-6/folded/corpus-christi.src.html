Corpus Christi's base is military, municipal and maritime. HUD's 2024 market analysis ranks Naval Air Station Corpus Christi first among employers with 5,000 workers, followed by the City of Corpus Christi at 4,100, the Corpus Christi Army Depot at 2,900 and Corpus Christi Medical Center at 2,000; the fifth entry was not captured in the research [^1]. Two of the four largest employers are defense installations, which gives the metro a payroll that does not move with oil prices but does move with federal budgets and base decisions.

The port is the economic engine, and it is an energy engine. The Port of Corpus Christi reported 203.4 million tons moved through the ship channel in 2025, down 1.5% from 206.5 million in 2024. Exports of liquefied natural gas rose 15.4% to 18.6 million tons, while crude oil shipments fell 2.3% to 127.4 million tons [^2]. Crude still dominates the volume, so the port's activity follows crude oil markets first and the newer gas trade second.

Private investment around the port has been very large and very concentrated. The Texas A&M Real Estate Research Center counted nearly $60 billion of private capital placed in petrochemical and industrial sites near the port from 2012 through 2023, and found manufacturing jobs concentrated in San Patricio County ZIP codes and along the ship channel [^3]. That capital built plants, not payroll on the same scale: a refinery or export terminal typically employs far fewer people per dollar invested than a hospital or a distribution center.

For a lender the point is that port and plant activity creates demand in bursts. Construction and turnaround crews fill hotels, RV parks and restaurants for months, then leave; permanent plant staff are fewer and are paid well. A study for a lodging, fuel or service business should tie its demand to named terminal and plant projects with their schedules, and show the year after each burst ends. Tonnage alone is not a demand driver for a hotel. Compare the steadier, household-driven base on our Lubbock page, and the data center construction wave on the El Paso page, which raises the same temporary-demand question in a different industry.

No other Texas metro on this site has a documented drought record like Corpus Christi's in 2025 and 2026. The city held Stage 3 water restrictions from December 2024 for 596 days, stepping down to Stage 2 on August 4, 2026, when combined reservoir storage stood at 36.9%, with Lake Corpus Christi at 67.8% and Choke Canyon at 24.9% [^6]. On August 27, 2026 the City Manager moved the city to Stage 1 [^7]. By September 25, 2026 Lake Corpus Christi held 83.6%, against 15.7% a year earlier, and repairs to the Mary Rhodes Pipeline were complete [^8].

The rains bought time, not a new supply. In late July 2026 the city's projected date for a Level 1 Water Emergency stood at September 2028, after earlier projections of September 2026 in April and September 2027 in June [^9]. An April 2026 draft plan for that emergency proposed cutting water allocations by 25% to 30% for every customer class, large industrial users included; it was not adopted [^10].

The long-term fix has not been chosen. In May 2026 the Texas Tribune reported that the council had killed its own seawater desalination plan after the price nearly doubled to more than $1.2 billion, and had opened talks with AXE H2O, a Houston company proposing a plant of 150 million gallons a day at about $1.3 billion, with water at $6.50 per 1,000 gallons [^11]. On September 1, 2026 the council voted 5-3 to reject the Inner Harbor plant again, despite a preliminary guaranteed maximum price of $978.8 million [^12]. No desalination plant was confirmed as proceeding when the research closed in October 2026.

For a lender, water belongs in the base case, not the risk appendix. A car wash, hotel, laundromat, food processor or any industrial user should show operations and revenue under Stage 1, Stage 3 and the draft emergency cuts, with the stage rules for that use quoted from the city's drought contingency plan. The study should carry a will-serve letter, the utility's position on new connections, and the cost of any recycling or storage the project needs to stay open in Stage 3. A project whose cash flow fails at a 25% allocation cut is a project whose debt depends on rain.

The Census Bureau's estimate, carried in FRED, puts the Corpus Christi metro of Aransas, Nueces and San Patricio counties at 451,191 residents in 2025 [^4]. Lacking the 2020 census count for the three counties, this page computes no five-year change. The city-level population figures the research used rested on an encyclopedia entry and a Census page for another city; they are left out, and a study should pull the city's own Census figures.

The coast is where the growth evidence is. Rockport, in Aransas County, had 11,348 residents in the Census Bureau's 2025 estimate, up 12.7% from 10,068 in 2020, and a median household income of $72,174 in the 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey [^5]. Rockport is a resort and retiree town, and its growth supports RV parks, second homes, marinas and seasonal retail, all of it inside the windstorm pool's catastrophe area and exposed to the same drought rules as the rest of the region. For a lender the split matters: a project serving the city's households underwrites a flat customer base, while a coastal project underwrites growth that arrives with higher insurance costs and a seasonal swing.

The payroll count is also missing. The Texas Workforce Commission's August 2026 metro figure in the research came from indexed text of a report the research could not open, and it does not match the federal series, so this page gives no job-growth rate. The metro's nonfarm employment series from the Bureau of Labor Statistics is the figure a study should cite.

No road in the metro is named as a commercial corridor in the research, and no traffic count was found. Its one geographic finding is the employment map: manufacturing jobs cluster in San Patricio County ZIP codes and along the ship channel [^3]. Sites that serve plant workers and contractors, such as fuel stops, quick-service restaurants, extended-stay lodging and equipment yards, compete for that traffic on the routes between the plants and the places workers live.

Each site study therefore has to assemble its own traffic record from the TxDOT count stations nearest the site, giving station numbers and at least three years of counts [^18]. Near the port, truck share matters as much as total volume, and a study for a truck stop or fuel station should report heavy-vehicle counts where TxDOT publishes them. Plant turnarounds and construction schedules change those counts for months at a time, so the study should say whether its count year was a busy one.

Self-storage is the only class with a current reading. RentCafe's February 2026 data put Corpus Christi at 11.7 square feet of storage per resident, above the report's national benchmark of 7, with 89,150 square feet expected to open in 2026, about 2% of the year-end 2025 inventory [^15]. That is a heavier stock than El Paso carries and a lighter one than Lubbock, with a small pipeline. A self-storage study here has room to argue for demand, but only from a trade-area count; the coastal towns and the city's suburbs are different markets.

Beyond storage, the research is silent on Corpus Christi supply: no apartment vacancy, hotel occupancy, industrial vacancy, retail, senior housing, campground or c-store count. HUD's 2024 market analysis is the federal source a multifamily study should start from [^1]; this page does not reproduce its housing tables. A hotel or RV park study must build its competitive set from operators' own sites and separate the leisure season from the construction-crew demand described above.

Windstorm insurance is a fixed cost of building here. All three metro counties, Aransas, Nueces and San Patricio, lie in the first tier of the catastrophe area served by the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA) [^14], the zone where the state decided private wind and hail cover is not reasonably available. A Corpus Christi pro forma should carry a written windstorm quote, a flood determination and the construction standard the policy requires, because the premium and the building cost both follow from them. The association's statewide rate filings are summarized on our Texas page.

The hotel tax figure in the research is old. The Texas Comptroller reported a 9% city hotel occupancy tax for Corpus Christi in fiscal 2017, and any county or venue tax on top of it was not found [^13]. Before netting room revenue, a lodging study needs the combined rate in force today, confirmed with the city and the state Comptroller.

The drought stages are the third local rule, and they change faster than zoning. The research found nothing on impact fees, recent rezonings or short-term rental rules in the three counties; the study should confirm each in writing for the site, along with the stage rules that apply to the use.

Corpus Christi has no SBA district of its own. It falls in the Lower Rio Grande Valley District, run from Harlingen with a Corpus Christi virtual office; the partial county list the research found includes Aransas, Nueces and San Patricio [^17]. By El Paso Inc.'s reading of the SBA's FY2025 regional figures, the district's SBA volume reached $75 million, likely through the third quarter, fifth of six in Texas [^16]. The full-year district total and its top lender, a McAllen bank, are on our McAllen page; the research found no lender ranking specific to Corpus Christi.

The local lending picture matters because many of the metro's likely projects carry the SBA's special purpose label. Car washes, hotels and marinas all do, and that sets the 504 equity for an established business buying or building one at 15% and for a new business doing the same at 20%, under SOP 50 10 8.1 [^24]. For a lender, the question beyond the equity tier is whether the cash flow covers debt service after the windstorm premium and in a Stage 3 year. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 page and the note on special purpose property risk set out the rules; larger industrial and port-service loans usually go to conventional and institutional lenders. A borrower should expect the lender to ask for the windstorm quote and the will-serve letter before the appraisal, because either one can change the loan amount.

The research settles no program status for any Corpus Christi address. These are the lookups a study should print, with dates. Coastal sites add one more check, the flood zone, which changes both the insurance budget and the environmental review a federal program requires.

  • USDA Business and Industry. The program excludes places of more than 50,000 people [^20], which rules out the city itself. Coastal and county towns such as Rockport, at 11,348 residents [^5], fall below that line, subject to USDA's address-level determination in the OneRD tool [^19]. See USDA Rural Development.
  • Opportunity Zones. Designation of the next round began July 1, 2026, and the Governor's office expects certification by November 28, 2026 [^21]. Port-area and downtown tracts should be checked against both the 2018 list and the new round.
  • HUBZone. Look the address up on the SBA map; its tracts next change in July 2028 [^22].
  • EB-5. Hotel and industrial projects raising EB-5 capital need a targeted employment area that USCIS confirms in the I-956F filing; the high-unemployment test is at least 150% of the national unemployment rate across the project's tracts [^23]. See EB-5.

There is no Corpus Christi case on the site yet, and the Texas cases are gathered on the Texas page. Three cases share the coastal, water and insurance questions a Corpus Christi study faces, and each shows a different way those costs reach the debt service coverage ratio.

  • Travel center, Hankamer, Texas, USDA B&IA Gulf Coast site in Chambers County, another first-tier windstorm county, financed under USDA rules.
  • Dry stack marina, Cape Coral, Florida, SBA 504A Gulf storm-exposed marina where insurance and special purpose rules shape the loan.
  • Car wash, Texas, SBA 504A water-dependent special purpose business, the use most exposed to Corpus Christi's drought stages.
