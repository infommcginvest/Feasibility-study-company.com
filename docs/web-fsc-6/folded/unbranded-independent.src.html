An unbranded or independent station buys gasoline without a refiner's flag, either at the terminal rack through a jobber or on dealer terms from a distributor, and usually runs as a single store or a small chain. It earns the same two ways a branded store does, fuel margin and inside margin, but with nobody else's contract standing behind the fuel price. That freedom cuts both ways. The operator can price below the branded store across the road, and it carries the whole risk when wholesale costs jump before the street price can follow.

The research found no published margin for independent operators and no revenue per independent store. The closest disclosed evidence is the supply side of the same trade. ARKO reports what its wholesale business earns in each channel. At fuel-supply locations, where an independent dealer runs the store and keeps the station margin, ARKO's distributor margin was 6.3 cents a gallon in 2025, against 6.0 cents in 2024 [^1]. At consignment-agent locations, where the supplier keeps title to the fuel until it is sold and the agent runs the store, ARKO kept 27.5 cents a gallon, against 27.4 cents [^1]. Inside the company, ARKO's fixed intercompany fuel margin rose from 5.0 cents a gallon through 2025 to 6.0 cents from January 1, 2026 [^2].

None of those is the independent's own margin, and a study should not present them as one. What they show is the shape of the deal. A dealer that buys at a distributor's cost-plus price keeps most of the retail margin and most of the risk. An agent on consignment hands much of the margin to the supplier in exchange for not owning the fuel. The pro forma has to say which arrangement the borrower has, and the margin line has to come from that arrangement's invoices.

The inside store does not automatically rescue a thin fuel margin. NACS Magazine reported that 70% of the stores in its 2024 State of the Industry sample would show a negative in-store operating profit once expenses are charged to the inside business [^6]. For an independent, whose store is often small and older, that finding argues for a study that shows inside sales, inside margin and the payroll and card costs behind them line by line, rather than a single inside contribution figure.

The floor under the fuel margin is also rising. NACS reported that the breakeven pool margin it tracks rose 1.83 cents a gallon in the first half of 2025, while inside transactions fell and foodservice growth slowed [^17]. An independent absorbs that rise on its own; there is no brand program to share card or marketing costs. The study should show the subject's own breakeven margin, built from its payroll, card fees and fixed costs, beside the margin it expects to earn.

The single-store owner is the typical borrower, not an exception: operators with ten or fewer stores held 63.0% of U.S. convenience stores at the end of 2025 [^4]. The middle of the market is thin: no size band between 11 and 500 stores holds even 6% of stores, so the likely buyers for an independent store are other small operators or the largest chains.

Those large chains are both buyers and sellers. Raymond James lists 2026 small-chain deals that pair Casey's with 24 Pak-A-Sak stores (August 8), Pump & Pantry with 21 Hy-Vee stores (June 5) and Blarney Castle with 20 Fleming Bros. stores (June 30) [^5]. At the same time, 7-Eleven's FY2026 plan converts 350 company stores to wholesale sites run by third parties on 7-Eleven-supplied fuel [^14], and ARKO ran 2,053 wholesale sites at the start of the fourth quarter of 2025, with 26 opened or reopened during the year [^3]. Each conversion adds an operator who looks like an independent but buys fuel under a supply contract with a chain. A study should say which kind of independent the borrower is.

Exit pricing evidence is thin. Wholesale fuel distributors traded at a median 8.2 times trailing EBITDA in September 2026 [^5], but that multiple prices supply businesses, not single stores. The research found no cap rate for independent fee-simple stations and no price per site for single-store sales. A lender relying on resale value should treat the appraisal, not a market multiple, as the evidence.

One exit route that does exist for a multi-site independent is the sale-leaseback. In October 2025 Getty Realty bought 12 sites from Now & Forever, a Houston chain of 22 travel centers and convenience stores, for $100 million under a long-term unitary net lease [^18], and Getty reported a weighted average lease term of 15.8 years on its 2025 acquisitions [^19]. For a lender, a sale-leaseback converts owned collateral into a lease obligation; a study for a borrower that may take that route should show coverage with rent in place of debt service.

The research found no development cost for an unbranded or independent station. The only disclosed fuel-and-store ranges come from franchise systems, which are set out on our branded fuel page; they describe a different product with a franchisor's specification and should not be quoted as an independent's cost. For a new independent store, the study's cost evidence has to be a contractor's bid and the borrower's own equipment quotes.

When an independent project begins on land that has held a station before, the larger cost risk is underground. A contractor's summary of EPA's 2022 leaking tank cleanup cost study put the median cost to clean up a former fuel site at $27,120 to $265,883, across the three states the study covered [^7]. EPA reports roughly 53,000 tank releases still awaiting cleanup nationally as of March 2026 [^15]. A Phase I that finds a historic release turns a feasibility question into a remediation question, and the sources and uses should carry either a quantified cleanup line or a lender condition that holds the loan until the Phase II prices it.

For a purchase, the cost evidence is the seller's equipment age and compliance record: tank and line test results, leak detection records, and the state tank registration. Those documents tell the lender whether the buyer is acquiring a working fuel system or a near-term replacement project, and a study should list them as reviewed or as outstanding.

Brand does not decide eligibility. SBA's example list of special purpose properties names gas stations without distinguishing branded from unbranded [^12], so an independent station sits in the same collateral class as a flagged one. For a new store under 504, that means a borrower contribution of 15%, rising to 20% for a new business [^12].

When the loan buys an operating store, the change-of-ownership rules govern. The buyer injects 10% of total project cost, a floor the lender cannot reduce [^12]. Seller debt on full standby, together with other full-standby debt and minority investors, may supply no more than half of that injection [^12]. Coverage of 1.25x is measured on historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two [^12]. On projections, the SOP is specific: projections are reviewed but cannot meet the floor, except an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports, where the floor may be met on projections within two years [^12]. A station purchase can fall inside that exception, which makes the appraisal and the study's projections carry more weight than they would for an ordinary business acquisition. When real estate is 85% or more of project costs, the whole loan may run 25 years [^12].

What the study adds to a purchase is the reconciliation the seller's tax returns do not give: gallons by month, the margin by supply contract, inside sales by category, and a competitor census that explains whether the history will repeat. The regulation leaves the study to the lender's judgment, 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^13], and lenders ask for one when the history is short, mixed with personal expenses or about to change because a competitor is opening. That reconciliation is the core of our SBA 7(a) and 504 work.

The rural route: USDA Business and Industry

For a station in a small town, USDA's Business and Industry guarantee is the main alternative to SBA. The site must lie outside any city or town of more than 50,000 people [^11]. USDA's FY2026 availability page showed $2,093,151,202 of program funding, $1,014,367,209 obligated and $1,078,783,993 remaining [^9]. The program page lists an 85% guarantee below $5 million and 80% at or above it, an initial fee of 3% of the guaranteed amount, an annual fee of 0.55% and a maximum term of 40 years [^16]; an undated program FAQ shows a lower schedule, so the lender should confirm the current percentage with the state office. For a loan over $1,000,000 to a new business, 7 CFR 5001.306 requires an independent feasibility study [^10]. Our USDA Rural Development practice covers those components, and the note on underserved gas station markets explains how we test whether a rural town can carry another station.

  1. The margin has no paper behind it

    A study that borrows a public chain's retail margin for a single independent store will be asked where the number came from. The only channel figures in the research measure the supplier's take, 6.3 cents at dealer sites and 27.5 cents at consignment sites in 2025 [^1]. The answer has to be the borrower's invoices and the competitors' posted prices.

  2. The supplier is a single point of failure

    Dealers depend on their jobber's solvency. When Mountain Express Oil Co., an operator inside Pilot's dealer network, went through bankruptcy in 2023, 19 of its dealer locations in that network closed temporarily [^8]. The study should name the supplier and an alternative source, and the lender should read the supply contract's termination terms.

  3. The site's history surfaces late

    An older independent site is the likeliest place for a historic release. With median cleanup costs of $27,120 to $265,883 per former site in EPA's 2022 study [^7], a Phase II finding can consume the equity injection. Ordering the Phase I before the study is finished keeps the remediation line out of the closing surprise list.

  4. The seller's books do not reach the floor

    Change-of-ownership coverage is tested on historical EBITDA [^12]. Cash sales, owner wages taken as draws and fuel bought outside the books all make the history weaker than the business. The study can reconcile gallons and supply invoices to the returns; it cannot replace them.

  5. The inside store is assumed to carry the fuel

    With 70% of surveyed stores showing negative in-store operating profit in 2024 [^6], an inside contribution that covers a fuel shortfall needs its own evidence: category sales, margins and the payroll schedule that supports them.

The Opelika, Alabama case is the closest test of this page's underwriting section. It buys an operating store at I-85 Exit 64 as a change of ownership, so it shows the historical coverage test and the seller-note limits in action, and its competitor census includes an independent food mart west of the exit whose brand could not be found. The subject there sells a refiner's brand, so the supply-agreement questions sit on the branded fuel page; an unbranded purchase would carry the same coverage test with a weaker paper trail on margin.

The Lockhart, Texas case shows why brand has to be verified before a census counts it: two in-town stations are listed as unbranded in directories, and several others carry different brands in different sources. The Lake Wales, Florida case includes one unbranded station among national chains on the US 27 corridor, and the Versailles, Ohio case sits in a village where a local operator's station appears under six brands across directories.

Our gas station and c-store hub sets independents beside the other fuel and store formats. Compared with a branded store, an independent trades a brand's support for freedom on price, which makes the margin evidence harder and the supply contract more important. Compared with a convenience store without fuel, it keeps the forecourt but takes on the full environmental file that comes with it.
