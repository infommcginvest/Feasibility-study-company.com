A convenience store without fuel is a neighborhood or urban store with no forecourt: packaged goods, beverages, tobacco, lottery and, increasingly, prepared food. It earns inside sales multiplied by inside margin, less a payroll that runs most hours of the day. The research could not find a published sales or profit figure for stores of this format. That is the central fact for a lender. Every operating benchmark in the market comes from companies that also sell fuel, so a study for a no-fuel store has to use those figures as proxies for the inside business and say plainly that it is doing so.

The proxies still tell a lender something. At a fueling store, merchandise and foodservice were 35% of sales but 57.4% of gross profit in 2025, by Paytronix's reading of NACS data [^3]. NACS's own release puts fuel at 38.8% of gross profit in the same year [^2], which leaves the inside business with a larger share than Paytronix reports; the two series do not reconcile, and a study should not quote one as if it settled the other. Either way the inside store earns more than it sells, which is the economic case for a store that sells nothing else.

The spread in that table is the warning. Inside margins in the same quarter ran from 20.1% at Murphy USA, a high-volume fuel retailer, to 35.7% at Yesway [^5]. Much of the difference is mix: food and fountain drinks earn more per dollar than tobacco and packaged goods, as the evidence on our foodservice-led page shows, so a store heavy in cigarettes earns less on each dollar of sales. A study that picks one operator's margin has picked a category mix along with it, so the margin line should be built category by category and checked against the range, not copied from the top of it.

Category mix is moving against the traditional store

Cigarettes fell from 33% of inside sales in 2017 to 18% in 2025, in Raymond James's reading of NACS data [^5]. For a store without fuel, where tobacco can be a large share of what the counter sells, that decline is the main demand risk, and the study should show tobacco's share of the subject's projected sales explicitly. The channel itself is large: NIQ measured c-store sales of $170.6 billion in 2025, 13.1% of the retail sales it tracks [^4]. Timing matters for the payroll line: Fridays carry 17% of weekly c-store transactions and Sundays 12%, per NACS citing SwiftIQ and PDI [^7], so a flat staffing schedule overstates cost on quiet days and understates it at the weekend peak.

NACS counted 151,975 convenience stores at the end of 2025, of which 122,620 sold fuel, which leaves 29,355 stores without fuel, or 19.3% of the total [^1]. The change during the year is the sharper fact: total stores fell by 280 while fuel-selling stores rose by 768, so stores without fuel declined by 1,048 [^1]. No state count of stores without fuel was available; NACS publishes most of its state figures only as a map image. A study therefore has to count no-fuel competitors itself, store by store, in the trade area.

The closures are concentrated in the kind of store a new no-fuel project resembles. 7-Eleven announced 444 closures of underperforming North American stores in October 2024, citing inflation, slower traffic and declining cigarette sales [^9]. Later reporting counts 474 closures for 2024 and 373 for 2025 [^10]; the 2024 figures come from different announcements and do not match. The company has also described weaker spending by low-income households as North American growth slowed [^11]. New entrants are arriving at the same time: FEMSA, the owner of the OXXO chain, entered the U.S. in 2025 with 255 locations, the former Delek DK stores [^12]. Trade press put the purchase at $385 million for 249 Delek stores in 2024 [^19]; the two counts differ, and a study citing the entrant should say which it uses.

For a lender, the supply picture means the subject's sales cannot be justified by a growing format. They have to come from a specific trade area where foot traffic, residents and workers support a store that does not offer fuel, and where the study can name each competing store. The trade-area research note explains the method, and our work on gas station demand per capita shows why national store-per-resident ratios mislead in urban neighborhoods.

The two investment ranges describe two different projects. A leased APLUS store at $239,850 to $726,700 is mostly fixtures, equipment, inventory and opening costs inside someone else's building [^8]. A newly built store at $808,950 to $1,973,600 adds the building, and construction alone runs $375,000 to $1,100,000 for about 3,000 square feet [^8]. Land is not in either range, and the disclosure as relayed gives no revenue or payback. A study should therefore take its cost from the franchisor's current Item 7, a local construction bid and the land contract, and use the APLUS range only to test whether the budget is plausible.

The leased version matters for underwriting. A borrower who leases the building finances a business, not real estate, which moves the file from 504 territory toward 7(a) and shortens the collateral package to equipment, inventory and the business itself. The study's sources and uses should make that choice visible, because it changes the term, the equity and what the lender can recover.

Start with classification. SBA's example list of special purpose properties names gas stations [^15], defined as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^15]. The research found no source that lists a convenience store without fuel as special purpose, and the list is examples, not a closed set. The CDC classifies the property it is lending against. If the building is a general retail box, the 504 contribution for an established business is 10%; a business two years old or less still contributes 15% because it is new, whatever the building [^15]. Under 7(a), a start-up injects 10% and must reach 1.15x within two years [^15].

Next, gaming. Under SOP 50 10 8, a business that earns more than a third of its gross revenue from legal gambling is ineligible, while revenue from a state lottery commission is permitted within that limit [^14]. A store with heavy lottery sales, or one hosting coin-operated amusement machines where a state licenses them, should show the revenue split for the last two years and in the projections. The research quotes the rule from SOP 50 10 8; the lender should confirm its current wording in SOP 50 10 8.1.

Then the environmental file. SOP 50 10 8 extended environmental investigation to all commercial real estate collateral, including secondary collateral [^17]. The research does not say whether the gas station appendix applies to a store that sells no fuel, so the study should not assume it away. A no-fuel store on a former station site inherits the site's tank history, and the Phase I will find it.

Last, value. A net-lease market review in October 2026 found that stores without fuel price materially wider than fuel sites [^13]. For a lender sizing to appraised value, a wider capitalization rate on the same rent means less collateral. No published cap rate isolates the no-fuel store, so the nearest small-box comparables are the ones an appraiser will reach for. In the second quarter of 2026, asking cap rates ran 7.49% for dollar stores, 7.85% for drug stores and 6.45% for auto parts stores [^20], against 6.60% for net-lease retail and 6.82% for net lease overall [^21]. A small store without fuel, without investment-grade credit, is likelier to price toward the dollar-store end of that range than near a credit c-store. How we tie a study to the lender's credit memo is set out under SBA 7(a) and 504 lending, and SBA's own text treats the study as optional: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^16]. For a franchised store, the notes on the c-store franchise category and franchise versus non-franchise cover the brand decision.

  1. Proxies are presented as benchmarks

    Every inside margin in the research belongs to a company that sells fuel [^5]. A study that cites them as no-fuel results misleads the reviewer. Label them as proxies, show the range, and build the subject's margin from its own category plan.

  2. Tobacco carries the projection

    With cigarettes down from 33% to 18% of inside sales between 2017 and 2025 [^5], a pro forma that holds the subject's tobacco share flat for five years is betting against the trend. Show the share, and show coverage if it keeps falling.

  3. The classification is assumed

    A file that assumes the 10% standard contribution without the CDC's determination can come back needing more equity. Ask the CDC early and put its answer in the study.

  4. Lottery and gaming cross the line

    Revenue from legal gambling above a third of gross revenue makes the business ineligible [^14]. Stores that report lottery commissions and gaming income together, or that add machines after closing, need the split documented.

  5. Value is set at a fuel-site cap rate

    Stores without fuel price wider than fuel sites [^13]. An appraisal or study that borrows a fuel-site rate overstates collateral, and a reviewer who knows the net-lease market will catch it.

No worked case on this site takes a convenience store without fuel as its subject. The closest is the Versailles, Ohio case. There, the village zoning code permits a convenience store by right in the B-2 district, but a gas station is a conditional use that needs a board permit, and the code prohibits underground storage of petroleum inside the drinking water source protection zone. Whether the subject lot lies inside that zone was not found. If it does, the fuel half of the project falls away and what remains is a store without fuel competing against three fuel-and-convenience operators in the village. That case is the one to read for how a study handles a project whose format depends on a site rule.

The Lockhart, Texas case shows the opposite pressure: a pending entrance-corridor overlay would require a special use permit for gas stations and convenience stores alike along US 183, so dropping fuel would not escape the permit there.

The gas station and c-store hub places the no-fuel store against the five fuel formats. It shares its inside economics with the foodservice-led format, which leans on a kitchen to lift those same inside margins, and it gives up the forecourt that an unbranded or independent station keeps, along with most of the environmental file that comes with fuel. Lenders comparing the two should see that the no-fuel store trades environmental risk for category risk.
