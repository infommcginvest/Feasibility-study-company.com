The McAllen-Edinburg-Mission metro is a single county, Hidalgo, and its economy stands on three legs: health care and education, government at the border, and trade that moves through the Hidalgo port of entry. HUD's July 2025 market analysis ranks the largest employers with school districts left out, and the top of that list is a hospital and a university, not a factory [^1]. HUD lists Doctors Hospital at Renaissance with 6,423 workers and the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley with 5,247. U.S. Customs and Border Protection follows with 3,700, ahead of Hidalgo County government at 3,579 and the H-E-B grocery chain at 3,150 [^1].

For a lender that ranking matters more than any population count. Payroll at a hospital, a state university and a federal agency does not follow the retail cycle, and it steadies demand for housing, medical office space, child care and everyday retail. The same list carries a border dependency: an inspection workforce of that size exists because of the crossing, and the crossing feeds the Valley's logistics, wholesale and cross-border retail trade.

The trade figures in HUD's analysis show the scale. The Hidalgo port of entry handled $15.3 billion of exports in 2024, more than 779,500 trucks entered through it, and Foreign Trade Zone No. 12 counted more than 410 firms [^1]. Those are crossing and membership counts, not road counts. They describe the freight engine, and a study for a truck stop, warehouse, cold storage building or fleet service site has to tie its revenue to that engine and show what happens when crossings fall.

Health care is still building. HUD records Driscoll Children's Hospital in Edinburg, a $100 million facility, as built and hiring in 2024 to 2025, and lists a Texas A&M nursing building in McAllen among the 2026 projects; the largest 2026 item it names is a $24.8 million Sam's Club distribution center in Weslaco [^1]. The research found no 2025 to 2026 project above $50 million in the metro, so a Valley study cannot lean on one large announcement the way an El Paso study can. Demand here is built from many mid-sized employers, which makes it slower to move in either direction.

Retail draws from beyond the county line. HUD's analysis repeats the city's own figures for annual gross retail sales and daily visitors, but neither carries a year, so this page does not print them and no study should use them as a base [^1]. A retail or hotel study has to measure the cross-border reach of its trade area with dated evidence instead: the Comptroller's sales tax allocations by city and month, and federal crossing counts at the McAllen-area ports, each with its period.

The Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 series, as carried in the St. Louis Fed's ALFRED archive, puts the metro at 921,549 residents on July 1, 2025 [^2]. HUD's own estimate for the same date is 927,000 [^1]. The gap reflects method, not error, and a study should name the series it uses and keep to it from the first demand table to the last. The city of McAllen held 150,640 residents on that date [^3], so most of the metro lives outside the central city, in Edinburg, Mission, Weslaco and the county's smaller towns. A trade area drawn around McAllen alone will miss most of its customers.

At that size the Valley is a larger market than the El Paso metro and roughly twice the size of Corpus Christi, yet its average apartment rent sits far below the levels on the Houston and Austin pages. Size does not translate into pricing power here, and a pro forma built on big-metro rents or room rates will fail review.

Two numbers the research could not settle. A Census-published 2020 base for the metro is not in hand, so this page states no five-year growth rate. And the state's August 2026 labor market review, which carries the metro's payroll count, could not be opened; its indexed text is unverified, so this page states no job-growth rate either. A study should pull both directly: the 2020 decennial count for Hidalgo County from the Census Bureau and the metro payroll series from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Housing construction stands in for the missing permit totals. On July 1, 2025 HUD counted 2,225 for-sale homes and 2,375 rental units under construction [^1]. Builders adding both kinds of housing at once confirms that households are forming. It also means a new apartment community will compete with new starter homes for the same young families, and the study should say how the subject's rents compare with a mortgage payment on those homes.

HUD's analysis names the Winter Texans, the retirees who spend the cold months in the Valley, as a source of seasonal demand [^1]. It gives no count, and the research behind this page found none. That gap touches more property types here than in any other Texas metro: RV parks, manufactured-home communities, senior housing, restaurants, pharmacies and clinics all see the winter swell and then a long summer trough.

A study that annualizes winter occupancy overstates revenue. The test a lender should apply is a month-by-month curve: winter months at the occupancy competitors actually post, summer months at what the property fills without the seasonal residents, and debt service coverage computed on the full year, not on the peak quarter. Monthly and seasonal rate cards collected from the subject's direct competitors are the cheapest evidence available. A dated visitor survey, where the study can cite one, supplies the head count; an undated estimate repeated in marketing material does not.

Seasonality also shapes staffing and expenses. A park or restaurant that runs full in February and half-empty in August carries fixed costs through the trough, so the operating budget should be built by month as well. The same logic drove the snowbird RV resort in the site's Arizona USDA case, where winter demand sets the revenue line. A Valley study can borrow that method, provided it replaces every Arizona input with a Hidalgo or Cameron County one.

The research found no named commercial corridor and no Texas Department of Transportation traffic count for any Valley site. That is a task, not a reason to skip traffic. A site study in McAllen should pull the annual average daily traffic station nearest the frontage, and the one nearest the closest interchange, from TxDOT's public count dataset, with the station number and count year, and should report at least three years so the trend is visible [^8].

Freight geography is better documented than road counts. The Hidalgo port is the node: its truck entries and export value, above, are the volumes a logistics or fuel study should start from [^1]. East of Hidalgo County, the U.S. Department of Transportation announced on October 14, 2026 a $9 million award for an access road to a public truck-parking facility of about 305 spaces at Los Indios in Cameron County [^6]. A truck stop or travel center study on the Valley's freight routes should treat that public lot as a competitor for overnight parking; the truck stop hub sets out how parking revenue is underwritten.

Retail sites need a second count, the cross-border shopper. Federal agencies publish pedestrian and passenger-vehicle crossings by port and month. The research holds no figure from that series, so the study should pull it, date it, and show how far the subject's sales move when crossings drop.

Multifamily is the only property type with published evidence for the Valley. HUD, citing CoStar, measured apartment vacancy at 11.2% in the second quarter of 2025, up from 10.6% a year earlier, with an average rent of $986, down $6 year over year, and it describes the rental market as slightly soft [^1]. On July 1, 2025 there were 2,375 rental units under construction, against HUD's estimate of demand for 5,400 units over the following three years [^1].

Read together, those numbers argue for patience rather than alarm. The pipeline covers less than half of the three-year demand, so the market can absorb it, but vacancy rose and rent slipped while that pipeline was under way, which means new units lease on price. A multifamily study here should underwrite concessions, a longer lease-up and an effective rent below asking; our note on effective rent versus asking rent sets out the gap. HUD's readings are from mid-2025, and the study must bring them current.

The research found no Valley figure for hotels, self-storage, industrial space, retail vacancy, senior housing, RV parks or convenience stores. Each is a census the study has to build, competitor by competitor, from operators' own sites and the county appraisal district's records, with the date each figure was read.

No hotel occupancy tax rate, impact fee schedule, 2025 to 2026 zoning change, short-term rental ordinance or water and sewer capacity limit is documented for McAllen, Edinburg, Mission or Hidalgo County in the research. Texas cities set their own local hotel tax within the state framework, so a lodging study must take the current rate from the city itself and must not borrow a neighbor's. Utility capacity works the same way: a will-serve letter from the city utility or the water supply corporation belongs in the file before a lender relies on the site plan.

Windstorm insurance splits the Valley. The Texas Windstorm Insurance Association's designated catastrophe area takes in Cameron and Willacy counties among the first-tier coastal counties, and Hidalgo County is not on that list [^7]. A project in Brownsville, Harlingen or on South Padre Island therefore budgets for wind cover from the state pool, while a McAllen or Edinburg project prices wind in the private market. The operating budget should show which applies and carry a written quote, not an allowance.

Zoning and permitting timelines belong in the study as well, because a delayed certificate of occupancy pushes the first revenue month into the summer trough for any seasonal property. The study should record the city's current approval path for the use, the hearings it needs, and the date the information was confirmed.

The SBA's Lower Rio Grande Valley District made nearly $103 million of 7(a) and 504 loans to more than 180 companies in fiscal 2025, and the SBA recognized Lone Star National Bank of McAllen as the district's top 7(a) lender by number of loans [^4]. The second and third lenders and the leading certified development company sit behind a paywall and are not named here. The district office is in Harlingen and covers 14 counties; the research could not obtain the full county list, and the partial list it found does not show Hidalgo, so a borrower should confirm the district assignment with the SBA before relying on a district contact [^5].

A local bank at the top of the count tells a sponsor two things. Valley loans are read by credit officers who know the border economy and will test the crossing and seasonal assumptions themselves. And a study written for a national lender has to explain those local factors rather than assume the reader knows them. A feasibility study consultant in McAllen earns its fee by showing the sensitivities, not by restating the metro's growth.

SBA rules set the frame. Under 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^15]. For fiscal 2027 the 7(a) upfront fee is 0% on loans of $700,000 or less to businesses in a rural area, meaning a county that the U.S. Census Bureau has defined as at least 30 percent rural [^16]. Whether Hidalgo, Starr or Willacy County meets that test is a lookup the study should record, because the waiver changes the sponsor's cash at closing. Program detail is on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page; loans above SBA limits are covered under conventional and institutional lending.

The research found no USDA rural determination, Opportunity Zone tract or HUBZone designation for any Valley site. Each is a lookup the study performs and prints with its date, and each can change the capital stack.

  • USDA Business and Industry. The program's rural area excludes any city or town above 50,000 people [^10]. McAllen is above that line [^3]; smaller Valley towns and unincorporated sites may fall below it, and USDA's OneRD tool gives the address-level answer [^9]. For a guaranteed loan above $1,000,000 to a new business, 7 CFR 5001.306(a)(3)(i) requires an independent feasibility study [^11]. See USDA Rural Development.
  • Opportunity Zones. The 2018 tracts remain on the Governor's list, and Treasury began designating the next round on July 1, 2026, with certification expected by November 28, 2026 [^12]. A study dated before certification should say which map it used.
  • HUBZone. Check the SBA map by address; the map notes that the next tract update comes in July 2028 [^13].
  • EB-5. A high-unemployment targeted employment area needs weighted unemployment of at least 150% of the national average across the project's tracts, and USCIS now decides it inside the I-956F filing [^14]. See EB-5.

None of the site's worked cases sits in the Valley; the Texas cases are listed on the Texas page. Three are the closest models for a Valley market study, each for a different part of the local economy.

  • RV resort, Arizona, USDA B&IA snowbird market where winter demand sets revenue, the seasonality a Winter Texan park faces.
  • Truck stop, Texas, SBA 504Diesel and inside sales driven by freight, the model for a site that lives on port truck traffic.
  • Assisted living, Texas, HUD 232Senior demand built from age and income cohorts, the test a Valley senior project must pass.

  1. Crossing sensitivity

    Revenue tied to the port or to shoppers from Mexico should be shown at lower crossing volumes, with the federal crossing series dated and cited. A study that cites only annual export value, or the city's undated retail-sales and visitor totals, has not tested the risk.

  2. The seasonal curve

    Occupancy and revenue by month, with coverage computed on the year. Winter rate cards from named competitors, and a dated count of seasonal residents if the study relies on one.

  3. The rental pipeline since mid-2025

    HUD's vacancy and rent readings are from the second quarter of 2025. Deliveries since then, current concessions and effective rents at the nearest comparables should be in the file.

  4. Figures the research could not verify

    The metro payroll count and a Census-published 2020 base. The study should pull both from federal series rather than repeat an unverified state figure.

  5. Program lookups

    USDA rural status, Opportunity Zone tract, HUBZone, the 7(a) rural fee waiver county test and the SBA district assignment, each printed with the date it was run.

  6. Insurance and utilities

    A written wind quote (state pool in Cameron and Willacy, private in Hidalgo), a flood determination, and a will-serve letter for water and sewer.

  7. Traffic at the site

    TxDOT station counts for the frontage and the nearest interchange, with station numbers and years, not a broker's range.
