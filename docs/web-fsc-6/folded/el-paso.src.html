El Paso's economy rests on the Army, the border and public institutions, and the record for all three is older than a lender would like. The most recent ranked employer list the research could find is HUD's 2011 market analysis, which counted 23,500 military and 7,000 civilian workers at Fort Bliss, 5,600 at T&T Staff Management, 3,100 at Tenet Healthcare and 2,550 at the University of Texas at El Paso [^10]. Those counts are fifteen years old. They show the shape of the base, a federal installation far larger than any private employer, but they cannot size demand for a 2027 opening.

Fort Bliss remains the anchor, and it is now also part of the data center story: the Army selected a private operator for a commercial data center on the installation in 2026 [^9]. The research holds no current troop or civilian headcount and no dollar value or size for that project. A study that uses the post as a demand driver should obtain a current installation figure and say where it came from.

The border economy, the cross-border manufacturing and freight trade with Ciudad Juárez, is the part of El Paso's base the research could not measure at all. It holds no current count of plants, crossings or trade value for the El Paso ports. Those are the first numbers an industrial, logistics, truck service or fuel study here must pull, from the federal crossing and trade series, each with its period. Without them a study is describing the city's reputation, not its market.

What sets El Paso apart from the other Texas border metro on this site is concentration. A McAllen and Rio Grande Valley study builds demand from a hospital system, a university and a busy port; an El Paso study in 2026 has to account for one private project large enough to move the labor market on its own.

Meta broke ground in far Northeast El Paso in October 2025 on a first phase it valued at more than $1.5 billion, with about 100 permanent jobs, more than 1,800 construction workers at peak and a site that could scale to one gigawatt [^2]. Local reporting described a 1.2 million square foot building on 1,000 acres [^4]. On March 26, 2026 Meta raised the commitment to more than $10 billion, said the campus would grow to one gigawatt, and put permanent jobs above 300 and peak construction employment above 4,000 [^2]. In July 2026 it formed a venture in which funds managed by BlackRock own 80% and Meta 20%, partly funded by a $12.5 billion debt financing; more than 2,300 workers were already on site, and the venture expects to bring capacity online from 2028 [^3].

The public side of the project is as large as the private one, and it is contested. El Paso Electric's 366-megawatt McCloud gas plant, dedicated to Meta for its first five years, was awaiting state utility commission approval in January 2026 [^8]. Local incentives total $110 million [^6] under agreements the City Council adopted in 2023, and in June 2026 a council member sought a vote to terminate the city's agreement [^7]. The city is also reimbursing $12.5 million of road improvements around Stan Roberts Sr. Avenue, near the site [^5]. On water, El Paso Water's chief executive said in December 2025 that Meta would not rank among the utility's ten largest users, and Meta holds a supply agreement with the utility [^9].

A second campus is rising just across the state line. In Santa Teresa, in Doña Ana County, New Mexico, the county authorized up to $165 billion of industrial revenue bonds for Project Jupiter in September 2025 [^14]. In 2026 the project was reported 26% complete with 3,686 construction personnel, of whom 466 were county residents [^15]. Oracle has issued a force majeure notice to the developer while saying the project is on schedule [^16], and the New Mexico Supreme Court rejected two challenges on September 17, 2026, letting permitting resume [^17].

For a lender the lesson is the gap between the peak and the permanent number. Thousands of construction workers on two campuses at once fill hotels, RV parks, apartments, restaurants and fuel stops, and they can push contractor wages up for every other project in the region. When construction ends, the permanent payroll is in the hundreds. A study should model the subject's revenue with the construction demand and without it, show the year the crews are expected to leave, and test debt coverage in the year after. A lodging pro forma that holds construction-era occupancy into its stabilized year will not survive review.

One state action needs checking rather than assuming. On August 3, 2026 the Governor paused new data-center connections to the ERCOT grid pending an audit [^18]. The research does not say whether that pause reaches El Paso or El Paso Electric's service area, so a study for any power-intensive use should state which grid and which utility serve the site, and confirm the interconnection timeline in writing.

The Census Bureau's estimates, carried in the St. Louis Fed's FRED series, put the El Paso metro at 881,291 residents on July 1, 2025, down from 883,344 a year earlier and up from 870,833 in 2021 [^1]. The metro lost 2,053 people in its latest year, the only population decline among the eight Texas markets on this site. A Census-published 2020 base is not in the research, so this page states no five-year rate.

A falling population under a construction boom is an unusual pairing, and it cuts both ways. Household-driven demand for retail, child care, storage and everyday services cannot be assumed to grow, so a study for those uses must find its customers among existing residents, often by taking share. Demand tied to the data center build is real but has an end date. Compare Lubbock, which added residents in the same year with no project of that scale; there, household growth carries the argument that El Paso's population figures cannot.

Two other figures could not be settled. No job-growth rate appears here: the Texas Workforce Commission figure the research found came from search-engine text of a report that would not open. The research also listed a city population figure under a Census page for another city, so it is left out. Both should come straight from the federal series. Residential permits for 2025 were not found either.

The research names no commercial corridor and no traffic count for El Paso. The one documented node is the Meta site in far Northeast El Paso, where the city's $12.5 million road reimbursement around Stan Roberts Sr. Avenue will change access and traffic [^5]. A retail, fuel or lodging site in that area should be studied on the road network as it will be after those improvements, with the construction schedule stated.

Everywhere else the study must build the traffic case itself. TxDOT's public annual average daily traffic dataset gives the counts for state-maintained roads; the study should cite the station, the year and at least three years of history for the frontage and the nearest interchange [^21]. Border sites need a second series, the commercial and passenger crossings at the El Paso ports by month, because a truck stop or c-store near a crossing lives on the queue as much as on the highway. Our truck stop hub and gas station and c-store hub set out how those counts become fuel and inside-sales volume.

Self-storage is the one property type with a current El Paso reading. RentCafe's February 2026 data, drawn from Yardi Matrix, put El Paso at 6.5 square feet of storage per resident, just under the national figure of 7 in the same report, but with 224,891 square feet expected to open in 2026, equal to 5% of the inventory at the end of 2025 [^13]. That is the reverse of the Lubbock picture on our Lubbock page, where the existing stock, not the pipeline, is the problem. In El Paso a self-storage study has to show the subject leasing up against the 2026 openings in its own trade area, at street rates the new competitors will set.

For apartments the research holds no vacancy, rent or construction figure. HUD's market analysis for El Paso as of January 1, 2025, which the city hosts, is the document a multifamily study should read first [^11]; its figures were not extracted for this page. Hotels, industrial space, retail, senior housing, RV parks and convenience stores have no El Paso figure in the research. For each of them the study builds its own competitor census, noting when every number was collected.

El Paso's hotel tax is the one local rule in the research, and the figure is old. A 2018 guide from the Texas Hotel & Lodging Association put the combined rate at 17.5%, citing a 2009 survey, and notes that a venue election approved before September 1, 2013 exempts El Paso from the general 17% cap on combined rates [^12]. A lodging study must take today's rate from the city and the Comptroller; the point the guide still makes is that El Paso can legally sit above the cap other Texas cities face, which lowers net room revenue for any given rate.

Data center policy is moving. The city held public-input meetings on regulating data centers in 2026 [^9], and the research holds no ordinance text. A study for a power- or water-intensive use should record the status of any rule the city has adopted since, and confirm the zoning path for the use in writing.

Impact fees, zoning changes and short-term rental rules were not found for El Paso. Water supply for ordinary commercial projects is not documented either; the only water statement in the research concerns Meta [^9]. A will-serve letter from El Paso Water belongs in every file.

El Paso Inc., reading the SBA's fiscal 2025 regional report, put the El Paso District at $37 million, apparently a figure through the third quarter and the smallest of the six Texas districts [^19]. The district's page lists nine outlying counties, among them Brewster, Culberson, Hudspeth, Presidio and Reeves; El Paso County is served but not listed [^20]. The research could not name the district's leading 7(a) lenders or certified development companies.

A thin district changes how a sponsor should prepare. Fewer local SBA lenders means more El Paso loans are read by credit officers in Dallas, Houston or out of state, who will not know the border economy or the data center timeline. A feasibility study company working in El Paso has to write for that reader: explain Fort Bliss, the crossings and the construction wave, and show the permanent-demand case in numbers.

Program rules still apply in full. A new hotel is both a new business and a special purpose property, so a 504 loan for one needs 20% equity under SOP 50 10 8.1 [^27]. Data center construction money is not a local lending signal: the $12.5 billion venture debt finances one campus and says nothing about bank appetite for a mid-sized hotel or a storage facility. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 and conventional lending pages cover the alternatives.

Nothing in the research settles whether an El Paso address is USDA-rural, inside an Opportunity Zone or in a HUBZone. Each answer comes from a dated lookup.

  • USDA Business and Industry. The city of El Paso is far above the program's 50,000-person limit [^23]. Unincorporated parts of El Paso County and the small towns of the outlying district counties may qualify; USDA's OneRD tool answers by address [^22]. See USDA Rural Development.
  • Opportunity Zones. Treasury began designating the next round of zones on July 1, 2026, and certification is expected by November 28, 2026 [^24]. A study for a site near the Meta campus or downtown should state whether the tract is in a 2018 zone and whether it was nominated for the new round.
  • HUBZone. The SBA map answers by address and notes the next tract update in July 2028 [^25].
  • EB-5. A hotel or mixed-use project funded partly by EB-5 investors must sit in a targeted employment area; for a high-unemployment area USCIS requires the tracts' combined, labor-force-weighted unemployment to reach 150% of the national rate or more [^26]. Job counts built on construction spending must follow the rules on our EB-5 page and the note on how EB-5 jobs are counted.

No worked case on this site is set in El Paso; for the Texas set, start from the Texas page. The closest models for an El Paso market study are cases where demand comes from workers and freight rather than from household growth.
