A foodservice-led convenience store is one where prepared food and dispensed beverages bring customers in, usually through a larger building with a full kitchen, a branded food program or a quick-service restaurant co-brand. Fuel may or may not be part of the site. What separates the format from a traditional store is where the gross profit comes from. NACS put foodservice at 28.5% of in-store sales and 38.9% of in-store gross profit in 2025, against 11.9% of in-store sales in 2005 [^1]. Prepared food made up 73.9% of foodservice sales [^1]. The year before, foodservice held 28.7% of in-store sales and 39.6% of in-store gross margin dollars, in CSP's report of NACS data [^2], so the share is high and roughly stable rather than still climbing fast.

A second series tells the same story over a shorter span: Raymond James, reading NACS data, shows foodservice rising from 22% of inside sales in 2017 to 29% in 2025 [^3]. Packaged beverages, which ride along with food trips, reached 18.7% of in-store sales in 2025, up 0.8 points [^1]. For a lender, the point is that food carries a gross profit share well above its sales share. A pro forma that gives the kitchen an ordinary merchandise margin understates the format, and one that gives the whole store the kitchen's margin overstates it.

The operator benchmark: Casey's

Casey's publishes the most complete numbers for a fuel-and-food chain. It earned a 58.3% margin on prepared food and dispensed beverages in the first nine months of its fiscal 2026 [^6]. For the full fiscal year to April 30, 2026, its inside margin was 42.2% on inside gross profit of $2,676.3 million [^7]. Prepared food and dispensed beverage same-store sales rose 5.2%, inside same-store sales rose 4.2%, and the fourth-quarter inside margin reached 42.4% [^8]. The company's fiscal 2027 outlook calls for inside same-store sales growth of 2% to 5%, an inside margin above 42%, and same-store gallons between minus 1% and plus 1% [^9].

The category split is the clearest evidence for the format. For the full fiscal year Casey's earned 58.6% on prepared food and dispensed beverages against 35.8% on grocery and general merchandise [^7], the nine-month figure of 58.3% having risen by year end. On fiscal 2026 revenue of $17,561.1 million it reported EBITDA of $1,483.6 million and net income of $714.4 million [^10]. Industry figures put a range around that operator. NACS's first look at 2024 foodservice data showed prepared food averaging $54,961 per store per month, and foodservice supplying 45.11% of inside margin at top-decile operators [^17]. A single-store kitchen will not match the leader's margin, but the study can place the subject between the industry average and the top decile and say why.

Read together, those figures describe a business that expects its growth from the kitchen while it plans for flat fuel volume. That is the thesis a foodservice-led study has to prove for a single site: that the food program can lift inside sales enough to carry debt service even if gallons do not grow. Casey's operates at the scale of 2,944 stores [^10]; a single-store borrower should expect a lower food margin, and the study should say how much lower and why.

Fuel demand follows traffic past the site. Food demand follows meal occasions, and those occasions have other options. NACS reports that one in three c-store customers visits a quick-service restaurant within 30 minutes of a c-store visit [^4]. That cross-shopping is the opportunity a co-brand captures and the competition a stand-alone kitchen faces. A study for a foodservice-led store therefore counts the restaurants inside the trade area, not only the fuel stations, and it sizes demand by dayparts: breakfast commuters, lunch from nearby employers, evening pizza and snack trips.

Scale helps frame the opportunity without settling it. NACS has cited 160 million convenience transactions a day industry-wide, in a release dated January 31, 2025 that does not state the period exactly [^5]. National traffic of that size says nothing about one corner, which is why our trade-area method starts from the ground: name the employers, the households and the competing kitchens inside a defined drive time, then assign a capture rate to each. Our hub for quick-service restaurants carries the restaurant-side evidence a co-branded study also needs.

The research found no published development cost for a foodservice-led store and no staffing standard. What it found instead describes the format and the market for it. 7-Eleven's New Standard store, launched in October 2024 with quick-service food and fresh food, runs about 5,000 square feet [^11]. RaceTrac bought the Potbelly sandwich chain, with 445 locations, for $556 million in 2025 [^14], which shows chains buying a food brand rather than building one; it is a deal value, not a cost per store, and should not be divided into one.

For a store that hosts a franchised restaurant rather than its own kitchen, the restaurant franchisor publishes the cost. Domino's 2026 disclosure document, as summarized by a secondary source, puts a non-traditional store, the format used in convenience stores and usually carry-out only, at $107,450 to $709,500 [^18]. The partner's own health matters as much as its cost. Subway, the restaurant inside one of the travel centers in our Cartersville case, ended 2025 with 18,773 U.S. units after a net loss of 729, and has closed a net 8,345 since 2016 [^19]. A co-brand whose system is shrinking can renegotiate hours, fees or the unit itself, so the study should report the partner's unit trend beside its sales claim. Capital for drive-thru restaurants beside fuel sites is available: Getty Realty, a landlord to convenience and fuel operators, invested $269 million in 2025, including 26 drive-thru quick-service restaurants, at a 7.9% weighted initial cash yield [^20].

For a single site, the cost items that separate this format from a traditional store are the kitchen line, the hood and fire suppression system, grease management, walk-in refrigeration, dining seats and the added parking that dining requires. None of them has a published benchmark in the research. A lender should expect each as a named budget item, priced from a contractor's bid and the food program's equipment specification, and the study should show the opening payroll for kitchen staff separately from the cashiers. Where fuel is part of the site, the fuel-and-store cost evidence on the branded fuel page applies to the forecourt half.

If the site sells fuel, it falls under the gas station entry on SBA's example list of special purpose properties [^15], with the 504 contribution at 15% and 20% when the business is also new [^15]. If it does not, the research found no source on how SBA treats a QSR-led store, and SBA's list does not name restaurants [^15]. That call belongs to the CDC, and a building with a drive-through lane and a commercial kitchen may read differently from a plain retail box, so the study should carry the CDC's determination rather than assume one.

The coverage test is where the kitchen matters. Under 7(a), the new store counts as a start-up, with 10% equity and projected coverage reaching 1.15x within two years [^15]. Because the food program earns the highest margin in the building, small changes in food sales move coverage more than the same change in packaged goods. The study should show coverage with the food program at its benchmark and at a lower ramp, and the sensitivity table should put food sales on one axis. A feasibility study is a lender's request, not a program mandate: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^16]. Our SBA lending practice builds that sensitivity into the format the lender reviews.

A QSR co-brand adds a second franchise relationship to the file. The research found no franchise disclosure Item 19 data for c-store or fuel systems and no authority that makes the restaurant franchise agreement a required lender document, but a lender will read it anyway for term, territory and fees. Our notes on franchise versus non-franchise and the c-store franchise category cover what those agreements change in the projections.

  1. Three uplift figures are blended into one

    7-Eleven's evidence comes in three forms. Reporting from a Seven & i investor presentation puts New Standard stores at $6,300 of sales per store per day in the first half of 2024 against about $5,500 for existing stores, 13% higher [^13]. On an earnings call the company's president described food-forward stores at about 18% above the system average and projected mature New Standard stores at 45% higher [^12]. In August 2025 the chief executive cited 45% higher sales than traditional stores [^11]. The first is a measured comparison; the others are statements about different baselines and a projection. A study that averages them, or uses 45% as if it were measured, will be challenged. We use the measured figure and treat the rest as upside.

  2. No ramp is shown

    No benchmark for months to stabilization was found for new c-stores. A food program takes time to build a following, and a pro forma that opens at stabilized food sales overstates Year 1 and Year 2, the years SBA's start-up test reads.

  3. The chain's growth is read as the site's demand

    7-Eleven's plan called for 115 stores in the New Standard format by the end of fiscal 2024, 615 stores by 2027 and 2,100 stores with quick-service restaurants by 2030, after opening 122 stores in the year before the April 2026 report [^13]. Those plans show where the format is going. They do not show that one more kitchen will work on one corner, and a file that cites them as demand evidence reads as unsupported.

  4. Fuel is still in the numbers

    If the site sells fuel, a food thesis does not remove fuel risk. Casey's fourth-quarter fuel margin of 46.9 cents a gallon [^8] came in an elevated market, and its own outlook holds same-store gallons flat [^9]. The study should normalize the fuel line as it would for any station.

  5. Kitchen costs are buried in contingency

    With no published cost for the format, kitchen equipment, hood systems and opening kitchen payroll are easy to understate. A reviewer will look for them as named lines.

The Lockhart, Texas case is the one that puts a kitchen in the subject: its hypothetical new store carries a made-to-order kitchen, so the food program has to earn its place in the coverage test. Its competitor field includes a Valero-supplied store listing a quick-service restaurant and another Valero-branded store with a restaurant, so the kitchen is competing with kitchens.

The Cartersville, Georgia case at I-75 Exit 296 shows the hardest version of that competition. The exit's two travel centers already run national restaurant brands: the TA site lists IHOP, Burger King, Popeyes and Taco Bell with Pizza Hut Express, and the Pilot site lists McDonald's and Subway. A new car-oriented store one turn off the interchange cannot win on menu breadth, which is why the evidence behind that case points to a commuter convenience store rather than a meal destination. In the Versailles, Ohio case, the village's Casey's sells made-to-order pizza, so the benchmark operator on this page is also the subject's direct competitor. The Opelika, Alabama case has a deli and fast-food counter at a competing station a short distance along the same I-85 frontage road.

For the asset class as a whole, start from our gas station and c-store hub. They share inside economics with the convenience store without fuel, but where that format leans on tobacco and packaged goods, this one leans on a kitchen with a higher margin and a higher cost. At an interchange, the food program competes with travel-center restaurants, a problem the interstate and highway page addresses from the traffic side.
