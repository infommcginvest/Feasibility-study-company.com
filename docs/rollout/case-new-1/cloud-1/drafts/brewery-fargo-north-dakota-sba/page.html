Feasibility Study Company prepares brewery feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A study joins a dated market read, a census of the competing field, a priced build and a coverage test that shows how much debt a project can carry. This page runs that work on one hypothetical project: a production brewery with a taproom in Fargo, Cass County, North Dakota, financed with an SBA 504 loan. If you are looking for a brewery feasibility study consultant or a brewery feasibility study company for a Fargo site, ask us to run the same work on your project.

The subject is generic: a one-story building of about {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet on about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres along the I-29 corridor, with about {{case:v.in_tap_sf}} square feet for the taproom and the rest given to brewing, storage, packaging and an office. Every market figure is public and dated. Every model figure is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed near the end, and each input carries a source or the word assumption. The project belongs to our brewery and distillery practice, the town sits in North Dakota, and the program rules are laid out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice page.

Federal rules say that SBA may require a feasibility study on a loan: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^7]. Whether yours does is decided file by file, and a lender often asks for one before SBA does.

The market read is favorable on the demand side and cautious on the supply side. Cass County held 126,659 covered jobs in March 2026 [^1], the Fargo metro labor force stood at 156.3 thousand in September 2026 [^2], and the county's real output reached $14,875,063 thousand in chained 2017 dollars in 2024 [^9]. Visitors arrive by air in growing numbers: Hector International Airport recorded 590,542 boardings in 2025 [^3] and a September 2026 record of 49,464 [^10]. Those are the people who fill a taproom on a weeknight and buy a case to take home.

Jobs and wages

Cass County is the state's employment center. Its 126,659 covered jobs in March 2026 are about three in ten of the 420,702 covered jobs in North Dakota [^1], and its average weekly wage of $1,376 sits above the state's $1,333 [^1]. A taproom lives on discretionary spending, and a county that pays above the state average and holds a large payroll base is the right place for it. The metro's jobless rate of 2.3% in September 2026 [^2] says the same thing from the other side: labor is scarce, which raises the cost of hiring bartenders and brewers. The model carries that cost in its wage inputs.

The trend is flat rather than strong. Statewide nonfarm jobs slipped from 448.7 thousand in August to 447.3 thousand in September 2026 [^12], and the twelve-month change was only 0.2% [^4]. The Fargo metro supports 155.9 thousand nonfarm jobs [^2]. The study therefore does not assume a hiring boom behind the taproom. Statewide leisure and hospitality employment of 41.6 thousand [^13] is the closest labor-market series the research holds for the trade the taproom competes in for staff.

Output and housing

Cass County's real GDP of 14,875,063 thousand chained 2017 dollars in 2024 [^9] is the county series; the older metro series, 20,535.575 million dollars for 2023, has been discontinued by its publisher and is cited only for scale [^14]. The state economy printed $82,349.8 million in current dollars and 65,268.3 million in chained 2017 dollars for 2025 [^15,16].

Housing shows household wealth. The median listing price in Cass County was $399,625 in September 2026, with 712 active listings [^17,18]. The federal house price index for the Fargo metro stood at 365.43 in the second quarter of 2026 [^19], and the statewide index at 539.91 [^20]; the two indexes use different base periods and are not compared. Permits show the building cycle: Fargo's monthly count peaked at 281 in July 2025 and fell to 16 in December and 3 in January 2026 [^21], a seasonal drop in a cold climate that also tells a builder to plan a winter schedule.

Visitors and travel

Hector International Airport recorded a record 590,542 boardings in 2025 [^3]. In June 2026 it set a monthly record of 50,959 [^22] and in September 2026 another of 49,464 [^10]. Across North Dakota's commercial airports, boardings reached 973,044 in the first nine months of 2026 [^10], and the state recorded 1,322,791 for all of 2025 [^3]. September 2026 alone brought 104,380 boardings statewide [^10], and March 2025 set a state monthly record of 123,180 [^23]. Airport traffic is an imperfect proxy for taproom visitors, but it shows that outside spending reaches Fargo in rising volume.

The state bank and credit

North Dakota has a state-owned lender that matters to a borrower here. The Bank of North Dakota reported total assets of $10.7 billion at the end of 2025 and a total return to the state of 4.69% for the year [^24]. Whether it takes a participation in a brewery loan is a lender question, not a finding, but a bank that knows the state bank can ask.

Downtown and the apartment base

Downtown Fargo is the district where an urban taproom would compete for foot traffic. A broker survey puts metro apartment vacancy at 6.2% in the second quarter of 2025 and downtown vacancy at just over 16%, after nearly 850 downtown units were built in five years [^25]. Vacancy that high downtown cuts two ways: new residents live near the taproom district, but the district has more apartments than renters for now. The subject sits on the I-29 corridor instead, where it can draw from both the downtown crowd and the suburban metro, and the lender should test that choice against a drive-time study.

What the count tells a newcomer

The class holds at least four establishments and 90 paid employees in the county, and at least five establishments and 101 employees in the metro area [^5]. Employees outnumber establishments by a wide margin, which says the existing operations are real production businesses with staff, not one-person shops. The rate per ten thousand residents is a small fraction of one establishment in both geographies, shown in the table, which suggests a thinly served market, though a low rate can also mean that a small market has little room. The study does not read it as an opportunity by itself.

The program's own history is thin. The SBA data show two 504 loans to this class in North Dakota in the ten fiscal years to 2025, totaling $1,126,000 [^26]. A lender and a CDC in the state have little local precedent for a brewery, which makes the documentation, the equipment appraisal and the job plan more important.

Distilleries in the same class

The class combines breweries and distilleries, so some of the establishments counted are distilleries that do not compete for the same pint. Part of the lender condition is to separate the two. The condition also asks for each brewery's taproom hours, tap count, distribution footprint and packaging format, none of which the research could find.

The subject is a hypothetical site of about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres along the I-29 corridor in Fargo. Its parcel, zoning district, utility territory and flood status are conditions to confirm; the research could not retrieve a parcel record, a zoning map, a traffic count or a utility tariff, because the city and state agency hosts refused the connection. The study says so rather than guess.

Zoning, frontage and utilities

A production brewery is an industrial use with a retail use attached, and many cities treat the two differently: the taproom may be allowed by right in a commercial district while the production hall needs an industrial or mixed district. The lender needs a written zoning determination that covers both, the parking requirement for the taproom and any limit on outdoor seating. It also needs the electric, gas, water and sewer providers for the parcel and the commercial capacity and wastewater discharge charges in force. A brewery uses far more water and discharges stronger wastewater than a retail building of the same size, so the pre-treatment allowance in site work is a real cost that the city's rule can raise or lower.

Traffic and visibility

The research holds no count for the corridor. A state count station and annual average daily traffic, the nearest interchange, the sign rules and the turning movements at the entrance are lender conditions. Retail space in the metro gives a rough view of how location is priced: Goldmark Commercial's survey put the average base retail lease rate at $17.08 per square foot in 2025 [^27], and the area around West Acres had a vacancy of 2.9% in mid-January 2026 against 18.3% downtown [^27]. A taproom in the strong retail pocket would pay more rent but meet more traffic; the subject owns its building instead and gives up that exposure to avoid the rent.

The commercial market behind that rent is soft in places. Metro retail leasing totaled 119,000 square feet in 2025 and leased space fell 43% from the year before, with about 642,000 square feet available at the end of 2025 [^27]. Sales of existing retail property rose 41% over the same year, and the average base office lease rate was $15.12 per square foot [^27]. A buyer of an existing building may find better terms than a ground-up build.

Land

The research records no Fargo land sale or listing, so the study neither prints nor cites one. The model carries land at a round {{case:v.in_land_per_acre}} an acre, and the appraisal and an executed land contract replace it. The spread between a prime retail pocket and a corridor site is large, and the lender should order a land comparison before the budget is final.

Property tax

The research holds no Cass County or Fargo mill levy, assessment ratio or abatement schedule. The model carries a planning rate of {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of the cost basis, and the actual levy is a lender condition, along with any tax incentive the city offers for a manufacturer.

Weather

Severe storms, tornadoes and straight-line winds on June 20 and 21, 2025 led to a federal disaster declaration, DR-4888, on September 11, 2025, covering 19 counties including Cass [^8]. A production building with a large roof, outdoor tanks and a patio is exposed to wind. Insurance quotes and the structural design should price that in.

The pint price behind the taproom figure is an assumption. Events, merchandise and visiting vendors add {{case:v.in_other_pct}} of taproom beer sales.

Revenue grows from {{case:v.rev_y1}} in Year 1 to {{case:v.rev_y3}} in Year 3 and {{case:v.rev_y5}} in Year 5. The mix matters more than the total: a year in which distributors take less than expected leaves the taproom to carry the loan, and the sensitivity table below tests exactly that.

{{case:loan}}

An SBA 504 project is split three ways. The rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) [^6] set the split as Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^6]. The borrower's minimum rises with risk: 10% in the standard case, 15% for a new business or special purpose property, and 20% for both [^6].

A brewery is a new business here, and the study treats the building as special purpose, because the SBA's example list of special purpose property names wineries and describes a limited-market layout; whether a lender treats a brewhouse the same way is a lender condition, and it changes the minimum between 15% and 20%. At the sponsor's {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} the proposal fails the equity test and the coverage test, even though the debenture of {{case:v.debenture_proposed}} would fit under the manufacturer limit of $5.5 million [^6]. The ordinary limit is $5 million [^6].

The manufacturer limit is relevant because a brewery's primary activity is making beer. It applies to a small manufacturer, and the lender must document the business's size and its production activity. The taproom does not defeat the claim, but a lender will want to see that production is the primary activity and that the taproom does not outgrow it. The same classification affects fees: the FY2027 notice waives the 504 fees for both 504 fees waived for the same three categories, including 504 refinance loans [^28], which include manufacturers. The model does not take the waiver and charges the standard upfront fee of 0.50% [^28], so a waiver would be an upside.

Job creation is a second manufacturer rule. A 504 debenture is measured against the jobs the project creates or retains: $95,000 of debenture per job in the standard case and $150,000 for manufacturers and energy projects, under Federal Register 2025-19072 (90 FR 47117), 504 loans approved on or after October 1, 2025 [^6]. Because the restructured debenture is small, the job count the project must show is modest compared with the production and taproom payroll in the model, and the lender should still ask for a payroll plan with start dates.

The debenture rate of {{case:v.in_debenture_rate}} and the bank rate of {{case:v.in_tpl_rate}} are assumptions. Prime was 7.00% in the Federal Reserve's H.15 release [^29], and the bank rate adds a half point for a start-up production business. The program's maturities are 10, 20 or 25 years [^6]. The owner-occupancy test asks the operating borrower to occupy 60% of a new building [^7], which a brewery that fills its own building meets easily.

Whether a feasibility study is needed is decided case by case: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^7].

{{case:inputs}}

The risks below follow from the numbers above.

  1. The taproom does not fill

    The model's second year depends on a taproom busy enough to sell {{case:v.in_tap_share}} of the early barrels. The metro adds jobs slowly, with a twelve-month statewide change of only 0.2% [^4], and the Census count of at least four county establishments in the class [^5] means a newcomer fights for regulars. A late ramp fails the Year 2 test.

  2. Distributors take less than modelled

    Wholesale carries the rest of the volume. If a distributor drops the line, the taproom must carry the debt alone, and the sensitivity table shows how little room there is. The program has funded the class only twice in the state in ten years [^26], so the CDC and bank have little local precedent.

  3. Labor is scarce

    A metro jobless rate of 2.3% [^2] and about 41.6 thousand leisure and hospitality jobs statewide [^13] mean wages for bartenders and brewers may rise faster than the model's inflation input.

  4. The district weakens

    Downtown retail vacancy stood at 18.3% in January 2026 and leased retail space fell 43% in 2025 [^27], and downtown apartment vacancy was just over 16% [^25]. A taproom that counts on downtown foot traffic could find less of it. The subject's corridor location reduces the exposure but does not remove it.

  5. Costs run high

    Land, building and equipment are assumptions, and every dollar of overrun adds to a project that already needs a large contribution. Building permits drop sharply in winter, with 3 in January 2026 [^21], so a late start can push the opening past the planned ramp.

  6. Weather and wastewater

    A June 2025 storm brought a federal declaration for Cass County [^8], and an unresolved wastewater charge can add recurring cost. Both are lender conditions.

  7. Rates and the loan market

    The bank and debenture rates are assumptions around a Prime rate of 7.00% [^29]. A higher rate at closing lowers coverage, and the restructured case has little spare cushion.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open.

  1. Competitor census

    A named competitor census with each competitor's own website or county assessor record, separating breweries from distilleries, with taproom hours, tap counts, distribution footprint and packaging. The Census count is a floor of at least four county establishments [^5], and no research document names the field.

  2. Site, title and zoning

    The parcel identifier from the county assessor, a written zoning determination that covers production and a taproom, the parking requirement, the appraisal, the survey and the sale history of the land.

  3. Utilities, wastewater and taxes

    The electric, gas, water and sewer providers, the capacity and wastewater discharge charges in force, any pre-treatment requirement, the commercial mill levy and any manufacturer tax incentive.

  4. Traffic, flood and environmental

    A state count station and annual average daily traffic for the corridor, the FEMA flood zone and panel, a Phase I environmental assessment, and a wind-load review in light of the 2025 declaration [^8].

  5. Licences and taxes on beer

    Federal brewer's notice and permit, state licences for production, wholesale and on-premises sale, and the federal and state excise rates, which are an assumption in the model.

  6. Volume, price and distribution

    Signed distribution letters, a taproom price list, pint and barrel volumes from comparable operators, and a demand study with drive times, since the research holds no brewery sales data.

  7. Costs and equipment

    Contractor bids, vendor quotes for the brewhouse, tanks and packaging line, an appraisal of equipment for the lender's collateral, and quotes for property, liability and liquor liability insurance.

  8. Program items

    Confirmation of the special purpose classification, the manufacturer classification and size, the job plan against the per-job figure, the CDC and bank terms at pricing, owner eligibility documents under the SOP [^6], and the NAICS code used for any fee waiver [^28].

  9. Sponsor capital

    Proof of the {{case:v.equity}} contribution in the restructured case, its source and seasoning, and a plan for the additional working capital if the ramp is slow.
