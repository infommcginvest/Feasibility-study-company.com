If you are searching for a retail store feasibility study consultant, or a retail store feasibility study company that shows its arithmetic, this page is a worked example. It follows one hypothetical owner-occupied retail store in Casper, Wyoming, financed with an SBA 504 loan, from the public record of the local economy to a debt service coverage test. Ask us to run the same feasibility study on your store.

The subject is generic: a single-story store of about {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet with a sales floor, a stockroom, a loading dock and an outdoor yard, on about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres along the Yellowstone Highway (US-20/26) corridor in Natrona County. It sells work clothing, hand tools, fasteners and ranch supplies to the trades, energy and agricultural households that make up much of the local payroll. The borrower's operating company is the only occupant. No parcel, owner, brand or listing is behind it. Every market figure below is public and dated, and every model figure is computed from the inputs near the end of the page, each of which carries a source or the word assumption. The project belongs to our retail practice, the town sits in Wyoming, and the program rules are set out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

{{case:glance}}

The short answer on demand is that the customers exist and the growth does not. Natrona County had 80.526 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 [^1], and the Casper metro area, which is the county alone, counted 39.9 thousand nonfarm jobs in September 2026, a figure the Bureau of Labor Statistics printed with a twelve-month change of -2.2% [^5]. Covered employment in the county rose by 361 jobs, or 0.9%, in the year to the first quarter of 2025, with construction and local government both losing jobs [^11]. A store here is selling into a market that is holding its size, not adding customers.

The customers it would sell to are paid more than the metro average. The metro's mean hourly wage across all occupations was $28.93 in May 2025, while installation, maintenance and repair workers averaged $30.75 and power-line installers $100,820 a year [^6]. The metro's location quotient for mobile heavy equipment mechanics was 4.26, which means the trade is far more concentrated in Casper than in the country as a whole [^6]. Households with that kind of pay are the customers a trade supply store courts, and they are a narrower base than a general shopping population.

The competitive answer is a count and not a list. No document in the public research names a store that would compete with the subject, and a name taken from a map or a search page would be a claim nobody confirmed. The only measure of the field is the Census Bureau's County Business Patterns: at least 206 retail establishments in the county in 2023 [^3]. The competitors section sets out that proxy and turns the named census into the first lender condition.

The online answer matters for a goods retailer. Online shopping was 8.6% of Wyoming's sales and use tax collections in fiscal 2025, up from 0.7% in 2017, and online collections grew 12.2% in the year [^13]. Hardware, apparel and supplies all travel by parcel. A store that sells trade goods has to give customers a reason to drive: stock on the shelf today, a counter that knows the product, and delivery to a job site.

Under the sponsor's proposal of {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} equity, which is the right tier for a new business, the project costs {{case:v.tpc_proposed}} and Year 2 coverage is {{case:v.dscr_y2_proposed}} against a floor of 1.15x. The store works, narrowly, once the debt is trimmed to its ramp.

Pay and the trades

Retail follows income, and the income in Casper is concentrated in work that happens outdoors and on equipment. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 2,450 jobs in installation, maintenance and repair occupations in the metro in May 2025, 6.1% of local employment, and the group's mean hourly wage was $30.75 against $28.93 for all occupations [^6]. Power-line installers averaged $100,820 a year, and the hourly mean of $48.47 sits well above the national mean of $33.54 that the same release prints for all occupations [^6]. The mix is the reason the subject is a trade supply store and not a general merchant. The study's thesis is that tradespeople buy consumables on a repeating cycle and value a counter that stocks the right size. The research does not test that thesis, and the sales assumptions below carry it.

The location quotient for mobile heavy equipment mechanics, 4.26, says that the trade is more than four times as concentrated in Casper as in the country [^6]. A related release row counts 340 industrial machinery mechanics in the metro [^6]. These are customers that a regional store can serve by name. They are also tied to the energy and mining cycle, which is the subject of the next subsection.

Jobs: steady at best

The county's job record is mixed. Covered employment rose by 710 jobs, or 1.8%, between the fourth quarters of 2023 and 2024, with covered payroll up $36.8 million, or 5.8% [^10]. By the first quarter of 2025 the year-on-year gain had narrowed to 361 jobs, or 0.9%, with payroll up $12.3 million, or 2.1%, and the release names construction and local government as the sectors that lost jobs [^11]. The September 2026 reading for the metro, 39.9 thousand nonfarm jobs, carried a twelve-month change of -2.2% [^5]. Trade, transportation and utilities, the sector that holds most retail and wholesale work, stood at 9.2 thousand [^5].

Statewide, the retail sector is the weak signal. Wyoming's retail trade sector lost 63 more jobs than it gained in the fourth quarter of 2025, a net rate of -0.2% [^14]. The same release counts 19,197 private-sector job gains and 17,023 losses in the quarter, so the labor market churns around a small net change [^14]. A new store hires from that churn, and it opens into a sector that is not expanding its payroll.

Spending: the county held up

The best sign for a goods retailer is tax. Natrona County's sales and use tax collections rose 3.1% in fiscal 2025, in a year when the statewide total fell 2.2% [^12]. The state's own fiscal summary says the same from the other side: total collections were $1.3 billion, down 2.2%, and the mining industry's contribution fell 21.9% [^13]. Casper's resilience in a year that hit the mining base suggests that its retail spending rests on more than the energy cycle, and a lender should still ask for the borrower's sales history before it leans on a single year of a news report.

Visitors add a little. Visit Casper credits its marketing with $34.2 million of economic impact in 2025, more than $2 million above the year before [^8], and the airport boarded 103,763 passengers in 2025, the first year above 100,000 in a decade, according to its annual report [^9]. A trade store sells little to a tourist, but the airport is a sign of business travel.

Housing and household means

The register describes a household base that is comfortable and not wealthy. Median household income in the county was $72,156 in the 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey, below the state's $76,176 [^2]. Personal income per resident in 2024 was $79,903 in the county against $86,477 in the state [^4]. The county's median home value was $273,000 [^2], and the median listing price in September 2026 was $401,875 [^7]. Owners of those homes buy repair and yard supplies, which is the second customer group for a store that stocks fasteners, paint-room tools and fencing.

The statewide backdrop

Casper is a state's regional center, and the state's numbers set the weather for it. Wyoming had 588.753 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 [^16]. The Bureau of Labor Statistics put the state's employed residents at 276.4 thousand and nonfarm jobs at 295.5 thousand in September 2026, a twelve-month gain of 0.9% [^15]. Within that, trade, transportation and utilities held 52.4 thousand jobs with a twelve-month change of -1.3%, government 71.3 thousand with -0.7%, and education and health services 29.7 thousand with 0.3% [^15]. Covered employment statewide rose by 717 jobs, or 0.3%, in the year to the first quarter of 2025, and covered payroll by $140.7 million, or 3.4% [^11]. The state economy is a flat one in which retail and public jobs are slipping and health care is edging up.

Output tells the same story with a lag. Wyoming's real gross domestic product was 40,511.7 million chained 2017 dollars in the second quarter of 2026, down from 40,679.7 in the first [^17]. The state's Economic Health Index stood at 106.7 for March 2026, against 106.5 a year earlier [^23]. Neither number is large, and the second is a reminder that the state's own indicator is not falling.

The base behind the trades payroll is energy and ranching. Wyoming withdrew 1,197,527 million cubic feet of natural gas in 2025 and marketed 938,131 million, and its dry production was 998,117 million cubic feet in 2024, the latest year with a value on the page [^21]. On January 1, 2026 the state held 1,160,000 head of cattle, of which 621,000 were beef cows [^22]. A store that sells ranch and field supplies draws on both: the first through the hands who run the equipment, the second through the households who run the stock. Neither figure is a forecast of the store's sales, and both are the kind of backdrop a lender asks to see.

Housing is the last piece. The state's median home listing price was $496,500 in September 2026 and its active listings numbered 2,589 [^18,19]. The Federal Housing Finance Agency's all-transactions index for Wyoming read 526.96 in the second quarter of 2026, on a base of 100 in the first quarter of 1980 [^20]. Natrona County's median listing price of $401,875 sits below the state's [^7], which fits a city that is more affordable than the resort counties and is part of why a trades household can own a home there.

A competitor census names each rival store with its size, product lines, prices, hours and website. This study could not build one. None of the research documents for Casper, Natrona County or Wyoming names a hardware, farm supply, workwear or general retail store, and a name found only on a map or in a search result would be a claim that nobody confirmed. The study does not invent a competitor and does not name one it cannot tie to a dated public document. The named census, with each rival's own website or county assessor record, is therefore the first lender condition for this project, and the table below is a supply count, not a census.

What the proxy count says

Natrona County has at least 25.77 retail establishments per 10,000 residents, against at least 27.67 for Wyoming as a whole [^3]. The county is slightly less dense in stores than the state, which includes small towns whose stores serve a wide rural area. The count does not say whether larger stores or fewer stores explain the gap. It is the first piece of evidence that the market is not an empty one, and it is not evidence that there is room.

The class holds at least 3,489 paid employees across those establishments, about seventeen an establishment [^3]. That is an average over everything from a gas-station-sized shop to a large department store, and it says little about a trade supply store, which would sit at the larger end. The lender should read the count as a ceiling on the number of rivals in one respect, because many of the 206 establishments sell food, pharmacy items or clothing and do not compete for a contractor's order, and as a floor on the pressure in another, because the contractor's other choices include a wholesaler's counter, a dealer's parts desk and a parcel from a catalog.

What the census must still show

A named census would list, for each rival, the building size, the categories on the shelf, whether it holds contractor accounts or extends credit, its hours, its delivery radius, the year opened and its assessed value from the county assessor. The model needs two figures from it above all: the sales per square foot that comparable stores achieve, and the share of the trade's spending that goes to counters in town and not to parcels. Neither is in the public record this study could read, so the model carries sales per square foot and gross margin as assumptions and lets the sensitivity table show how far each can move.

Online competition

Online shopping was 8.6% of the state's sales and use tax collections in fiscal 2025, up from 0.7% in 2017 [^13]. For heavy, bulky or urgent items a parcel is a weaker substitute, and the subject leans on those. For work clothing, small tools and standard fasteners it is a direct rival, and the model's gross margin is set with that in mind.

The subject is a hypothetical site of about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres on the Yellowstone Highway corridor in Natrona County. Its parcel, zoning district, access permit and utility territory are conditions to confirm. The research did not retrieve a traffic count for the corridor, a zoning district for the site, a land sale or a building permit count, and it listed each as not found. The paragraphs below describe what each gap means for the loan.

Zoning, access and traffic

A trade supply store with an outdoor yard needs commercial or light industrial zoning that permits outdoor display and storage, a curb cut that trucks and trailers can use, and a lot that drains. Whether the city or the county allows that on the site, with what setbacks and screening, decides what can be built. The research could not establish any of it: the city's website returned an error to the researcher, and no state traffic count was found. The lender needs a written zoning determination for the actual parcel and the average daily traffic at the site. A store of this kind lives on repeat customers, so visibility matters less than a restaurant's, but a turning lane and an apron wide enough for a flatbed matter a great deal.

Utilities and the ground

A heated building of {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet draws a good deal of gas and power in a Wyoming winter, and the model carries site work of {{case:v.in_sitework}} for paving, drainage, utility taps and lighting. No provider, tap fee or fire flow was confirmed. A Phase I environmental assessment is part of the allowance for professional fees, and it matters here for a particular reason: the corridor serves oilfield and industrial users, and a lender will want to know what the previous use of the lot was.

Land

The research holds no Natrona County land sale, assessed value or asking price. The only price in the file is a house price: the county's median listing price was $401,875 in September 2026 [^7]. A listing's price describes one property, and a house price describes none of the land a store needs, so the study cites neither as a land price. The model carries land at {{case:v.in_land_per_acre}} an acre, a round planning figure that an appraisal and an executed land contract replace. A lender should expect the appraisal to move the project.

Property tax

The research does not hold Natrona County's mill levy, assessed valuation or commercial assessment ratio. It holds one statewide figure: K-12 mill levy income of $353.7 million in 2025, up 26.1% from $280 million in 2019 [^24]. The same article attributes about 6.6% of statewide property tax to commercial property [^24]. Neither number prices a levy on the subject. The model carries property tax at {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of land, building and site cost, and the actual levy on a commercial parcel is a lender condition.

{{case:revenue}}

Revenue is net store sales, which is the price of what the store sells less returns and discounts. The model builds it from the size of the building and the sales each square foot produces in each year. It does not split sales into categories, because the research holds no sales mix and no category margin, and a split would only look precise. A single line makes the assumption plain: the whole case turns on how much a square foot sells and what the store keeps from each dollar.

The model opens the store at about two thirds of its stabilized sales per square foot and reaches {{case:v.in_sales_psf}} in Year 3, the stabilized year, with a small gain in each of the two years after. Revenue grows from {{case:v.rev_y1}} in Year 1 to {{case:v.rev_y3}} in Year 3. That ramp reflects a store that opens to a customer base it has to win, in a county whose employment grew 0.9% in the latest year the research reached [^11] and whose metro nonfarm jobs fell 2.2% in the twelve months to September 2026 [^5]. The model holds sales per square foot flat in nominal terms after Year 5 and does not assume that the county grows into the store. If the borrower's own sales history from an existing location is available, it replaces the ramp and the lender should ask for it.

Gross margin is {{case:v.in_gross_margin}} of net sales after shrink and freight. The research holds no Wyoming retail margin, so this figure is a planning input, and the sensitivity table lets it move by a tenth of its value either way. Gross profit is {{case:v.gp_y3}} in Year 3.

{{case:loan}}

An SBA 504 project is split three ways. The rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^25]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or a limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the business is both [^25]. The subject is a new business, so the sponsor's proposal of {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} is the right tier and the equity test passes as proposed. The failure is in coverage, not in the contribution.

Whether the building is a limited or special purpose property is a question for the CDC. SBA describes such a property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^25]. A pre-engineered shell with a sales floor and a loading dock can be sold to another store, a distributor or a contractor, which is the reason the model does not assume special purpose treatment. A CDC that took the other view would move the contribution to the top tier, and its written view is a lender condition.

At the proposed contribution the debenture would be {{case:v.debenture_proposed}}, under the $5 million limit [^25], so the size test passes. At {{case:v.equity_pct}} the debenture falls to {{case:v.debenture}}, {{case:v.debenture_position}}, and the bank lends {{case:v.tpl}}, {{case:v.tpl_share}} of cost. The program offers 10, 20 or 25 years and follows the larger asset class; land, building and site work are the majority of proceeds here, so the model uses a 25-year debenture and a 25-year bank amortization [^25]. Because the bank's share is fixed at half of cost, extra equity can only displace the debenture, the cheaper and longer money, which is why a restructured project keeps less of the program than the sponsor hoped for.

The owner-occupancy test is the reason this case belongs in the 504 program at all. For a new building the test is 60% [^25]. The operating company occupies the whole store, so the test is met if the company owns the property, or leases it from an affiliate that the CDC accepts. A landlord who leased the store to an unrelated retailer would fail the test. The debenture rate is an assumption of {{case:v.in_debenture_rate}}, set when the debenture is sold, and the bank rate is an assumption of {{case:v.in_tpl_rate}}: Prime was 7.00% in the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [^27], plus half a point for a new store, and SBA caps the third-party spread at 6% over New York Prime [^25]. The FY2027 upfront fee of 0.50% on the debenture is added to project cost [^26].

Whether a feasibility study is needed is decided case by case: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^28]. On a ground-up store with a new operating company, a lender may ask for one. Our retail hub explains how a store is valued on its cash flow and its building separately. The rural fee waiver is not assumed: whether Natrona County meets the program's rural definition is a lender condition.

{{case:sensitivity}}

Rows move sales per square foot by up to a fifth in every year, and columns move the gross margin by up to a tenth of its value, as the headers show. The table reads Year 2, the test year, on the restructured loan, and its range runs from {{case:v.sens_min}} to {{case:v.sens_max}}. The spread is wide because operating costs hardly move when sales do: a store pays its staff, its heat and its insurance whether the yard is busy or empty, so a change in sales or margin falls almost entirely on income. {{case:inputs}}

No input comes straight from a source. Everything is an assumption, including land, construction, sales per square foot, gross margin, staffing and the operating allowances, and the interest rates borrow a published benchmark or a rule. The research for Casper holds market evidence on people, jobs, pay and tax collections, but no retail unit economics, so the assumptions carry the model. The most important to verify are sales per square foot, gross margin and the cost of land, in that order.

  1. The ramp is slower than modelled

    The Year 2 test leaves no room. A store that wins fewer customers in its second year fails the floor, and the research holds no sales series or store-level data to show how fast a new store fills in this market.

  2. The jobs base keeps shrinking

    The metro's nonfarm jobs carried a twelve-month change of -2.2% in September 2026 [^5], and construction and local government lost jobs in the county in the year to the first quarter of 2025 [^11]. A store is selling to employees of the sectors that are losing them.

  3. The energy and mining cycle turns again

    Mining sales and use tax collections fell 21.9% statewide in fiscal 2025 [^13], and gas withdrawals in 2025 were 1,197,527 million cubic feet against 1,343,290 in 2024 [^21]. Many of the store's customers work in or beside the sector.

  4. Online sellers take the standard items

    Online shopping was 8.6% of state sales and use tax collections in fiscal 2025 and grew 12.2% in the year [^13]. Work apparel and small tools are the first lines to move.

  5. Margin is lower than assumed

    No Wyoming retail margin is in the research. A margin one step below the planning figure takes Year 2 under the floor, and a rival that cuts prices on common items sets the ceiling on what the store can charge.

  6. Land and construction cost more than the model

    The county's median home listing price was $401,875 in September 2026 [^7], and the model's land and building costs are round planning prices. Every dollar above them adds debt to a project that already needs a large contribution.

  7. Staff are hard to find at the assumed pay

    The metro paid $28.93 an hour on average across all occupations in May 2025 [^6]. A store that pays well below that competes with its own customers' employers for people.

  8. The CDC treats the building differently

    A view that the building is special purpose would raise the borrower's contribution to the top tier. A finding that the borrower does not occupy enough of a new building would end the 504 structure altogether.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open. Several group related items that the research reported as not found.

  1. A named competitor census

    A census of rival stores in Casper and the nearby trade area, with each competitor's own website or county assessor record, its building size, product lines, contractor accounts, delivery radius and year opened. The register's County Business Patterns count is a proxy and is not a census [^3].

  2. Sales and margin evidence

    The borrower's own sales by month and category from any existing location, vendor terms and the gross margin by category, and the share of sales on account. The research found no Casper retail sales, margin or inventory-turn figure.

  3. Site, title and zoning

    The parcel identifier from the county assessor, a written zoning determination that permits the store with an outdoor yard, setbacks and screening requirements, and the appraisal, survey and sale history of the land. The research found no land sale.

  4. Traffic, access and environmental

    A state count station and annual average daily traffic for the corridor at the site, the access permit and apron requirements, the FEMA flood zone and panel, and a Phase I environmental assessment. The research found no traffic count.

  5. Utilities

    The electric, gas, water and sewer providers for the parcel, their connection fees and the fire flow. No provider was confirmed.

  6. Taxes and local rules

    Natrona County's assessed valuation, mill levy and commercial assessment ratio, which the research did not find, and the city's sales tax treatment of the store's sales. The only property-tax figures found are statewide [^24].

  7. Costs and equipment

    Contractor bids for the shell and site work with the labor and materials premium of the Casper market, quotes for racking, point-of-sale and the forklift, and quotes for property, liability and inventory insurance.

  8. Wages and staffing

    The Casper wages for retail occupations from the Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational release, and a staffing plan by shift. The research holds the all-occupation mean of $28.93 an hour [^6] and no retail wage.

  9. Occupancy and special purpose

    The CDC's written view on whether the building is a limited or special purpose property, and evidence that the operating company occupies the share of a new building that the program requires. The structure of the owning entity, if it differs from the operating company, is part of the same file [^25].

  10. Program items

    The CDC and bank terms set at pricing, owner eligibility documents under the SOP, the jobs standard for the debenture of $95,000 per job [^25], and the rural, HUBZone and Opportunity Zone status of the tract, which were not found.

  11. Opening reserve

    A funded reserve for the Year 1 shortfall between operating income and debt service, held until the stabilized year is reached.
