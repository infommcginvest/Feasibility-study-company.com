The demand answer is a qualified yes. Larimer County's average weekly wage was $1,627 in the first quarter of 2026, up 11.5% over the year and the largest increase among Colorado's nine large counties [^2], and the register puts the county's median household income at $93,765 [^22]. Net tuition at the model's level is a real but bounded call on that income. The weakness is employment: covered jobs in the county were 167,827 in March 2026, down 0.4% over the year [^2], so the wage gain has come without job growth.

Income and what tuition asks of it

The median Larimer County household earns $93,765 a year, and the county's per capita personal income of $73,894 is higher than the ACS per capita figure of $51,165 because it counts transfers and investment income as well as pay [^22,24]. A school is sold to the household, so the median household is the better yardstick. Net tuition of {{case:v.in_tuition_net}} in the third year, after financial aid, asks a median household for a meaningful share of its income for one child and a much larger share for two or three. That is why a school of this kind fills from the upper half of the income range, and why its enrollment is sensitive to wages and to housing cost. The model holds enrollment close to the school's own record and lets tuition rise about two percent a year.

Wages are rising, jobs are not

The county's average weekly wage reached $1,627 in the first quarter of 2026, up 11.5% over the year, and the release notes it as the largest rise among the nine large counties [^2]. Covered employment, however, was 167,827 in March 2026, down 0.4% [^2]. A wage gain with a small loss of jobs can mean that a few high-paying employers drove the average, and the figure is preliminary. For a school the safer reading is that incumbent households are doing well and that the pool of newly arriving households is not growing quickly. The metro's mean hourly wage across all occupations was $33.95 in May 2025 [^3], which is also the wage a school's teachers and aides can compare with when they weigh a job offer.

The metro's gross domestic product was 26,974.178 million dollars in 2023, the last year of a series that BEA has since marked discontinued [^7]. It is a scale reference and says nothing about this year's growth.

Housing cost and household formation

The Fort Collins metro's median listing price was $567,500 in September 2026 [^6], close to the register's median home value of $569,100 for the county [^22], and median gross rent was $1,716 a month [^22]. Housing takes a large share of a family's budget here, so a family that pays tuition has usually already solved housing. Permits for 2,436 new private housing units were issued in the county in 2025 [^5], which is the closest the research comes to counting new households. A school wants those new households within a short drive, and the permit record does not say where they are being built.

The state's housing market sits close to the metro's. The statewide median listing price was $564,000 in September 2026, with 34,082 active listings, and the Federal Housing Finance Agency's all-transactions index for Colorado read 853.19 in the second quarter of 2026 on a base of 100 in the first quarter of 1980 [^15,16,17].

Colorado's population and births

Colorado had 6,012,561 residents on July 1, 2025, up 0.4% over the year [^1]. Growth came from abroad: net international migration added 15,356 people while net domestic migration was negative 12,100, a total net gain of 3,256 [^1]. The state recorded 65,380 births between July 2024 and June 2025, up 4.6% on the prior year and the highest since 2017 [^1]. Births matter to a K-8 school with a long delay: a child born in that year reaches kindergarten in about five years, and the study does not extrapolate from one year's count. The count shows that the supply of young children is not shrinking in the state, which is a conditions check and not a forecast for Larimer County. The county's own birth, school-age and enrollment counts were not found, and they are lender conditions.

The state economy behind the families

Colorado's household survey counted 3,047.4 thousand employed residents and 131.9 thousand unemployed persons in September 2026, seasonally adjusted and preliminary, and employers reported 2,973.8 thousand nonfarm payroll jobs, up 0.4% from a year earlier [^4]. The state's covered employment was 2,848,514 in March 2026, down 0.7% over the year, and its average weekly wage was $1,774, up 5.4% [^2]. The same pattern appears at the state level as in the county: pay rising and jobs flat. Professional and business services employed 491.5 thousand people and leisure and hospitality 359.1 thousand in September 2026 [^11,12]. The first is a broad sector with many salaried professionals; the second employs many households whose hours and income can vary with the season, and a school should expect more requests for tuition assistance from them.

State output was 609,250.7 million dollars at a seasonally adjusted annual rate in the second quarter of 2026, and 462,626.5 million chained 2017 dollars in real terms [^13,14]. Colorado's visitor economy is large: travel spending reached a record $29.2 billion in 2025, up about 2% from the prior year, and the Colorado Tourism Office counts 187,860 tourism-supported jobs [^20,21]. These are state figures, and the research found no tourism figure for Larimer County.

The state budget is tight. Legislative Council Staff cut its general fund revenue forecast by $304 million versus its September forecast in December 2025, according to a news report on the forecast [^18]. Public school finance is one of the claims on that budget, and families compare a private school with the public alternative, so a squeeze on public school budgets can send families toward a private school, while a squeeze on family budgets can send them away. The research does not say which effect dominates, so the model assumes neither.

A census of competitors lists each school that would bid for the same families, with a page the school controls or the county assessor's record of its campus. For this case the research holds no news article, broker report or agency list that names a private school, a charter school or a school district campus in Fort Collins, Loveland or Larimer County. Naming a school the record does not name would be invention, so the census is a lender condition: a named competitor census with each competitor's own website or county assessor record.

What the supply table tells a newcomer

The count is small beside the county's households, which the register puts at 154,232 [^22]. It would be a mistake to read that ratio as a gap. Each establishment in the class has a catchment, grades and a price, and a count carries none of them. What the table does prove is that the business of running a school is established here, so a lender will expect the borrower to show a position among existing schools and not a vacuum.

The school in this case has one advantage that the count cannot measure: it already has families. A new campus does not have to win a first cohort, only to keep its present one through a move and add seats. That is why the model starts from the school's record and why the first lender condition is its enrollment history. What the study cannot supply from public records is the rest of the field: which schools sit within a short drive, which grades they serve, what they charge, how many applicants they turn away, and whether a new campus nearby is on the way. The lender conditions ask for all of these.

The alternative the family compares

Every private school competes first with the public school a child is assigned to and with other families' choices among public options. The research holds no public school roster, enrollment or capacity figure for Larimer County, so the study cannot state how many seats sit empty or full. It treats the public alternative as an unquantified competitor and makes it a condition: the district's enrollment and capacity trend, the open-enrollment and charter options in the school's drive area, and any state or district tuition support program for which the school's families qualify.

The subject is a hypothetical site of about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres between Fort Collins and Loveland. Its parcel, zoning district, utility territory and flood status are conditions to confirm. The research could not retrieve a parcel identifier, a zoning code, a traffic count, a utility fee schedule or a flood map for any site in the county, so the paragraphs below set out what the record does show and what each missing item would cost.

Build new, or buy and convert

A school can occupy a purpose-built campus or a converted commercial building, and the record speaks only to the second. CBRE's Northern Colorado office report for the first half of 2026 puts the direct average asking rate at $16.81 per square foot on a triple-net basis, flat on the prior period, and shows net absorption of 8,000 square feet [^8]. The same report notes no new completions in the half [^8]. The report prices leases, not sales, and it covers the Fort Collins, Loveland and Greeley submarkets together.

The industrial market is the other candidate for conversion, because a school needs a large floor plate and a lot of parking. CBRE reports net absorption of 189,000 square feet in the first half of 2026 for Northern Colorado, excluding one large facility [^9]. Matthews' small-bay report for the first quarter of 2026 gives an asking rent of $14.30 per square foot, up 2.0% over the year, and shows 446,965 square feet under construction [^10]. Neither report prices a conversion. The case therefore models a new building on land, which isolates the school's own economics from a conversion's unknowns, and treats conversion as an alternative the sponsor should price before it commits to the new building.

Zoning, access and traffic

A school generates a short, intense peak twice a day, and the site's most important physical feature is a queue. The study carries site work that includes a drop-off lane long enough to hold vehicles off the public street, but the research holds no zoning district, permitted-use table, traffic count or access standard for any parcel here. Whether a school is a permitted or conditional use in the district, whether the city or the county reviews the plan, and what a traffic study will require for turning lanes are therefore lender conditions. A site that cannot clear its queue onto an arterial will be restricted by the road authority, and any access permit the road authority requires is among the facts to confirm.

Utilities, land and tax

The research found no water, sewer, electric or gas provider for the corridor and no tap or capacity fee. The model carries an allowance for taps and extension inside site work, and the provider list and fee schedule are conditions. Land is carried at a round {{case:v.in_land_per_acre}} an acre. The research holds a median listing price for homes in the Fort Collins metro of $567,500 in September 2026 [^6], which says the area is costly and prices no acre of land; an appraisal and an executed contract replace the planning figure. Property tax is a planning level of {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of cost for a for-profit school, which does not enjoy a nonprofit exemption; the research holds no mill levy or assessment ratio for the county, so the actual levy is a lender condition.

Hazards

Colorado has had recent wildfires and flooding. FEMA announced on September 28, 2026 that it approved more than $784,000 in Public Assistance funding for the state to manage recovery after the Aspen Acres Fire and other wildfires and flooding [^19]. The release does not place those events in Larimer County, and the study does not either. It is a reason for a lender to ask for the FEMA flood zone and panel, a wildfire hazard review, the insurer's quote for property and liability coverage, and an evacuation and shelter plan for the school, because insurers and fire authorities look hard at a campus that holds hundreds of children. A Phase I environmental assessment belongs in the budget before closing on land.

{{case:loan}}

An SBA 504 project is split three ways. The rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^25]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the project is both [^25]. The school in this case has operated for years, so the model takes the standard tier, which is the sponsor's proposal.

The tier is the first thing a CDC will question. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^25]. SBA's example list of special purpose property is not all-inclusive, a classroom building can serve uses other than the original school, and the CDC decides building by building [^25]. If it classified this campus as special purpose, the contribution would move to the 15% tier. The classification is a lender condition, and the restructured equity share in this case is well above either tier, so the answer would not change the structure.

The debenture is {{case:v.debenture}} as restructured, {{case:v.debenture_position}} of $5 million for a project that is not a small manufacturer [^25], and the bank lends {{case:v.tpl}}, {{case:v.tpl_share}} of cost. The program offers 10, 20 or 25 years and follows the larger asset class; land, building and site work are the majority of proceeds here, so the model uses a 25-year debenture and a 25-year bank amortization.

The owner-occupancy test is met by design, because the school occupies the whole building. For a new building the test is 60% (51% for an existing one) [^25]. A lender will still ask for the floor plan, because a school that leases a wing to a tenant, a church or a care provider would count that space against its own share.

The bank rate is an assumption of {{case:v.in_tpl_rate}}: Prime was 7.00% in the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [^27], plus half a point for a school real estate loan, and SBA caps the third-party spread at 6% over New York Prime [^25]. The FY2027 upfront fee of 0.50% on the debenture is added to project cost in the model [^26]. SBA's jobs standard is one job per $95,000 of debenture for loans approved on or after October 1, 2025, and the school's payroll records document it. The borrower must be a for-profit business, and the lender confirms eligibility under the SOP; the owner's tax status and the entity that owns the building and the entity that operates the school are lender conditions.

{{case:inputs}}

Every input is an assumption. The research for this town holds market evidence, rents and wages but no unit economics for a school: no tuition, enrollment, staffing or construction cost. Land, construction, enrollment, tuition, payroll and the operating allowances are therefore planning figures. The inputs to verify first are enrollment history, net tuition and teacher pay, in that order, because they set revenue and the largest cost; building cost follows.

  1. Families leave in the move

    An established school keeps most of its revenue only if its families follow it. A move to a new part of the corridor lengthens some commutes. A loss of ten students reduces coverage in the test year more than a drop in tuition does, and the break-even table shows how thin the cushion is.

  2. Jobs are not growing

    Larimer County's covered employment was 167,827 in March 2026, down 0.4% over the year, while the average weekly wage rose 11.5% [^2]. A wage rise with falling jobs can reverse. A household that loses a job moves a child to a public school first.

  3. Housing cost limits the pool

    The Fort Collins metro's median listing price was $567,500 in September 2026 [^6]. Families that spend heavily on housing have less to spend on tuition, and the new households arriving may be fewer than the permits suggest.

  4. The state budget squeezes the alternatives

    Legislative Council Staff cut its December 2025 general fund revenue forecast by $304 million [^18]. The effect on public school options, and on family finances, is uncertain, and the model assumes neither.

  5. Costs run high

    Land and construction are assumptions, and every dollar of overrun adds debt that the school can only just carry. Operating costs in an owned building, which include upkeep, utilities and property tax, may exceed the allowances.

  6. Teachers cost more than modelled

    The metro's mean hourly wage for all occupations was $33.95 in May 2025 [^3], and the county's weekly wage rose fast in early 2026 [^2]. A school that has to raise pay faster than the two percent growth in the model will see its margin narrow.

  7. Hazards and insurance

    FEMA reported funding for recovery from wildfires and flooding in Colorado on September 28, 2026 [^19]. A site in a hazard zone may cost more to insure or may be refused coverage, and a school cannot operate uninsured.

  8. The tier or the structure is questioned

    A CDC that classifies the campus as special purpose moves the equity tier up from the standard one [^25], and a lender that looks at the operating company and the property company together may require guaranties and a lease between them. The restructured equity share is already above both tiers.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open. Several group related items the research reported as not found.

  1. Competitor census

    A named competitor census with each competitor's own website or county assessor record: every private, charter and public school within a short drive of the site, with grades served, enrollment, tuition and discount, waitlist, year opened and any planned campus. The research named none, and the Census count is an establishment total only.

  2. Enrollment and tuition history

    Three years of audited enrollment by grade, applications, acceptances, withdrawals and waitlist; the tuition schedule, financial aid awarded, collection rate and bad debt; and the retention of families through grade transitions.

  3. Entity structure and eligibility

    The legal entities that own the building and operate the school, the tax status of each, a lease between them at market, and the lender's eligibility confirmation under the SOP [^25]. The model assumes a for-profit operator.

  4. Site, title and zoning

    The parcel identifier from the county assessor, a written zoning determination that a school is a permitted use on it, the plan review authority, an appraisal and survey, and the sale history of the land.

  5. Traffic and access

    A traffic study of the drop-off and pick-up queue, the road authority's access requirements, a count for the nearest arterial and the school's plan for staggered arrival.

  6. Utilities, fees and taxes

    The water, sewer, electric and gas providers for the parcel, the tap and capacity fees in force, the county mill levy, the assessment ratio for commercial property and the combined rate for the parcel.

  7. Hazards and environment

    The FEMA flood zone and panel, a wildfire hazard review, an insurer's quote for property and liability coverage and a Phase I environmental site assessment [^19].

  8. Costs and equipment

    Contractor bids, an architect's building program, a construction cost comparison against the national level, vendor quotes for technology and furnishings, and an appraisal of the finished building on a school use and on an alternative use.

  9. Staffing and wages

    The school's pay scale and a local teacher salary survey, benefit costs, the staffing ratio by grade and turnover for three years.

  10. Demand gaps

    The county's school-age population and its birth counts, the district's enrollment and capacity trend, drive-time demographics for the site, and a catchment map of current families. The research found no Larimer County birth, school-age or enrollment figure.

  11. Conversion alternative

    A priced alternative to the new building: a purchase and conversion of a commercial building, with the broker's sale comparables, the conversion scope and the cost, because the Northern Colorado reports price leases and not sales [^8,9,10].

  12. Program items

    The CDC and bank terms set at pricing, the debenture rate on the day it is sold, the jobs documentation, the CDC's classification of the campus as general or special purpose, and Larimer County 504 loan history from SBA's public loan data. The rural, HUBZone and Opportunity Zone status of the tract were not found.

    A funded reserve for the Year 1 shortfall between operating income and debt service, held until the stabilized year is reached.
