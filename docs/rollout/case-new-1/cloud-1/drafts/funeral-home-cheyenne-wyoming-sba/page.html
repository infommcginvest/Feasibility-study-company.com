Feasibility Study Company prepares funeral home feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A study joins a dated market read, a census of the competing field, a priced acquisition and a coverage test that shows how much debt a deathcare business can carry. This page runs that work on one hypothetical deal: the purchase of an established, independent funeral home in Cheyenne, Laramie County, Wyoming, near the I-25 and I-80 corridor, financed with an SBA 7(a) loan. If you are looking for a funeral home feasibility study consultant or a funeral home feasibility study company for a Cheyenne purchase, ask us to run the same work on your deal.

The subject is generic: a single-story chapel with arrangement rooms and a prep room on about one acre (a round assumption), together with the firm's case files and a small preneed book, bought as one going concern. No name, street address, listing, broker or asking price is used, and the seller's results are labelled illustrative wherever they appear. Every market figure is public and dated. Every model figure is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed near the end, and each input carries a source or the word assumption. The deal belongs to our funeral home and deathcare guide, the town sits in Wyoming, and the lending rules are laid out in our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice area.

Federal rules say that SBA may require a feasibility study on a loan: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^21]. Whether yours does is decided file by file, and a lender often asks for market evidence on a deathcare purchase before SBA does.

{{case:glance}}

The market read is steady rather than strong, and it is incomplete in the one place a funeral home cares about most. Laramie County counted 102,938 residents on July 1, 2025 [^1], and its population grew 1.2% in the year to that date with 1,120 more people arriving than leaving [^2]. The Cheyenne metro area held 49.1 thousand nonfarm jobs in September 2026, 1.4% fewer than a year earlier [^4]. Deaths, not jobs, drive call volume, and the research holds no count of deaths in the county, no Wyoming cremation rate and no price list from any funeral home. The study says so instead of borrowing a national figure, and it makes the missing evidence a lender condition.

The supply read is just as thin. No document in the research for this case names a funeral home, cemetery or crematory in Cheyenne, so the competitor census is a lender condition and the competitors section carries the Census Bureau's own count: at least three establishments in the deathcare class in the county, and the same at least three in the metro area, which is the county [^12]. The count is a floor, because the Bureau withholds some small cells.

A funeral home sells to the families of the people who die in its trade area, so the best demand evidence is a count of deaths by place of residence, the age of the residents and the share who choose cremation. The study looked for all three. The research found none of them for Laramie County, so this section reads the people and the economy around the question instead, and the model's call volume is a labelled assumption. People and age

The county's resident count of 102,938 on July 1, 2025 [^1] is up from 101,687 residents a year earlier [^1], and it is slightly above the 5-year survey estimate in the register table, which averages 2020 to 2024. Growth has picked up. The Census Bureau's own 2020 to 2023 window shows only a small gain in the register table, and then the 2025 estimate year added 1.2% [^2]. Net migration of 1,120 people in that year [^2] accounts for much of the gain, and migrants skew younger than the residents who die. That matters for a funeral home in a plain way: a growing population raises the number of future families the firm can serve, but it does not raise this year's deaths.

The age evidence is the register's count of residents 65 and over. The 5-year survey puts them at the figure in the table and the 1-year survey for 2024 puts them higher; both are sample estimates with margins of error, and the two are not interchangeable, so the study reads the older cohort as large and still growing rather than as a point value. A funeral home's volume follows that cohort with a lag of years. The study does not turn it into a death rate, because the research holds none.

Jobs, wages and household strength

Families pay for a funeral out of savings, insurance and the estate, and the local economy sets what they can afford. The county's median household income was $82,615 in 2024 [^3] and the register's survey figure is in the table above. The Cheyenne metro area is government-heavy: government employers account for 13.7 thousand of its 49.1 thousand nonfarm jobs, and that sector shrank 5.5% over twelve months while total nonfarm jobs fell 1.4% [^4]. Unemployment is low, at 3.0% in the metro area in September 2026, after 2.6% in July and 2.9% in August [^4] and 2.9% in the county in August 2026 [^16], and the county added jobs in each of the two latest quarterly reports: 834, or 1.8%, in the year to the first quarter of 2025 [^9], and 635, or 1.3%, in the year to the fourth quarter of 2024 [^10]. County unemployment was 2.8% in July 2025, down from 3.4% a year earlier [^11], and covered payroll rose $40.2 million, or 5.6%, in the year to the first quarter of 2025 [^9] and $36.0 million, or 4.9%, in the year to the fourth quarter of 2024 [^10]. Construction added more than 600 of the jobs in the first of those years [^9]. A stable public payroll gives a funeral home dependable customers with life insurance and pensions, while a shrinking one is a reason to test the firm's accounts receivable and preneed book rather than assume them.

The metro area has 5,170 jobs in transportation and material moving occupations, 10.7% of local employment, with an annual mean wage of $52,940, and these are the drivers a funeral home competes with for removal and transfer staff [^5]. The mean hourly wage across all Cheyenne occupations was $30.33 in May 2025 against a national mean of $33.54 [^5]. Management occupations make up 6.3% of local employment, healthcare practitioners 6.3% and food preparation and serving 8.6% [^5]. Spending is softer. Laramie County's sales and use tax collections fell 1.2% in fiscal year 2025 [^7], and a firm whose revenue per call depends on merchandise choices should expect families to be careful. The average funeral does not follow retail sales, but it does follow what families feel they can spend on the visible parts of a service.

Where the metro jobs are

By sector, the September 2026 release counts 10.2 thousand jobs in trade, transportation and utilities, unchanged over twelve months, 4.7 thousand in mining, logging and construction, up 2.2%, 2.2 thousand in financial activities, up 4.8%, and 5.1 thousand in leisure and hospitality, down 3.8% [^4]. The financial and construction gains matter to a funeral home because they point to households with life insurance and mortgages, but the base is small, and the loss in hospitality and government is larger in count than the gains.

Housing and the cost of staying

The median home listing price in Laramie County was $473,975 in September 2026, with 377 listings and a median of 43 days on market [^6]. The register's survey value of owner-occupied homes, from 2020 to 2024, is lower, as the table shows. High home values mean that a long-time resident's estate is often a house, and the estate settles slowly when the house must be sold. That affects collections for a funeral home that extends credit until the estate clears, which the model carries as bad debt in its overhead. The register's counts of households and owner-occupied homes, in the table above, show the size of the pool of homes behind those estates.

Visitors and the county's other buyers

Laramie County recorded 2.8 million overnight visitors who spent $485 million in 2025 [^8], and Cheyenne Frontier Days alone sold 263,640 tickets in 2024, drew about 550,000 attendees and brought $50.4 million of visitor spending, up from $40.3 million in 2021, of which $11 million went to overnight accommodations [^15]. The event supported approximately 660 jobs and $17.6 million of earnings, returned $1.7 million to the state in tax revenue, and 67% of attendees stayed overnight [^15]. Visitors are not the funeral home's customers, but they shape the business in two ways. They raise lodging prices in the week families from out of town need rooms for a service, and they fill the labor market for hospitality workers, whom a funeral home must compete with for attendants and drivers.

The data center build-out and the workforce

The largest local change is industrial. Cheyenne LEADS counted 10 locations with operating data centers, 5 projects under construction and 9 more planned or in due diligence in Cheyenne and Laramie County in June 2026 [^14], and a county workforce housing requirement now follows that boom: the data centers employ about 2,500 construction workers and are expected to keep 250 employees once operating [^13]. Construction workers bring younger households and a high demand for rentals, not a surge in deaths, and many leave when the work ends. For a funeral home the boom matters mostly through wages and labor supply, because the same workers are the pool from which attendants and drivers are hired.

What the research could not find

The county's death count, the share of families choosing cremation, any funeral home's price list or case volume, the age distribution below the 65 and over line, hospice and hospital referral patterns, and the county's cemetery capacity are all absent. Each becomes a lender condition below, and the call volume and price inputs in the model are labelled assumptions that the seller's own records must replace.

The standard of this page is a competitor census with one row per funeral home, each row linked to a dated public document that names the firm, or to the firm's own website or the county assessor record. The research for this case holds no news article, broker report or agency list that names a funeral home, cemetery or crematory in Cheyenne or Laramie County, and the repository's rules forbid naming a business no research row names. So the census is a lender condition: a named competitor census with each competitor's own website or assessor record, before commitment.

What the study can state is how many establishments exist. The table gives the Census Bureau's County Business Patterns counts for the register's funeral home class, which the register grades as an exact fit and which combines funeral homes with cemeteries and crematories. The counts are lower bounds: the Bureau withholds some detailed cells in small geographies, so the true number can be higher, and each count is read as "at least". Each row carries the register's source line with its URL, and the figures date from the week of March 12, 2023, so they trail the market by years. The Cheyenne metro area is the county, so the two geographies return the same counts.

What the count tells a buyer

The class holds at least three establishments and at least 26 paid employees in the county [^12]. A county of this size cannot support many full-service firms, and the Bureau's count already includes cemeteries and crematories, so the number of competing funeral homes is probably smaller than the number of establishments. The rate per ten thousand residents is a small fraction of one in the table, which reads as a thin field. A thin field is not the same as an open one: funeral homes sell to families who often choose a firm their relatives used, and the firms that hold those relationships can be hard to move.

What the census must still answer

The condition asks for each firm's address, its ownership and whether a consolidator holds it, whether it runs a crematory, its published General Price List, and the cemeteries and hospitals it works with. It also asks which firm the seller's families go to when the seller is full. None of that is in the research. The first-year call volume in the model is therefore the number the census can most easily overturn, and the sensitivity table later on shows how much the loan depends on it.

The subject is a hypothetical site: a single-story chapel with arrangement rooms and a prep room on about one acre in Cheyenne, within reach of the I-25 and I-80 corridor. The acreage is a round assumption. The parcel, its zoning district, utility territory and flood status are conditions to confirm; the research could not retrieve a parcel record, a zoning map, a traffic count or a utility tariff, because the city and state agency hosts refused the connection, and the traffic counts for the two interstates at Cheyenne were not found. The study says so rather than guess.

Zoning, parking and neighbors

A funeral home is a use that many cities permit only in some commercial or office districts, or by conditional approval, and it draws neighbor attention when it adds a crematory or extends its hours. The lender needs a written zoning determination that covers the funeral use, the chapel's seating and the parking the city requires for a service, because a funeral can bring dozens of cars at once. It also needs a statement of any conditional use permit and whether it transfers to a new owner. A building that is legal today under the seller's permit may not be legal for a buyer who changes the operation, and the transfer is a closing item.

Utilities and the building

The electric, gas, water and sewer providers for the parcel, the commercial capacity and any discharge rule for a prep room, and the age and condition of the roof, heating plant and cooler are lender conditions. The model carries utilities, repairs and grounds at {{case:v.in_utilities}} in the first year and grows them with costs. A property on a high plain with winter heating and snow removal has real fixed costs, and a mechanical failure in the cooler or the hearse bay can close the preparation room until it is fixed.

Land and building value

The research records no Cheyenne commercial sale or listing, so the study neither prints nor cites one. The model allocates {{case:v.in_re_alloc}} of the price to land and building. The county's median home listing price of $473,975 [^6] gives a sense of the local market for houses, but a chapel is a larger and more specialized building, and the appraisal and an executed contract replace the placeholder. Because the real estate share sets how much of the loan amortizes over 25 years instead of 10, the allocation affects debt service directly.

Property tax

The research holds no Laramie County or Cheyenne mill levy or assessment ratio. The model carries a planning rate of {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of the real estate allocation, and the actual levy is a lender condition, along with any exemption that applies to the buyer.

Weather and access

The research holds no storm or disaster declaration for the county that it can date, and no flood map. Hail, high wind and snow all affect a building with a large roof, a covered porch for hearse loading and a long driveway. Insurance quotes and the roof's age are early items, not afterthoughts.

Volume

The model holds the call count nearly flat, at {{case:v.in_calls}} in Year 3, a few calls a year above Year 1. The count is a placeholder for a mid-sized independent firm. A funeral home does not choose its volume: the county's deaths set the pool, the relationships the firm holds set its share, and the buyer inherits the share only if the families who used the seller are comfortable with a new name on the door. The ramp is therefore small, well below the pace at which the county's population grew [^2]. The sensitivity table tests how far the loan can bend if the volume does not hold.

Price and mix

Revenue per call is {{case:v.in_rev_per_call}} in Year 1, a blend across burial, service with cremation and direct cremation, merchandise included, growing {{case:v.in_price_growth}} a year. The research holds no price list, so the figure is a placeholder, and the firm's own price list, with its sales by service type, is what replaces it. The mix matters more than the average. A firm whose families move steadily from burial to cremation earns less per call even when its list prices rise, and the study cannot see the county's cremation rate. That is the largest unknown on the revenue line.

Other income of {{case:v.in_other_rev}} in Year 1 covers preneed commissions, florals, chapel rentals and cemetery lot commissions. Revenue grows from {{case:v.rev_y1}} in Year 1 to {{case:v.rev_y3}} in Year 3 and {{case:v.rev_y5}} in Year 5.

{{case:loan}}

Buying an operating business is a change of ownership under the rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) [^17]. The equity injection for an initial acquisition is 10% of total project cost, and it cannot be reduced or eliminated [^17]. Part of the injection may come from sources other than the buyer's cash, but with a limit: half is the cap on the share that seller debt on full standby, other full-standby debt and non-controlling minority investors may supply together [^17]. The model assumes the whole injection is the buyer's cash.

The loan amortizes in two layers. The business portion runs for 10 years and the real estate portion for up to 25 years [^17]. Because real estate is the smaller part of project cost here, most of the debt runs on the shorter schedule, and that is why a goodwill-heavy purchase carries heavy debt service for its price. The engine prices the loan at Prime of 7.00% [^19] plus the maximum spread the rules allow for a loan this size, and a lender may price lower.

Two valuation rules shape the diligence. A lender may value the business itself only when the business purchase price is at or below $350,000 [^17], and the business portion here is above that, so an independent valuation is a lender condition. The rules also set a quality of earnings threshold of $3 million for the business purchase price [^17], which the business portion here does not reach, so the valuation and the lender's own review of the seller's returns carry the evidence. Seller earnouts are not allowed: seller earnouts prohibited; buyer rebates must pay down the 7(a) loan; purchase-agreement working-capital true-ups are not rebates [^17]. The fee schedule for fiscal year 2027 is in the SBA notice [^18], and the guaranty share the model uses is in the program terms [^20].

Whether a feasibility study is needed is decided case by case: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^21].

{{case:inputs}}

The risks below follow from the numbers above.

  1. The families do not follow the sale

    The model's volume assumes the seller's families stay. A funeral home's value is its name, and a change of ownership puts that to a test the research cannot measure. A drop of a few dozen calls moves the coverage from adequate to thin, as the break-even table shows, and the county's thin field of at least three establishments [^12] cuts both ways: few rivals, but loyal families.

  2. Cremation compresses the check

    The research holds no Wyoming cremation rate, so the mix is unknown. A steady move from burial to cremation lowers revenue per call, and the sensitivity table shows how a fall of a tenth in the average check erodes the margin of safety.

  3. The public payroll shrinks

    Government jobs in the metro area fell 5.5% over twelve months to September 2026 and total nonfarm jobs fell 1.4% [^4]. A shrinking public base changes who dies where and who pays. The county added jobs through early 2025 [^9], so the signal is mixed, but a buyer should watch it.

  4. Labor is competitive

    The metro unemployment rate was 3.0% in September 2026 [^4], the data center build-out employs about 2,500 construction workers [^13], and visitor spending of $485 million supports 3,700 jobs elsewhere in the trade [^8] that compete for the same attendants and drivers. Licensed directors are scarcer still, and the wage inputs may prove low.

  5. Goodwill is not collateral

    Most of the price is intangible. If the business falters, the lender's recovery rests on a chapel building valued at {{case:v.in_re_alloc}} in the model against total debt of {{case:v.loan_total}}. The equity share in the restructured case is a response to that.

  6. A rival or a consolidator enters

    The Census count is a floor [^12] and the research names no rival. A national consolidator buying the largest local firm, or a low-price cremation provider opening, would change the field without any warning in the public record.

  7. Rates and the loan market

    The rate is Prime of 7.00% [^19] plus the maximum spread the rules allow, held for the term. A higher Prime at closing lowers coverage, and the restructured case has little spare cushion.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open.

  1. Competitor census

    A named competitor census with each competitor's own website or county assessor record, covering funeral homes, cemeteries and crematories in Laramie County, with each firm's ownership, price list, crematory, and referral relationships. The Census count is a floor of at least three establishments [^12], and no research document names the field.

  2. Deaths, age and cremation

    Deaths by place of residence for Laramie County for at least three recent years, the age distribution of residents, the state and county cremation rate and the share of deaths that are hospice or hospital deaths. The research holds none of them.

  3. The seller's records

    Three years of tax returns, monthly sales reports, a call log by service type, the General Price List, the preneed contract register with the trust or insurance carrier for each contract, and interim statements through the latest month. These replace the illustrative FY2024 and FY2025 results on this page.

  4. Valuation, appraisal and environmental

    An independent business valuation, a going-concern appraisal of the building and land, a Phase I environmental assessment that covers embalming chemicals and fuel storage, and a survey.

  5. Site, title and zoning

    The parcel identifier from the county assessor, a written zoning determination that covers the funeral use and parking, any conditional use permit and its transfer, and the sale history of the land.

  6. Utilities, taxes and building condition
