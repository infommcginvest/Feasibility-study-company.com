{{case:glance}}

The demand answer is yes, with limits. Vermont's visitor economy supported 31,780 direct jobs in 2024, about 10% of the state's workforce, according to a Tourism Economics study relayed by a trade magazine [^12]. In the winter the town is part of that trade through its ski area, which the owner of the Stowe, Okemo and Mount Snow mountains targeted to open on November 21, 2026 [^14]. A retreat that sells treatments, meals and a program does not need to be a ski lodge, but it draws on the same visitor stream and the same roads.

The supply answer is that the town is full of beds. Town data counted about 1,450 short-term rentals in Stowe in July 2026, and about 600 of them were commercial or inactive [^1]. A retreat is not a rental, because it sells a program and not a bed, but it competes for the same weekend guest. The town's selectboard adopted a target cap of 850 short-term rentals by a vote of 5 to 0 [^1], and a January 2026 article reported that resorts were lobbying for carveouts from such a cap [^2]. The direction of local policy is to hold casual lodging down. The research does not say how the cap treats a commercial retreat, and the resorts' lobbying suggests the answer matters.

The competitive answer is partial. The research names two Stowe resorts, one in a news report and one in a state lender's release. It does not say whether either runs treatment rooms or programs, and it holds no wellness operator's rate, menu or booking calendar. The competitors section sets out what the record supports and turns the rest into a lender condition.

The land answer is that the research holds no Stowe land sale. It does show the price level of housing around it: a statewide median sale price of about $353,000 for primary homes in 2024 [^15], and a median listing price of $625,000 in September 2026 in Windsor County, which holds another resort town [^7]. Neither is a land price. The model carries land at a round planning price, and an appraisal will move it.

The visitor stream

A study by Tourism Economics counted 31,780 direct jobs in Vermont's visitor economy in 2024, about a tenth of the state's workforce [^12]. That is a statewide figure, and the research did not reach a Stowe or Lamoille County visitor count, a lodging tax receipt or a hotel occupancy series that can be printed with a date. What it does give is the winter shape of the trade. Ski Vermont counted 339,046 cross country skier visits at its member areas in the 2025 to 2026 season [^13], and the next season's announced target opening is November 21, 2026 [^14]. Those counts show that the town is a place people already travel to. They are not a measure of retreat demand, and the research gives no profile of the Stowe guest: no age, income, home state or length of stay. The drive in is not free, with regular gasoline averaging $4.34 a gallon in Vermont on October 26, 2026 [^23].

The local economy and the labor pool

Lamoille County had 12,865 covered jobs in March 2026 and an average weekly wage of $1,170 in the first quarter, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics [^3]. Windsor County, which holds Ludlow and its ski area, had 22,478 jobs and a weekly wage of $1,265 in the same release [^3]. Both counties pay about twelve hundred dollars a week, and both share a staffing problem that a retreat will feel before it sees a competitor. Treatment staff, cooks and housekeepers are drawn from the same pool as the ski areas, the inns and the medical offices. The model therefore carries payroll above the county average and charges a market salary for management even where an owner fills the role.

The state's labor force participation rate was 62.1% in September 2026 [^18], and its resident population was 644,663 on July 1, 2025 [^8]. A small labor pool means that wages and housing, not demand, may set the ceiling on how many rooms a retreat can staff. The household side looks healthier: personal income tax led a September 2026 surge in state revenue, with the General Fund collecting $276.5 million against a consensus target of $251.7 million, and the three main funds together $372.6 million against $346.8 million [^21]. The previous fiscal year ended with the General Fund $88.2 million above its consensus target of $2,362.3 million [^22]. Strong income tax receipts suggest households with money to spend, though the figure is statewide and says nothing about Stowe's guests.

Housing and the cost of land

Lamoille County authorized 155 new private housing structures in 2025 [^11], against 119 in Windsor County [^9], and Vermont as a whole authorized 152 housing units in September 2026 [^19]. Windsor County had 358 active listings and a median listing price of $625,000 in September 2026 [^20,7], while the Federal Housing Finance Agency's all-transactions index for Vermont stood at 902.53 in the second quarter of 2026 [^16]. In Lamoille County the register shows a median home value of $304,700 and a median gross rent of $1,160 [^4]. Those are owner-reported figures for 2020 to 2024, and they sit below the statewide median sale price of primary homes, about $353,000 in 2024 [^15]. The measures differ and none describes a resort-town acre. A buyer of land for a retreat competes with second-home buyers, and the same scarcity makes it hard for staff to live nearby. The model does not price staff housing, and the lender should ask for the plan.

The register describes a county of 26,148 residents with 11,923 households, of which 3,236 rent their homes, a median household income of $74,908, and personal income per resident of $71,627 in 2024 [^4,6]. The household figure is what the middle household earns, and the per-person figure includes investment income, rent and transfers. Neither profiles the retreat's day clients or overnight guests, and a client survey has to supply that profile.

What the named resorts tell us

The two rows do not measure competition. They show that Stowe has resort lodging that is investing, building condominium units and borrowing from the state's development lender, and that resorts lobbied over how the town's cap on short-term rentals treats them [^2]. A retreat entering that field would be a smaller property that has to win on program and privacy, and the model gives it a luxury-positioned room rate to say so. Whether either resort sells treatments, meals to non-guests or retreat packages is not in the record, and a lender should not infer that a resort with a spa competes for the same guest or that a resort without one does not. The census must visit each property's own website and record its treatment menu, price list, minimum stay and seasonal closures.

What the census must still show

A named census adds, for each rival, the rooms, the nightly rate with and without a program, the treatment menu and price list, the minimum stay, the seasonal closures and the year opened. The model needs two numbers from it above all: the room rate a program-led guest pays in Stowe, and the share of weekend and midweek nights that rivals fill. Neither is in the public record this study could read, so the model carries both as assumptions.

Zoning, use and the short-term rental cap

A wellness retreat is a mixed use: lodging, a personal-service treatment business, a program space and a restaurant for guests. A zoning code may treat those uses separately, so the lender should ask the town for a written determination of how the use is classified. The January 2026 article on resort lobbying refers to resort and ski lodging zoning areas in the town's registry [^2], and the July 2026 article reports a cap with a limit of two licenses per homestead owner [^1]. The research does not say whether the cap reaches a commercial retreat, whether a retreat's rooms count as rentals, or whether the carveouts the resorts sought were granted. That reading belongs to the town's counsel, and it is the first item in the site condition.

Water, wastewater and heat

A fourteen-room lodge with a kitchen, a laundry and a thermal area discharges and heats more than a house. The model assumes an on-site wastewater system and a drilled well and carries them in an allowance. If the parcel is inside a municipal sewer or water district, the allowance falls and connection fees rise, and the research found neither the district nor the fee. The utilities line in the operating model is also an allowance, because no provider, rate or heating fuel was confirmed.

Flood and storm exposure

Lamoille County was among the six counties in the Individual Assistance declaration for the July 2023 flooding, and the state's one-year report counted $25,987,190.49 of Individual Assistance approved by FEMA and the Small Business Administration as of July 11, 2024 [^25]. The state's congressional delegation wrote in September 2025 that Vermont had 21 federally declared disasters since 2013 [^10]. The governor's announcement of the final recovery award on October 15, 2026 put the state's CDBG-DR funding for the 2023 flooding at $67,845,000 across 28 projects [^26]. None of these figures locates a flood zone on a Route 100 parcel. A flood determination, a FEMA panel number and an insurance quote are lender conditions.

Traffic, access and land

A retreat is a destination and does not need a high-count road, but it does need a legal driveway, a safe turn off the highway and winter access for guests and deliveries. The research lists the state's traffic counts for Route 100 as not found. It also holds no Stowe land sale, assessed valuation or tax rate, so the model carries land at {{case:v.in_land_per_acre}} an acre and property tax at {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of the cost basis, both labelled assumptions that an appraisal and the town's rate will move.

{{case:revenue}}

The model sells five things. The room line is occupied nights times a room rate. The program line is what an in-house guest spends on treatments, classes and guided sessions during a stay. The meals line is the dining room. The session line is treatments sold to people who do not stay, and retail is a small share of the other four. The units are the {{case:v.in_rooms}} rooms, the six treatment rooms and the program studio.

{{case:loan}}

An SBA 504 project is split three ways. The rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^29]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the project is both [^29]. This subject is both. It is a new business, and the SOP's example list of special purpose properties in the 504 chapter includes lodging facilities [^29]. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^29], and whether a retreat with treatment rooms and a program studio falls under that definition is for the CDC to decide.

At the proposed contribution the debenture would be {{case:v.debenture_proposed}}, under the $5 million limit [^29], so the size test passes. The equity test fails: the sponsor offered {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} where the tier is {{case:v.equity_req_pct}}. The debenture carries an upfront fee of 0.50% for loans approved in the 2027 fiscal year [^30], which the model adds to project cost.

The owner-occupancy test shapes who holds the building. For a new building the test is 60% [^29]. The operating borrower should own and occupy the lodge, the treatment wing and the studio, and a retreat does that by definition, because the guests and clients are customers and not tenants.

The lodging rule

A lodging borrower faces an eligibility rule that has nothing to do with coverage: more than 50% of the prior year's revenue from transients who stay 30 days or less at a time; a start-up shows it in projections [^29]. A retreat whose programs run a weekend to a week passes easily, and the projection should show the length of stay in a line of its own. The test bites if the sponsor sells month-long residencies or leases rooms to staff, and a lender will ask for the booking mix.

A shorter amortization on a special purpose property would raise debt service and the equity needed to meet Year 2 coverage.

Whether a feasibility study is needed is decided case by case: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^32]. On a ground-up lodging and treatment project with a start-up borrower, a lender may ask for one. {{case:inputs}}

Almost every input is an assumption. The research for this town holds market evidence, county wages and short-term rental counts but no unit economics for a retreat: no rates, no occupancy, no treatment prices, no construction costs and no wage for a practitioner. Where an assumption leans on the research, its reasoning cites the county wage release or the Federal Reserve's prime rate, and the SBA rules come from the program data and not from the inputs. The assumptions carry the model, and the most important to verify are the room rate, the occupancy ramp, the program spend per guest, the cost of construction and the wages of treatment staff, in that order.

  1. The ramp is slower than modelled

    The Year 2 test leaves no room. A retreat that fills its calendar a season late fails the floor, and the research holds no booking or occupancy series to show how fast a new program-led property fills in Stowe.

  2. The rate is lower than assumed

    No Stowe retreat or resort rate is in the research. A rate a step below the planning figure takes Year 2 under the floor, and a resort that discounts midweek sets the ceiling on what a smaller property can charge.

  3. The short-term rental cap reaches the retreat

    Stowe's selectboard set a target cap of 850 short-term rentals against about 1,450 counted in July 2026 [^1], and resorts lobbied for carveouts [^2]. If a commercial retreat's rooms are counted against the cap, or if carveouts favor existing resorts, the lender needs a written ruling from the town before commitment.

  4. Flood and storm damage

    Vermont has had 21 federally declared disasters since 2013 [^10], and the July 2023 flooding reached Lamoille County [^25]. A retreat in a flood zone faces higher insurance and an interrupted season, and the model's insurance allowance is a round number.

  5. Staff cannot be found or housed

    Lamoille County's covered employment was 12,865 in March 2026 [^3], and the county authorized only 155 new housing structures in 2025 [^11]. A retreat needs practitioners and cooks who can live within reach, and the model prices neither housing nor a recruiting premium.

  6. The costs exceed the budget

    Hard cost, site work and equipment are all allowances. A contractor's bid above the model raises project cost and the equity needed to hold the floor, and a cold-climate building season adds delay risk to a project that carries pre-opening cost.

  7. The CDC treats the building differently

    The model treats a lodging retreat as special purpose, which sets the contribution at the top tier. If the CDC reads the property differently, the tier changes, and so does the restructured equity. A lender should have the CDC's view in writing.

  8. Stays drift longer than 30 days

    The lodging rule asks for most revenue from transient stays. A retreat that sells long residencies, or houses staff in guest rooms, can move toward the line, and its projection should show the length of stay.

  9. Demand is seasonal

    Vermont's visitor economy supported 31,780 direct jobs in 2024 [^12], but the research holds no Stowe lodging series by month. A retreat carries a year of payroll against a calendar that thins in the shoulder months.
