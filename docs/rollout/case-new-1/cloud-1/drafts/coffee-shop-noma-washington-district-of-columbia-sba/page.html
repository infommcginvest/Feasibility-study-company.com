Feasibility Study Company prepares studies like this one for owners, developers and their lenders. As a coffee shop feasibility study company and coffee shop feasibility study consultant, we build a dated market read, a competitor census, a priced build-out and a coverage test that says how much debt a cafe can carry. This page runs that work on one hypothetical project: a new owner-operated coffee shop and cafe leasing ground-floor space in NoMa and the Union Market area of Northeast Washington, financed with an SBA 7(a) loan. Ask us to run the same coffee shop feasibility study on your site.

The subject is generic: one shop of about {{case:v.in_space_sf}} sq ft in a mixed-use building, with a coffee bar, a pastry case and a small seating area, and no hood or full kitchen. It is a leasehold, so the sponsor buys no land and no building; the loan funds a build-out, equipment and the opening reserve. Every market figure is public and dated. Every model figure is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed near the end, each of which carries either a source or the word assumption. The study belongs to our coffee shop practice, the neighborhood sits in the District of Columbia, and the program rules are set out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

{{case:glance}}

The short answer is that the neighborhood can feed a coffee shop, but not on a fast ramp and not with a thin cushion. NoMa had 12,299 apartment and condominium units at the end of 2025, against 5,922 in the summer of 2021, so its residential stock has more than doubled in about four years [^1]. It also held 12.68 million sq ft of office space, 1,356 hotel rooms and 701,000 sq ft of retail [^1]. The people those buildings hold are the shop's customers: residents on their way out in the morning, office workers at lunch and in the afternoon, hotel guests and visitors on weekends.

The same source shows why a sponsor should not underwrite the stock as if it were demand. Office vacancy in NoMa was 15.7% in the second half of 2025, up from 14.8% a year earlier, and residential vacancy was 10.3% [^1]. Retail vacancy stood just under 29% for 2025, about ten points lower than in 2024 [^1], which is an improvement from a very high base. Only 12 new businesses opened in the neighborhood in 2025 [^1]. A tenant in this market has bargaining power on rent and a landlord's incentive to see the unit occupied, and a lender should read the vacancy as a warning about foot traffic as much as an opportunity.

The wider backdrop is a District economy that lost jobs through 2025 and 2026. The Office of Revenue Analysis counted 739,200 jobs in the District in November 2025, the lowest since the year after the pandemic lows, and 29,500 fewer than in January [^4]. The District's resident unemployment rate was 5.6% in September 2026 (preliminary) [^6]. Downtown office attendance, the nearest published gauge of commuter traffic, averaged 48% of the pre-pandemic level from March to December 2025 [^2]. NoMa is not the Downtown business improvement district, and no NoMa attendance count was found, so the Downtown figure is a comparison, not a measurement of the subject's trade area.

Weighing the two sides, the model underwrites a shop that opens at about two thirds of its stabilized daily traffic, reaches it in the third year, and is financed with more equity than the program's minimum. The sponsor's request for the minimum fails the coverage test in Year 2, and the restructured loan reaches the floor only with {{case:v.equity_pct}} equity. That is a conditional finding, not a verdict on the neighborhood: it says what the assumed income can carry, and it names the inputs, rent, build-out cost and daily traffic, that a lender must replace with evidence before commitment.

A coffee shop sells a habit, and the habit comes from three groups with very different reliability. The first is residents. NoMa's 177 multifamily units expected for 2026 are small beside the 12,299 already built [^1], but the neighborhood is not finished: the Union Market area could add up to 3 million sq ft over the next five years, and the same page describes Union Market as about 2 million sq ft of mixed-use space with more than 1,900 homes and about 220,000 sq ft of office [^1]. Across the District, the Deputy Mayor for Planning and Economic Development counts more than 73,000 housing units added since 2015 [^7]. Residents buy coffee at the weekend and in the morning, which suits a shop that opens early, and they are the one group whose numbers have grown without interruption.

The second group is the daytime worker, and it is the group in doubt. The District held 718,700 jobs in September 2026 [^6], and its federal government employment stood at 169,000 in November 2025, down from 192,800 in January of that year, with 15,300 of the loss falling in a single month, September to October, when the deferred resignation program took effect [^4]. The Downtown business improvement district reports federal jobs as 23% of the city's total at the end of 2025 [^2], a share the report relays from federal labor statistics. The metro area lost 100,500 jobs between May 2025 and May 2026, of which 54,500 were federal [^8]. Those figures describe lost commuters, and they explain why the model does not give the shop a full office-day traffic count in Year 1.

Office space adds a second warning. Cushman & Wakefield put citywide office vacancy at 23.5% in the third quarter of 2026, with net absorption of negative 123,800 sq ft in the quarter [^9], while CBRE reported positive absorption of 360,154 sq ft for the same quarter [^10]. The two brokers measure the market differently, and this page does not choose between them: the direction of the quarter is disputed, but the level of vacancy is high on Cushman & Wakefield's reading, and NoMa's own office vacancy of 15.7% is lower than that citywide level [^1,9]. A shop in NoMa is therefore better placed than a Downtown one, but its worker base is still tied to a metro economy that lost federal jobs.

The third group is the visitor. Destination DC counted 27.2 million visitors to the District in 2025, spending $11.9 billion [^11], and NoMa had 1,356 hotel rooms at the end of that year [^1]. Downtown hotel occupancy was 68% in 2025, down from 73% [^2]. No count of visitors to NoMa or Union Market was found, and the model gives tourist traffic no separate weight: it sits inside the catering line and the weekend share of counter sales. The hotel rooms in the neighborhood are an upside the lender does not need.

A competitor census normally lists named shops, each tied to its own website or its assessor record. This study cannot build that list. None of the public documents in the research names a coffee shop or cafe in NoMa, Union Market or anywhere else in the District, and nothing may be fetched to find one. The census is therefore a lender condition: a named competitor census with each competitor's own website or assessor record. No business is named below, and the subject is not presented as a competitor to anyone.

What the counts say

The District has nearly twice as many snack and beverage bar establishments for every ten thousand residents as the metro area as a whole, a density that follows the daytime population: workers and visitors buy coffee in the District and sleep in the suburbs, so a resident count understates the demand the shops serve. For the subject, density cuts both ways. It proves that the District supports a large number of cafes, and it says that a new shop must win a customer from an incumbent within a short walk, not open a market. The class also includes establishments that are not coffee shops, so the true number of coffee competitors in NoMa is smaller than the District count and unknown.

The restaurant counts show where the competition widens. Quick-service outlets outnumber snack and beverage bars in the District several times over, and a breakfast sandwich from one competes with a cafe's pastry case. A coffee shop here sells a morning habit against both categories.

The SBA row is a different kind of evidence. It shows that 7(a) lenders approved a small number of loans to borrowers in this industry code in the District over ten fiscal years: the program has financed shops of this kind here, and the sample is too small to draw a default rate from. The register flags the class's loss rate as resting on a small denominator, so this page prints none.

What the census must show

A named census should list every coffee shop and cafe within a short walk of the unit and near the main commuter and residential approaches, with each competitor's own website or assessor record, its hours, seating, a dated menu with prices, and whether it is a national chain, a regional roaster or an independent. It should include cafes inside office lobbies and hotels, which draw the same customer and do not always show in an online search. The sponsor should visit at the hours the model assumes are busy, and record the queue.

The subject is a hypothetical ground-floor unit of about {{case:v.in_space_sf}} sq ft in a mixed-use building in NoMa or the adjoining Union Market area. It has no street address in this study, no named landlord and no listing behind it. The neighborhood's retail stock was 701,000 sq ft at the end of 2025, up from 425,000 sq ft in the summer of 2021, so a tenant can choose among many storefronts, and just under 29% of that retail space stood vacant [^1]. The choice that matters is the corner: a shop placed on the path between a Metro entrance and the office buildings, or at the foot of a large residential building, earns its traffic; a shop on a quiet block waits for it.

No traffic count was found for the neighborhood. The research looked for annual average daily traffic on the area's main streets and for Metro station entries at NoMa and found neither, so a pedestrian and vehicle count taken at the proposed corner during the model's peak hours is a lender condition, and so is the station ridership. The pipeline the research did find is large but uneven: 177 multifamily units expected in 2026 [^1], a market-rate redevelopment of the Sursum Corda site that the neighborhood plan puts at 900 market-rate units beside more affordable ones [^1], and up to 3 million sq ft in the Union Market area over five years [^1]. These are plans, and the page counts none of them as open doors.

The lease is the real estate in this deal, and it sets the loan more than the location does. The model needs a term long enough to recover the build-out, renewal options, a rent schedule, the landlord's tenant improvement allowance (assumed to be none here), a defined delivery condition for the shell, and an exclusive-use clause that bars a second coffee seller in the same building. No NoMa retail rent was found, so the base rent in the model is a round placeholder, and the research prints no office or retail operating costs for the neighborhood either. Citywide full-service asking rent for office space was $55.15 per sq ft in the third quarter of 2026 [^9], which is an office figure and is not used for the shop's rent. The lender will also ask that the lease term, with options, run at least as long as the loan, and that the landlord sign a waiver or consent that lets the lender step in.

Zoning and licensing are lender conditions because the research found none of the rules. A coffee and pastry shop without a hood is a lighter use than a restaurant with a kitchen, but the unit's certificate of occupancy, the District's food establishment license and health inspection, signage approval, and any condition in the building's own covenants need a written answer before the build-out is priced. The general District sales tax rate was 6% in the February 2026 revenue estimate, with a planned increase to 7% postponed through September 30, 2027 [^17]. The rate on prepared food and drink was not in the research, and the model's revenue is stated before sales tax, so the lender should confirm how the shop's register collects and remits it.

{{case:revenue}}

Revenue has two lines. Counter sales are the number of transactions per open day, times the average ticket, times the open days in the year, with the ticket rising by {{case:v.in_price_growth}} a year. Catering, events and packaged beans add {{case:v.in_catering_pct}} of counter sales. There is no delivery platform line: delivery orders are inside the counter count, and the commissions they carry sit in the card and software line of the cost base.

How many customers. The model reaches {{case:v.in_tx}} transactions per open day in Year 3, after lower counts in Years 1 and 2. No transaction count for a NoMa or Union Market shop was found, so the ramp is a judgment shaped by what the research does show: a neighborhood whose homes have doubled since 2021 [^1], a daytime worker base that is only partly present, with Downtown offices at 48% of their pre-pandemic attendance [^2], and a metro economy that lost jobs through 2026 [^8]. Year 1 sits at about two thirds of the stabilized count so that the model can absorb a slow first season, and the stabilized count stays flat from Year 4 so that the lender sees no growth the market has not shown.

What each customer pays. The average ticket of {{case:v.in_ticket}} blends a drink with a pastry some of the time. It is not a posted price: shop menus carry no publication date in the research, so none is printed. The ticket grows at {{case:v.in_price_growth}} a year, below the 4.5% pay rise reported for the District over the year to the first quarter of 2026 [^12], which means the model does not let price fully offset wage growth. A sponsor who intends to charge more has to show it with a comparable shop's sales receipts, and the lender should ask for them.

Where the revenue comes from. Counter sales in Year 3 are the product of the stabilized count, the ticket, the price steps and {{case:v.in_days}} open days. The shop is open every day but a few holidays, because weekend trade from residents is the part of demand that has grown with the housing stock. The catering line is small on purpose. The area's office space is large, at 12.68 million sq ft [^1], and an office order is the most valuable single sale a cafe makes, but with office attendance at 48% of its pre-pandemic level in the Downtown district for most of 2025 [^2], the model does not lean on it.

The District's all-industry average weekly wage of $2,725 in the first quarter of 2026 is context, not an input: it averages every industry, and this page makes no claim about what a counter job pays [^12].

Base rent is {{case:v.in_rent_psf}} per sq ft on {{case:v.in_space_sf}} sq ft, rising {{case:v.in_rent_esc}} a year, and taxes, insurance and common area charges are {{case:v.in_nnn_psf}} per sq ft. Other lines. Card fees, software and delivery commissions are {{case:v.in_card_pct}} of revenue; utilities, supplies, repairs and marketing are {{case:v.in_other_pct}}; insurance starts at {{case:v.in_insurance}} and licenses at {{case:v.in_licenses}}, both growing {{case:v.in_cost_growth}} a year. A leasehold tenant pays no property tax directly, so the real property tax rates the research found for the District, which carry no tax year, are not printed here, and the landlord's pass-through budget is the lender's item to verify.

Total project cost is {{case:v.tpc}} after the restructure, including the SBA guaranty fee the model adds to cost. The lines are all assumptions explained in the inputs table: leasehold improvements at {{case:v.in_buildout}}, equipment, furniture and point of sale at {{case:v.in_equipment}}, design, permits, legal and contingency at {{case:v.in_soft}}, and pre-opening costs and working capital at {{case:v.in_preopen}}. No supplier quote, contractor bid or city fee schedule stands behind any of them, and the first lender condition on this page is to replace the largest of them, the build-out, with a general contractor's bid for the actual unit.

The shape of the uses matters more than the total. A leasehold project has no land and no building to pledge. The improvements become the landlord's property at the end of the lease, and the equipment of a coffee bar recovers less than it cost when sold. A lender will therefore weigh the sponsor's other assets and personal guarantee heavily, and it will look at the lease more closely than at the appraisal. For the same reason the model classes the build-out as a non-real-estate use, and the loan takes the shorter blended maturity described in the next section rather than the long term a real estate purchase would earn.

The pre-opening line deserves attention. NoMa recorded only 12 new businesses in 2025 [^1], so a new shop cannot count on a wave of neighboring openings to lift its traffic, and the reserve has to carry the sponsor through a slow first season. The model's Year 1 coverage is far below the floor, as the pro forma shows, which means the reserve and the sponsor's own liquidity do the work the operations cannot. A sponsor who has less liquidity than the restructured equity should look at a smaller build-out, a landlord allowance or a cheaper unit before asking for more debt.

The rules are those of SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, effective October 1, 2026 [^3]. The guaranty fee for fiscal 2027 is charged on the guaranteed portion and added to project cost [^20]. {{case:inputs}}
  • Daytime traffic. Downtown offices were at 48% of their pre-pandemic attendance for March to December 2025, against 44% a year earlier [^2]. NoMa's own attendance was not found. If the buildings around the unit stay half empty on the days that matter, the morning queue the model assumes in Year 2 does not form.
  • Federal employment. District federal jobs fell from 192,800 in January 2025 to 169,000 in November 2025 [^4], and the metro area's federal count fell from 375,800 to 312,500 between January 2025 and May 2026 [^8]. A further round of cuts hits the customers of every cafe in the city at once.
  • Office market. Citywide office vacancy of 23.5% in the third quarter of 2026 [^9] and the brokers' disagreement over absorption [^10] mean that tenants may keep shedding space. NoMa's office vacancy rose from 14.8% to 15.7% in the second half of 2025 [^1].
  • Retail oversupply. Retail vacancy of just under 29% in NoMa [^1] means many storefronts compete for few tenants, which helps on rent and hurts a neighborhood's feeling of life.
  • Food and beverage churn. The Downtown district recorded 36 restaurant closings in 2025, the fewest since 2017, and a net gain of six openings [^2]. The same report says food and beverage fills more than half of Downtown retail space [^2].
  • Pipeline timing. The Union Market area could add up to 3 million sq ft over five years [^1], and the neighborhood expects 177 multifamily units in 2026 [^1]. New residents help only when the buildings open, and construction in the meantime can disrupt access.
  • Cost overruns. The build-out is the largest line and rests on no bid. A bid above the {{case:v.in_buildout}} placeholder would eat the reserve or call for more equity, and a late opening runs rent without sales.
  • Lease and collateral. The improvements revert to the landlord, and the equipment has a low resale value. If the shop fails, a lender's recovery depends on guarantees and the sponsor's other assets, not on the unit.
  • Sales tax and wages. The District's general sales tax rate was 6% in the February 2026 estimate and a planned increase to 7% was postponed to September 30, 2027 [^17], and average weekly pay rose 4.5% over the year to the first quarter of 2026 [^12]. A rate change or a faster pay rise lowers margin faster than the model's price steps recover it.
