Feasibility Study Company is a laundromat feasibility study company: we prepare these studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A study joins a dated market read, a count of the competitors, a priced build and a coverage test that shows how much debt a store can carry. This page runs that work on one hypothetical project, a new attended self-service laundromat in a leased bay, financed with an SBA 7(a) loan in Richmond, Virginia. If you are looking for a laundromat feasibility study consultant for a site of your own, ask us to run the same work.

The subject is generic. It is an in-line bay of about {{case:v.in_bay_sf}} square feet in a neighborhood center on a Southside corridor such as Hull Street Road, with {{case:v.in_washers}} washers, {{case:v.in_dryers}} dryers, card payment and a bench for drop-off wash-dry-fold service. The owner-operator is new to the trade, so the loan is a start-up loan and the projection, not a history, carries the test. The buyer does not purchase a building: the money goes into a build-out, machines and working capital. Every market figure on the page is public and dated, every model figure is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed near the end, and each input carries a source or the word assumption. The project belongs to our laundromat practice, the city sits in the Richmond market of Virginia, and the program rules are laid out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

{{case:glance}}

Demand is the easier half of the answer. More than half of the city's households rent, and the register table in the demand section shows the count of renter households next to the count of laundromat-class establishments the federal business census places in the city. The metro economy is large and steady rather than booming: the Richmond metro had 724.2 thousand nonfarm jobs in September 2026, below the 731.2 thousand of a year earlier, and an unemployment rate of 3.8%, up from 3.3% [^1]. The city's own rate was 4.1% [^4]. A laundromat does not need a boom. It needs households that cannot or will not buy and house their own machines, and a city with these renter numbers has them.

The competitive half is thinner. No document in the research names a Richmond laundromat, so this study names none, and it makes a named competitor census a lender condition. In its place the competitors section counts the establishments that County Business Patterns places in the class, for the city and for the metro. The count is small beside the city's renter base, but it counts only establishments with paid employees, so it is a floor for the number of stores a customer can walk to, not a census of them.

The weaker half is space. The Cushman & Wakefield retail report puts Richmond retail vacancy at 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026, down 10 basis points in the quarter and up 30 over the year [^13]. Net absorption topped 240,000 square feet in the first three quarters and the pipeline holds 379,000 square feet [^13]. A tight retail market means a laundromat bay with the plumbing, power and parking the use needs is hard to find, and the landlord has the leverage on rent and term. No retail asking rent is in the research, so the model carries a rent placeholder and the lease is the first lender condition.

On the loan, the sponsor's request is the program minimum of {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} equity on a {{case:v.tpc_proposed}} project, a {{case:v.loan_total_proposed}} loan that is well inside the program limit. It fails on coverage in the test year by a narrow margin: Year 2 debt service is covered {{case:v.dscr_y2_proposed}} against a floor of 1.15x. Restructured, the sponsor brings {{case:v.equity}}, {{case:v.equity_pct}} of cost, the loan falls to {{case:v.loan_total}} and Year 2 coverage reaches {{case:v.dscr_y2}}. Year 1 runs well below one, at {{case:v.dscr_y1}}, so the file also needs an opening reserve. By Year 3 the store covers its debt {{case:v.dscr_y3}}.

Employers and wages

The Richmond metro is a state capital with a regional economy around it. BLS counted 724.2 thousand nonfarm jobs in September 2026, not seasonally adjusted and preliminary, against 731.2 thousand a year earlier, and a civilian labor force of 708,583 [^1]. Government supplies 116.1 thousand of the jobs and professional and business services 123.7 thousand, the latter down from 125.3 thousand in September 2025 [^1]. Two features matter to a laundromat. First, public employment is steady, and steady pay keeps a household doing laundry in a facility rather than skipping a cycle. Second, the metro lost jobs over the year, so the case should not lean on income growth. It leans on the number of households with a need.

The city is where the store would sit, and the city has its own record. Richmond held 167,696 jobs in covered establishments in March 2026, up 1.4% over the year, and paid an average weekly wage of $1,868 in the first quarter, up 4.8% [^3]. A different BLS release, the occupational wage survey for the metro area, gave a mean hourly wage of $32.89 across all occupations in May 2025 [^2]. Both numbers describe the whole pay scale, executives and civil servants included, and an attendant earns far less. The model therefore sets its attendant wage well below the mean and says so in the inputs. LAUS puts the city's unemployment rate at 4.1% for September 2026 (preliminary) [^4], above the metro's 3.8% [^1]. In plain terms, the city has a little more slack than its suburbs, which is common for a central city and is part of why the customer base is large.

Output is a different question. The last metro gross domestic product figure BEA published for Richmond is 116,959.541 million dollars for 2023, relayed by FRED from a series now marked discontinued and last updated on December 4, 2024 [^8]. It shows the scale of the economy, not the direction, and the case does not rely on it.

Households, renters and rent

Laundry demand follows households, and above all renting households, because an owner is far more likely to have machines at home. The register table below shows the City of Richmond from the federal American Community Survey for 2020 to 2024. Renter-occupied homes outnumber owner-occupied homes. A reader should treat the table as the case's core demand evidence: a large base of households, most of them renters, with incomes and rents that make a self-service store a rational choice for a share of them.

Two features of the table drive the model. Renters are more than half of all households, which is the base the store's turns rest on. And rent is a real cost against the median income, so a household weighs a trip to a laundromat against the premium a building with in-unit machines charges. Neither feature is a forecast of how many of those households will walk into one particular store. That depends on distance, parking, hours and price, which is why the site section and the lender conditions ask for a trade-area study of the actual bay.

The housing market around the renter

Three dated readings show where the renter base is heading. The multifamily broker reports that effective rents rose only 0.6% over the year to the third quarter of 2026 [^14]. It also reports 5,941 apartment units in development, mostly in Chesterfield County, with seven groundbreakings in the quarter [^14]. A new building usually puts washers and dryers inside the unit, so a pipeline that size, concentrated in the suburbs, adds renters that a city laundromat will not reach. The city's own permit count points the same way: 2,681 new private housing structures were authorized by building permits in 2025, relayed from the Census Bureau by FRED [^5]. New units in the city may sit near the store, and a lender should ask which of them have in-unit machines.

The ownership side is expensive. The median listing price in the city was $387,000 in September 2026, relayed from Realtor.com by FRED [^6], and the FHFA price index for the metro read 399.26 in the second quarter of 2026 against 386.23 a year earlier [^7]. High prices keep households renting longer, which is good for laundry demand, and the flat rent trend limits how much a landlord can pass on, which is good for the household's ability to pay a vend price. Neither reading is a count of households without machines. The study therefore does not claim one, and the lender conditions ask for the trade area's apartment inventory and its laundry amenities.

Regional activity that does not drive the store

Richmond International Airport handled 4,922,994 passengers in 2025, up 0.8%, with 223 million pounds of cargo and 108,527 aircraft movements, up 5.8% [^15]. These numbers show a busy regional economy. A neighborhood laundromat does not draw on air travel, and the case does not count the airport as demand. It uses the figures only to say that the region is active and the metro's weakness in 2026 is a softening, not a collapse.

A competitor census lists each operator that would bid for the same customers, with a page the operator controls or a public record that confirms it. For this case the research holds no news article, broker report or agency list that names a laundromat, an attended wash-dry-fold operator or a coin-operated dry cleaner in the city or the metro. Naming a business the record does not name would be invention, and a subject that is a generic bay cannot be listed as its own competitor. The census is therefore a lender condition: a named competitor census with each competitor's own website or county assessor record.

The federal industry code for coin-operated laundries and drycleaners is the self-service laundry industry, and the register grades the fit as exact. What the supply table tells a newcomer

The city count is small next to its renter base in the demand table, and the metro count is a few times the city's, so the city is not served by an unusually large share of the metro's stores. Two cautions limit the reading. The first is method: the survey leaves out businesses without employees, so an older owner-operated store that pays no wages is invisible, and the true number of stores a customer can reach is higher. The second is quality. A count says nothing about the age of the machines, the payment system, the hours, the attendant or the cleanliness of the store, which are the things a household chooses on. A new store with card payment, a bench for drop-off service and a staffed floor competes against that mix, not against a count.

That makes the lender conditions specific. The census a lender should ask for lists every store within a short drive of the bay, with machine counts, vend prices, hours and the presence of wash-dry-fold, and a note on which stores sit in apartment complexes where tenants have their own machines. The same list says which stores have closed in the last few years, because a closed store near the site is evidence of the trade area's limits.

The subject site is hypothetical. It is assumed to be an in-line bay of about {{case:v.in_bay_sf}} square feet in a neighborhood center in the Southside of the city, with parking in front and a landlord willing to accept a heavy water and drainage use. The study names no parcel, street, owner, landlord or listing, and the research holds no Richmond site record. The bay is the unit of analysis, and everything below describes the market that the lease will be signed in.

Retail is the tight segment

Cushman & Wakefield reports Richmond retail vacancy of 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026, with leasing of more than 1.3 million square feet in the first three quarters, net absorption above 240,000 square feet and 379,000 square feet in the pipeline, 74.3% preleased [^13]. These are tenant-scarce readings. A bay that already has the water service, floor drains and electrical capacity a laundromat needs is worth more to a landlord of a retail center than a bare shell, and a landlord with few vacancies has little reason to give a start-up a free-rent period or a build-out allowance. The model assumes none, and a landlord's letter of intent that adds either is a gain the study cannot count.

No retail asking rent is in the research. The case therefore brackets it with the two rents that are. CBRE reports an average office asking rent of $22.18 a square foot for the third quarter of 2026, up 2.9% over the year [^9], and an industrial asking rent of $9.02, up 1.8% in the quarter and 11.6% over the year [^11]. Retail space with a storefront and parking is a better address than industrial space, and in a tight market it prices closer to the office figure than the industrial one. The model's base rent is set near the office figure and labeled an assumption, and the lender condition is a landlord's written rent and a broker's retail rent survey for the corridor.

Office and industrial space as context

The other two segments say what a landlord can do if the store does not open. On office, the two brokers disagree, which is common, because each defines the Richmond market in its own way. CBRE reports office vacancy of 12.3% for the third quarter of 2026, up 70 basis points from 11.6%, with availability of 14.3% and net absorption of -281,000 square feet for the quarter [^9]. Cushman & Wakefield reports 12.5% overall and 18.6% in the central business district [^10], and 93,000 square feet of speculative construction in two medical office projects in Chesterfield County [^10]. The case does not combine or compare the two. Both say that office space is loose.

On industrial, CBRE reports vacancy of 5.2%, down 20 basis points, net absorption of 668,000 square feet and 12.2 million square feet under construction across 25 properties, with leasing of 2.6 million square feet in the quarter against 1.0 million a year earlier [^11]. Cushman & Wakefield reports overall vacancy of 4.1% and direct vacancy of 3.1%, year-to-date net absorption above 2.7 million square feet and 3.7 million square feet of speculative space underway [^12]. A distribution shell is the wrong building for a walk-in laundry, and an office floor is no better. The contrast is the point: retail, the segment a laundromat has to use, is the tightest of the three for a tenant that wants a storefront.

Utilities, zoning and traffic

The decisive site facts are not in the research. A laundromat is a water, sewer, gas and electric user, and the study cannot say whether the bay's water meter, sewer lateral, gas line and electrical service can carry the machines without costly upgrades, what the city charges for connections, or whether the zoning district permits the use. These are the first three physical conditions in the lender list. Traffic is the fourth: the research holds no VDOT count for any Southside road. A laundromat draws mainly from the surrounding blocks, so the questions that matter are the number of apartment buildings within a short walk or drive, the sightline from the street and the parking in front of the door. All of them need a site visit and a trade-area count, which is what a feasibility study of a real bay would provide.

A laundromat sells three things: wash cycles, dry cycles and attended wash-dry-fold service, plus a small amount of vending. The model builds each from a driver. Wash revenue is the number of washers times turns per washer per day times {{case:v.in_wash_price}} of average revenue per cycle. Dryer revenue is a share of wash revenue, {{case:v.in_dry_ratio}}, because most customers who wash also dry and dryers earn less per cycle. Wash-dry-fold revenue is pounds per day times {{case:v.in_wdf_price}} a pound. Turns per washer start low in Year 1 and reach {{case:v.in_turns}} in the stabilized third year, with only slight gains after it, a ramp that assumes a new store takes about three years to build its regulars. The inputs table shows the full path.

All the drivers are assumptions. The research holds no Richmond vend price, no turn data, no wash-dry-fold price and no operator financials, and the study uses no figure from any operator's books or from any client engagement. What the research does support is the shape of the customer base: a metro of 708,583 in the labor force with 116.1 thousand in government jobs [^1], a city with weekly pay of $1,868 [^3], and a register showing that renters outnumber owners in the city. Those facts argue that the store has customers. They do not say how many will choose this store, which is why the turns ramp is the first item a lender should test.

Revenue is {{case:v.rev_y1}} in Year 1, {{case:v.rev_y2}} in Year 2, {{case:v.rev_y3}} in Year 3 and {{case:v.rev_y5}} in Year 5. Prices are held flat through the five years, so all growth comes from turns and from the drop-off service. A lender should ask for meter data from two or three comparable stores, a measured count of washer cycles by machine size, and the posted vend schedule of each. Drop-off volume deserves the same test, because attended service is where an owner's labor produces revenue, and it is the line most dependent on the owner's marketing.

The cost structure is simple and heavy. Attendant payroll is paid hours times {{case:v.in_wage}} an hour plus a payroll burden of {{case:v.in_burden}}, with wages rising {{case:v.in_wage_growth}} a year. The wage is set well below the area's all-occupation mean of $32.89 an hour [^2], because that mean includes every profession, and the city's average weekly wage of $1,868 [^3] is also a poor guide to an attendant's pay. Fold-room labor is {{case:v.in_wdf_labor_pct}} of drop-off revenue, and an allowance of {{case:v.in_manager}} stands in for the owner's own pay, because a lender deducts a market wage for the owner's time.

Utilities are modeled at {{case:v.in_util_pct}} of revenue for water, sewer, gas and electricity. They are the largest cost after labor and rent in a business that heats water and air all day, and the research holds no Richmond water, sewer or gas rate, so the share is a judgment. Repairs and supplies take {{case:v.in_repairs_pct}}, the payment system and processing {{case:v.in_system_pct}}, and marketing {{case:v.in_marketing_pct}}. Rent is a triple-net lease at {{case:v.in_rent_psf}} base plus {{case:v.in_nnn_psf}} of charges per square foot a year, growing with the same rate as wages. Insurance and the business tangible property tax are fixed lines. Virginia localities tax the machines and fixtures a business owns, and the city's rate and method are not in the research, so the tax line is a labeled placeholder.

{{case:opex}}

Operating costs grow from {{case:v.opex_y1}} in Year 1 to {{case:v.opex_y5}} in Year 5, and net operating income rises from {{case:v.noi_y1}} to {{case:v.noi_y2}} in Year 2 and {{case:v.noi_y3}} in Year 3. The store is fixed-cost heavy in its first year: rent, insurance, tax and a minimum staff are paid whether or not the machines are turning, which is why Year 1 income is thin and the opening reserve is part of the loan. Once the machines turn at the stabilized rate, most of each extra dollar of revenue falls to income, which is why the sensitivity to turns is steep.

{{case:loan}}

The rules are those of SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, effective October 1, 2026 [^17]. A start-up borrower injects at least 10%, and its projections have two years to reach 1.15x coverage, so the test falls on Year 2. This project buys no land or building. The SOP's maturity rule for a mixed-purpose loan is blended maturity, or up to 25 years when 51% or more of proceeds are real estate; not available for changes of ownership [^17]. With equipment, a leasehold build-out and working capital as the uses, no part of the proceeds is real estate, and the model uses a ten-year term. The rate is Prime, 7.00% on October 9, 2026 [^19], plus the largest variable spread SBA allows for a loan of this size, {{case:v.rate}} in all; a lender may price lower, and the rate is set at closing. SBA guarantees {{case:v.in_sba_guaranty_pct}} of a loan this size [^16].

The size limit does not bind. The request of {{case:v.loan_total_proposed}} is far below the $5 million 7(a) maximum, and the equity tier is met at the program minimum. The test that fails is coverage. Annual debt service on the request is {{case:v.ds_proposed}} and the Year 2 income covers it {{case:v.dscr_y2_proposed}}. At the restructured loan, debt service is {{case:v.ds}} and coverage is exactly at the floor.

The guaranty fee for fiscal 2027 is charged on the guaranteed portion and added to project cost [^18]. The fee waiver for rural borrowers stops at $700,000, so it cannot apply to a loan of this size whatever the city's status. Laundromat equipment is a movable asset, and the lender will want a first lien on it, an assignment of the lease with the landlord's consent and a personal guaranty from the owner. The owner's eligibility is tested at the lender's review under the SOP in force at approval. Nothing here is a statement that SBA or any lender would approve the loan.

The study is the lender's tool here, not a program checkpoint: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^20]. For a first-time operator opening a store with no history, a lender may well ask for one, and a laundromat feasibility study consultant can put the market read and the coverage test in front of the credit committee before the application.

{{case:sensitivity}}

The matrix tests Year 2 coverage on the restructured loan across five steps of turns per day and five of average revenue per wash. Coverage ranges from {{case:v.sens_min}} to {{case:v.sens_max}}. Both drivers act on wash revenue and, through the dryer ratio, on dryer revenue, while most costs are fixed or follow revenue by a small share, so a small change in either moves income by a much larger share. A price cut to win customers from a competitor is therefore as dangerous as a shortfall in traffic: the same revenue loss, with no relief in costs. The restructured loan sits on the floor in the center cell, so every step down in either driver is a failure and every step up is a cushion.

{{case:inputs}}

  1. The lease

    Retail vacancy of 3.4% gives a landlord the upper hand [^13]. A rent above the model's placeholder, a short initial term, a renewal at market, or a landlord who will not consent to the loan's lien would each undo the structure. The leasehold build-out has no value outside the lease.

  2. Turns and the ramp

    The model's ramp is a judgment, and the break-even cushion is modest. A store that opens into a trade area with several established stores may take longer than three years to fill.

  3. Apartments with their own machines

    The multifamily pipeline holds 5,941 units [^14], and the city authorized 2,681 new private housing structures in 2025 [^5]. New buildings tend to carry in-unit machines, which shrinks the pool of renters who need a laundromat near the site.

  4. A softening metro

    Nonfarm jobs were 724.2 thousand in September 2026 against 731.2 thousand a year earlier, and unemployment rose from 3.3% to 3.8% [^1]. A weaker labor market lowers the household's budget for drop-off service first.

  5. Utility cost and water rates

    The utility share is a judgment because the research holds no local rate. A rate increase on water and sewer, which are billed by volume, lands directly on the margin.

  6. Equipment and technology

    Machines wear, card systems change and a competitor with newer equipment can take customers. The model carries repairs as a share of revenue and no replacement reserve; a lender may ask for one.

  7. Labor and the owner

    The owner's time is the unmodeled input. An owner who cannot attend the store, or an attendant market that pushes wages above the model's, reduces income. The mean hourly wage across all occupations was $32.89 in May 2025 [^2], and a store competes for attendants with every low-wage employer in the metro.

  8. Rates and fees

    The variable rate moves with Prime, so a rise in Prime raises debt service on a loan priced as a spread over it. The guaranty fee for the fiscal year is added to project cost and falls on the borrower.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open.

  1. Named competitor census

    A named competitor census with each competitor's own website or county assessor record: every laundromat, attended wash-dry-fold operator and coin-operated dry cleaner within a short drive of the bay, with machine counts, vend prices, hours and closures.

  2. Lease terms and retail rent

    The landlord's signed letter of intent: base rent, triple-net charges, term, renewal options, any build-out allowance or free rent, consent to the lien and assignment, and a broker's retail rent survey for the corridor. The research holds no Richmond retail rent.

  3. Trade-area demand

    A count of apartment buildings and households within the trade area, the share with in-unit machines, the traffic count on the access road, and walking and driving distances. The research holds no VDOT count and no inventory of laundry amenities.

  4. Utilities, zoning and permits

    Written confirmation of water and sewer capacity and connection charges, gas and electrical service, the zoning district's treatment of the use, parking, and the permit fee schedule. The research holds no Richmond rate or fee.

  5. Operating evidence

    Meter data and utility bills from two or three comparable stores, a measured turn rate by machine size, the posted vend schedule of each, and a wash-dry-fold price survey. These replace the turns, price, dryer ratio, drop-off and utility assumptions.

  6. Costs and equipment

    A contractor's bid on the bay, a vendor's machine schedule and quotation, delivery and installation quotes, and bound quotes for property, liability and business-interruption insurance.

  7. Local taxes

    The city's business tangible property tax rate and assessment method for machines and fixtures, which the research does not hold.

  8. Owner and program eligibility

    The owner's experience, time commitment and personal financial statement, the injection's source and documentation, and the lender's eligibility review under the SOP in force at approval.

  9. Opening reserve

    A funded reserve for the Year 1 gap between operating income and debt service.

  10. Apartment supply

    The broker's apartment vacancy level and rent, which the multifamily page used here does not print, and a list of new buildings within the trade area.
