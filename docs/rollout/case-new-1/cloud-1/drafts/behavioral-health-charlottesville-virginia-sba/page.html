This page runs that work on one hypothetical project in Charlottesville and Albemarle County, Virginia: an outpatient practice that owns no real estate today and wants to buy a building, convert it and move in, financed with an SBA 504 loan. The subject is generic. A group of therapists and prescribers, in business well past its second year, leaves rented rooms for an existing two-story office building of about {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet on the US 29 corridor north of the city. The practice sees adults and adolescents in the office and by video, and it runs no beds. Every market figure is public and dated. Residents and employers

The Charlottesville metro is a small labor market that a clinic can describe street by street. Its September 2026 payroll of 128.4 thousand compares with 124.9 thousand as the 2025 annual average on the same BLS page [^1]. The city holds 40,208 covered jobs [^2] for 44,388 residents [^3], a high count of jobs for a city of its size, which fits a regional employment center whose workers partly live elsewhere. A clinic on the county side of the line can reach both the residents who live there and the people who work in town.

The metro's economic output was 18,817.343 million dollars in 2023, the last year of a Bureau of Economic Analysis series that FRED marks discontinued [^7]. The state's real output was 639,147.7 million chained 2017 dollars at the annual rate for the second quarter of 2026 [^13]. Both numbers frame the payer base, the employers whose health plans pay for visits, without sizing a single practice's revenue.

The state labor market

Virginia is in a period of adjustment. The state counted 4,250.6 thousand nonfarm jobs and a civilian labor force of 4,461.2 thousand in September 2026, both preliminary [^9]. A news report on state labor data put the number of unemployed Virginians at 173,934 in January 2026 [^11]. Federal jobs in the state fell by 22,400 between November 2024 and November 2025, according to a regional news report on state workforce data [^12]. A payroll loss that large moves households from employer coverage toward Medicaid or marketplace plans, which shifts a clinic's payer mix. The research holds no payer data to size that shift, so the case treats the payer mix as the largest unknown in the model.

Small-business churn is the other signal. In the second quarter of 2025 Virginia's private sector gained 197,863 jobs and lost 196,049, with 43,606 jobs added at opening establishments and 33,616 lost at closing ones [^10]. The labor market turns over constantly, which supports a steady inflow of patients changing jobs and coverage, and it also means a newly hired clinician has choices.

Visitors and the service workforce

Charlottesville's visitor economy is large next to its population. Visitors spent $603.5 million in Albemarle County and $427.7 million in the city in 2025, and tourism supported 7,217 jobs across the city and county [^8]. Statewide, 46.6 million overnight visitors came in 2025 [^19]. Tourism jobs are a large part of the local workforce, and the record does not say how many of them carry employer health coverage. A practice meets that part of the workforce through employee assistance programs, Medicaid and self-pay in unknown proportions, so this is context and not a measure.

Housing and the cost of recruiting

Housing sets the labor supply that staffs the clinic. The federal index for the Charlottesville metro read 421.33 in the second quarter of 2026 against a 1995 first-quarter base of 100 [^6], while the state index read 780.93 against a 1980 base [^14]; the two bases differ, so the pair cannot be compared directly. Statewide, the median listing price was $455,953 in September 2026, with 26,222 active listings and 11,204 new listings that month [^16,17,18]. Local governments authorized 2,641 new housing units statewide in September [^15]. Albemarle and the city list above the state median [^4,5,16], and a clinician who lives in the county pays that premium. The model therefore pays wages that are planning figures and flags a local wage survey as a lender condition.

What the supply table tells a newcomer

Two readings matter. First, the county's count of establishments is larger than the city's, so a clinic in Albemarle is not entering an area with few providers. Second, every figure is a lower bound, because the register flags cells that the census withholds, so the share that a new practice would add to the class's employment cannot be computed. What can be said is that a practice with this case's roster needs a steady inflow of new patients to keep its rooms booked, and that inflow competes with every establishment counted above.

The unknown is quality of fit. A count of establishments says nothing about who accepts new patients, which payers they take, or how long a new patient waits. A study for a real site needs the waitlists and payer panels of the nearest competitors, and the lender conditions below ask for them.

The subject site is hypothetical. It is assumed to be an existing two-story office building with its lot on the US 29 corridor north of the city in Albemarle County, with surface parking and a street frontage that patients can find. The study names no parcel, street, owner, seller or listing, and the research holds no site record for the area. The purchase price, renovation and soft costs are round assumptions set out in the inputs, and an appraisal and a purchase contract replace them.

Several site facts decide whether a behavioral health use is allowed and none is in the research. The county's zoning district must permit a medical or counseling office in the building, and a change of use from general office may bring a site plan amendment, a parking count and accessibility work. The flood zone, the septic or sewer service and the water and electric providers are not in the record either, and the building's age and condition drive the renovation allowance. Each is a lender condition.

Traffic and access matter differently to a clinic than to a retailer. Patients arrive at scheduled times, often anxious, and a confusing entrance or a hard left turn off a busy road is a missed visit. The research holds no VDOT count or transit schedule for the corridor, so the study asks for a traffic count at the site entrance and a walkthrough of the patient's route from the parking space to the waiting room. Privacy is a design fact: the building needs sound isolation between rooms, a waiting area that is not visible from the street, and a secure entry. The renovation allowance carries the cost.

The labor shed is the last site factor. The city's 40,208 covered jobs in March 2026 [^2] sit inside a metro of 128.4 thousand [^1], and a clinic north of the city draws clinicians from both. A site that works for staff living in the city and the county alike is easier to staff.

The operator is an established practice, not a start-up, and not a landlord. Its revenue is the visits it delivers times what it collects per visit. The model carries two lines. Therapy revenue is the number of therapists times the visits one therapist can see in a year at full booking, times the share of that capacity actually booked, times the net collected per visit. Medication-management revenue repeats the build for prescribers, with shorter visits and more of them. A collected-rate index multiplies both, so that a payer rate cut or a worse payer mix can be tested in one move.

At stabilization the practice rosters {{case:v.in_therapists}} therapists and {{case:v.in_prescribers}} prescribers. A therapist sees {{case:v.in_ther_cap}} visits a year at full booking and a prescriber {{case:v.in_presc_cap}}, and the practice books {{case:v.in_fill}} of that capacity. Net collected revenue is {{case:v.in_net_ther}} per therapy visit and {{case:v.in_net_med}} per medication visit. Every one of these is an assumption. The research holds no payer mix, allowed amount, collection rate, caseload or wait time for the Charlottesville area, and the study uses no figure from any practice's books. What the research does show is a metro of 128.4 thousand jobs [^1] and a county of 118,356 residents [^3] in which a practice of this size can plausibly fill rooms; it does not show how fast.

A behavioral health practice spends most of its revenue on the people who deliver visits. A billing and credentialing service takes a share of collected revenue. Insurance, records and telehealth software, utilities, outreach and general administration are allowances. {{case:loan}}

An SBA 504 project is split three ways. The rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^20]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the business is new and the property is special purpose [^20].

The subject is an established practice, so the question is the property. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built and lists these examples: Amusement parks; Bowling alleys [^20]. A converted office with small sound-isolated rooms is arguably adaptable to ordinary office use, which would keep the standard tier. The list, however, names health and medical facilities, and a lender or CDC could read a clinic as one. The case takes the conservative reading and applies the higher tier. Whether the clinic is special purpose, and what an appraisal says a general office user would pay for the converted building, are lender conditions.

The size limit passes at both structures. The debenture of {{case:v.debenture_proposed}} as proposed is under the $5 million limit for a project that is not a small manufacturer [^20]. As restructured, the debenture is {{case:v.debenture}}, {{case:v.debenture_position}}, and the bank lends {{case:v.tpl}}, {{case:v.tpl_share}} of cost. The program offers 10, 20 or 25 years and favors the longer terms where real estate is the larger part of the project; the building and its conversion are, so the model uses a 25-year debenture and a 25-year bank amortization.

The owner-occupancy test shapes the purchase. For an existing building the operating borrower must occupy 51% (60% for a new building) [^20]. The model has the practice use the whole building, so the test is met on its face, and a lender will check the floor plan for any space that might be sublet. The eligibility of the operator itself, including its ownership, its licences and its size, is a lender and CDC determination that the study does not make.

The coverage test looks at history first. The program's basis is historical EBITDA over combined post-transaction debt service, last fiscal year-end or two-year average; projections analysed only where history falls short [^20]. This case has no practice financials and cannot run the historical test, so it applies the projection and lists the practice's last two years of earnings as a lender condition. {{case:inputs}}

  1. Payer mix and rates

    The model rests on assumed collected rates, and the sensitivity matrix shows they are the sharper driver. Federal job losses of 22,400 in the state over the year to November 2025 [^12] can move insured households to other coverage, and the record holds no payer data to size it.

  2. Clinician recruiting

    The roster is the revenue. Median listing prices of $530,250 in the city and $596,870 in the county [^4,5] make relocation expensive for a recruit, and a vacancy in the prescriber seats removes the visits with the highest rate.

  3. Competing capacity

    The register counts at least 70 establishments in the metro class, a partial fit and a lower bound (source line in the competitors section); the field is not empty, the competitors are unnamed, and a hospital-based or community program can add capacity faster than a private practice can.

  4. The special purpose reading

    If the lender or CDC reads the clinic as a general-purpose office, the equity tier falls. If it reads the building as a special purpose property with a thin resale market, the appraisal may value it below cost and the borrower covers the gap.

  5. Conversion cost and timing

    The renovation is an allowance. An older building may hide sound, fire-code, accessibility and system costs. Local governments authorized 2,641 housing units statewide in September [^15], which shows competing demand for the same trades.

  6. Rates

    The bank rate is set at pricing and the debenture rate at sale. A higher rate raises debt service and lowers coverage in the same proportion.

  7. Regulation and licensure

    A change of address and a change of use may require licence amendments and local approvals before the first visit. The research holds no licensing timetable, so a delay is possible, and the practice pays debt service during it.

  8. Costs after Year 3

    Rates are held flat and costs inflate, so coverage declines after the roster is full. A practice that cannot negotiate higher payer rates sees its margin fall every year.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open.

  1. Named competitor census

    A named competitor census with each competitor's own website or assessor record: group practices, hospital-based outpatient programs, the community services board and solo therapists within a short drive, with their payer panels and waitlists.

  2. Operator eligibility and history

    Confirmation by the lender and CDC that the operator is an eligible business, including ownership, licences and size, and the practice's last two fiscal years of earnings, trailing visits by clinician and payer, kept-visit rate and waitlist, to run the history test and replace the booking, rate and cost assumptions.

  3. Special purpose determination

    The lender's and CDC's written reading of whether the clinic is special purpose property, an appraisal that states what a general office user would pay for the converted building, and the resulting equity tier.

  4. Payer contracts and reimbursement

    The practice's payer contracts, allowed amounts by visit type and the share of revenue from commercial plans, Medicaid, Medicare and self-pay, with the effect of any pending rate change.

  5. Site, title and zoning

    The parcel identifier from the county assessor, a written zoning determination that permits a counseling and medical office, the survey, the FEMA flood zone and panel, a Phase I environmental assessment and the building condition report.

  6. Traffic, access and parking

    A traffic count at the site entrance, the parking count the county requires for the use, and an accessibility review of the route from parking to the waiting room.

  7. Utilities, fees and taxes

    The electric, gas, water and sewer or septic providers for the parcel, capacity fees, the adopted real estate tax rate and the assessment method.

  8. Costs and equipment

    Contractor bids for the conversion, a sound-isolation specification, quotes for the records and telehealth systems, and quotes for professional liability, property and flood insurance.

  9. Wages and recruiting

    A local wage survey for licensed counselors, social workers and prescribers, and signed offers or a recruiting plan for the roster the model assumes.

  10. Licensure and approvals

    The state licensing agency's requirements for the services offered and the change of address, and the timeline for approval before the first visit.

  11. Opening reserve

    A funded reserve for the Year 1 gap between net operating income and debt service.
