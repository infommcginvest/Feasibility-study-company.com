Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A study joins a dated market read, a census of competitors, a priced build and a coverage test that shows how much debt a project can carry. This page runs that work on one hypothetical project: a physician-owned ambulatory surgery center (ASC) with a leased medical office wing, financed with an SBA 504 loan in Lebanon, Tennessee. Ask us to run the same work on your site.

The subject is generic: a two-story building of about {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet on about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres in the South Hartmann Drive medical corridor, with {{case:v.in_asc_sf}} square feet for the surgery center (two operating rooms and a procedure room) and {{case:v.in_leased_sf}} square feet of office space for outside physician tenants. Every market figure is public and dated. Every model figure is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed near the end, and each input carries a source or the word assumption. The project belongs to our medical office and ASC practice, the town sits in Tennessee at the eastern edge of the Nashville metro, and the program rules are laid out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

{{case:glance}}

The demand answer is yes. Lebanon counted 53,412 residents on July 1, 2025, up from an estimates base of 38,495 in April 2020, a gain of 38.8% [^1]. Wilson County, which supplies most of the patients a center here would draw on, grew 18.5% over the same span to 175,033 [^1]. The county's median household income of $95,839 is well above the city's $72,848 [^1], and commercially insured patients carry a surgery center's margin.

The competitive answer is harder. A health system has just converted its Lebanon hospital outpatient department into a licensed surgery center, which the state commission approved on June 25, 2025 [^7]. It stands in the same corridor as the subject [^6]. A second system runs a hospital outpatient surgery department nearby, a third has a gastroenterology center on Physicians Way, and the one independent physician-owned center in the census sits in Mt. Juliet [^13,14,15,16].

The regulatory answer governs timing. Tennessee still lists the ambulatory surgical treatment center among the institutions that need a certificate of need [^19]. The study does not rely on any change to that rule. The model does not predict when the permit comes. It assumes the permit is in hand before the first case, and the lender conditions make that a gate.

The financing answer follows from all three. Under the sponsor's {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} equity proposal the project costs {{case:v.tpc_proposed}} and the SBA debenture would be {{case:v.debenture_proposed}}, above the $5 million limit for a project that is not a small manufacturer. Year 2 coverage is {{case:v.dscr_y2_proposed}} against a floor of 1.15x. Three tests fail: the equity tier, the debenture size and the coverage floor.

Restructured, the sponsor brings {{case:v.equity}}, {{case:v.equity_pct}} of project cost, the bank lends {{case:v.tpl}} and the debenture falls to {{case:v.debenture}}. That structure meets all three tests on these numbers and reaches {{case:v.dscr_y3}} at stabilization. It is a different deal from the one proposed, and the sensitivity table shows how little room the ramp year leaves.

A city filling in faster than its county

The city grew far faster than the county around it, 38.8% against 18.5% [^1]. The county's gain of 4,693 residents in the year to July 2025 was the third largest numeric gain of any Tennessee county, according to a relocation survey built on Census estimates [^5], and county permitting points the same way, with 1,982 residential building permits in 2025 [^1]. A surgery center needs the households within a short drive of its corridor, and those are the ones moving in.

Income and age

The county's median household income is far above the city's [^1]. The gap implies that households outside the city limits earn more still, and a center in the corridor draws from both groups. Residents 65 and over are 16.2% of the city and 17.0% of the county, below Tennessee's 18.6% [^1]. A younger-than-state profile favours work that younger and middle-aged adults need, such as sports injuries, hand and spine surgery, ENT and pain procedures, over a cataract-heavy mix. That will drift as retirement housing fills: the mayor told a local paper that emergency need would climb as residential projects and a major Del Webb retirement community are completed [^7]. The research also reports a share of residents under 65 without health insurance, but with no stated vintage, so it is not printed. The payer mix of the trade area is a lender condition.

The hospital and the health economy

Vanderbilt Wilson County Hospital is the area's anchor. The Vanderbilt system took it over on August 1, 2019, when it was licensed for 245 beds on two campuses [^10], and it describes itself as the only hospital in the county with a Level III trauma designation [^12]. The county's economic development board put its 2017 activity at more than 5,000 admissions, more than 65,000 outpatient visits and nearly 6,700 surgeries [^11]. That is old data, but it sets the scale, and the physicians who work at that hospital are the likeliest owners of a competing center. The 2022 Economic Census put the county's health care and social assistance receipts at $559,308,000 [^1]. In November 2025 the same system announced a 40-bed inpatient rehabilitation hospital in Lebanon, expected to open in 2028 [^9], a sign that it expects growth to continue.

What the field tells a newcomer

The surgical market here is run by systems. HCA, Vanderbilt and Ascension Saint Thomas each hold a Lebanon outpatient position, and the only confirmed independent center, Providence, is in Mt. Juliet [^13,14,15,16,17]. The newest entrant, HCA's conversion, stands on Willard Hagan Drive in the same medical stretch as the subject [^6]. A new center there would compete head-on for orthopaedic, spine, ENT and pain cases, and its advantage would have to come from the physicians who bring cases. The model's volume cannot be stronger than a signed commitment from those physicians, which is why the lender conditions ask for one.

Providence offers one more fact. A 2022 bid record describes the relocation of a multi-specialty surgery center with one added procedure room, a renovation of 38,235 square feet valued at $2,500,000 [^18]. It is one project of a different type, a renovation and not a ground-up build, so this study does not use it for cost. It does show an independent center investing in new space while the systems expanded.

Listings the study does not rely on

Two more names turned up in directories only. A center formerly named Phoenix Ambulatory Surgery Center, now registered under an Ascension Saint Thomas entity name, is listed on Physicians Way with no operator website. An eye surgery center on West Main Street is listed in directories, but one directory marks it permanently closed and the Vanderbilt system reported in December 2019 that a surgery center next to its Lebanon eye institute had closed [^10], so its status is unresolved. A further registry entry beside the Vanderbilt campus is unverified. None is a census row, because none can be tied to a page the operator controls, and all three enter the lender conditions as sites to verify. The research also excluded a new surgery center in a different Lebanon, in Missouri.

Medical office competition

The lease listings in town include an established building on Physicians Way, a new clinic building on Willard Hagan Drive and an older medical office building on West Baddour Parkway. The asking rents were read from a listing site in October 2026, and a viewing date does not date a rent, so none is printed. The model rents the office wing at a round planning figure inside the listings' spread and fills it over several years, because it would lease beside existing advertised supply.

New supply and the permit gate

The pipeline is mostly institutional. On September 24, 2025 the Health Facilities Commission approved a freestanding emergency department proposed by Sumner Regional Medical Center on Highway 109 North, at a stated cost of $21,583,185, and denied a competing TriStar Summit proposal near the HCA surgery center, at a stated cost of $18,060,545, an application Vanderbilt Wilson County Hospital opposed [^8]. Neither is a surgery center, but together they show a regulator that decides contested Lebanon projects on the record. The research found no other surgery center application in Wilson County for 2025 or 2026 [^8].

The rule for the subject is simpler to state than to time. The commission lists the ambulatory surgical treatment center among the institutions that need a certificate of need and excludes premises used exclusively as a physician's professional practice office [^19]. The office wing therefore sits outside the permit; the surgery center does not. The state licensing form says the certificate must come before the licensure application [^21]. HCA's file shows how long one application took: its notice was published by April 15, 2025, it was due to file on May 1, and the commission approved it on June 25 [^6,7].

The study does not count on the rule changing. The research could tie no change to a primary legal text, so counsel must confirm whether and when any change reaches a new surgery center. The General Assembly's 2024 amendment conditions licensure of a non-hospital center on TennCare participation at levels comparable to hospital-based centers and on comparable charity care [^20]. That pulls the blended revenue per case down, a reason to read the model's {{case:v.in_net_per_case}} as a planning figure that could prove high. Further 2026 bills would end the requirement for hospitals and satellite emergency departments, but they are aimed at hospitals and not at surgery centers [^22].

A ground-up building takes time to design, approve and build, and a lender cannot rely on a change in the law. The model assumes the center opens with the permit in hand, and Year 1 carries the cost of waiting.

The subject is a hypothetical site of about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres in the South Hartmann Drive medical corridor. Its parcel, zoning district and utility territory are conditions to confirm. The research could not retrieve a parcel identifier, a flood zone, an electric provider or a traffic count, so the paragraphs below describe the rules that would apply and what they would cost.

Zoning, frontage and utilities

The city's office and medical district permits health care facilities and medical services as uses, and its only conditional use is secure personal and group care [^23]. The district sheet also sets building height, minimum lot and setback limits that a site plan must meet. Whether a surgery center is permitted by right in the city's residential-professional office districts was not found; a 2021 zoning diagnostic report describes one only as a medium-density residential and professional office zone [^24]. The subject therefore needs a written zoning determination for its actual parcel. No traffic count for South Hartmann Drive or nearest interchange was found. A surgery center is an appointment-driven use, but a medical office wing depends on visibility from the road.

The city publishes a water tap schedule by meter size, but the page carries no effective date, so no tap fee is printed [^25]. The capacity fees are better documented. In 2022 the city raised its water capacity fee from $350 to $2,500 and its sewer capacity fee from $2,200 to $4,500 per single-family unit, and grandfathering of the old fees was set to end on July 31, 2025 [^26]. Those are residential rates; a commercial equivalent was not found. Staff drafted a fee schedule raising building, plumbing, sewer and fixture fees in July 2025, and its adoption was not found [^27]. The fees are small beside the site work allowance. The real exposure is a line that does not reach the parcel.

Land

The research records only broker listings for corridor land. A listing's size and price describe one property, and an asking price is not a sale, so the study neither prints nor cites them. {{case:loan}}

An SBA 504 project is split three ways. The rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^35]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the project is both [^35]. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built, and its example list names surgery centers and other medical facilities [^35]. A newly formed surgery center company is a new business on special purpose property, which puts it in the top tier. The sponsor's {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} applies the tier for a new business alone and misses the special purpose test.

At {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} the proposal also fails the size test: the debenture would be {{case:v.debenture_proposed}}, above the $5 million limit for a project that is not a small manufacturer [^35]. At {{case:v.equity_pct}} the debenture falls to {{case:v.debenture}}, {{case:v.debenture_position}}, and the bank lends {{case:v.tpl}}, {{case:v.tpl_share}} of cost. The program offers 10, 20 or 25 years and follows the larger asset class; land, building and site work are the majority of proceeds here, so the model uses a 25-year debenture and a 25-year bank amortization.

The owner-occupancy test shapes the building. For a new building the test is 60% (51% for an existing one) [^35]. The model gives the surgery center {{case:v.in_asc_sf}} of the {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet and leases the other {{case:v.in_leased_sf}} to outside tenants, which leaves the operating borrower in about five eighths of its own building. A lender or CDC will want the floor plan and the lease schedule to confirm it.

The debenture rate is {{case:v.in_debenture_rate}}, the 25-year effective 504 rate from the October 8, 2026 pricing [^34]; the actual rate is set when the debenture is sold. The bank rate is an assumption of {{case:v.in_tpl_rate}}: Prime was 7.00% in the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [^37], plus half a point for a start-up surgery center, and SBA caps the third-party spread at 6% over New York Prime [^35]. The FY2027 upfront fee of 0.50% on the debenture is added to project cost in the model [^36]. SBA's jobs standard is one job per $95,000 of debenture for loans approved on or after October 1, 2025; the proposed debenture is far larger than the restructured one, so the proposal would face a much higher job count. The Mid-Cumberland Area Development Corporation lends across a group of Middle Tennessee counties that includes Wilson [^32], and public 504 loan records for the county are a lender condition, because the SBA dataset the research found is not filtered to Wilson County [^33].

{{case:inputs}}

One input comes straight from a source, the 25-year debenture rate. Everything else is an assumption, including land, construction, case volume, revenue per case, rent, staffing and the operating allowances. The research for this town holds market evidence and rules but no unit economics for a surgery center, so the assumptions carry the model. The most important to verify are net revenue per case, case volume and building cost, in that order.

  1. The permit takes longer than the building

    A new surgery center needs the commission's certificate [^19]. HCA's file went from a published notice by April 15, 2025 to approval on June 25 [^6,7], and a contested file will not move as fast. A finished building that waits for its permit sits idle while interest runs.

  2. The ramp is slower than modelled

    A system-owned center has just opened in the same corridor, and two more systems run surgery in the city [^6,13,14,15]. A late ramp fails the Year 2 test.

  3. Payer terms are worse than modelled

    A 2024 amendment conditions licensure of a non-hospital center on TennCare participation and charity care comparable to hospital-based centers [^20]. A heavier public-payer share lowers the blend, and the sensitivity table shows what a one-step shortfall does.

  4. The surgeons do not bring the cases

    A physician-owned center lives on its owners' referrals. If the founding surgeons keep operating at a hospital for convenience, the ramp does not happen. Signed commitments are a lender condition for this reason.

  5. The office wing does not lease

    Existing medical office buildings in town already advertise space, and a new clinic building is on the market. If tenants do not come, the borrower must still occupy enough of the building to pass the occupancy test, and the rent line shrinks.

  6. Property tax resets

    The county reappraisal raised values by an average of 66% and the rate fell to $1.1657 to keep the county budget neutral [^30,31], but a commercial parcel's levy depends on an assessment ratio and a city rate the research did not find.

  7. Costs run high, or staff run short

    Land and building cost are assumptions, and every dollar of overrun adds debt the center can only just carry. Operating rooms also need nurses, technologists and sterile processing staff from the first day, and a shortage delays the opening or forces agency staff at higher cost.

Each condition below closes a gap the public record left open. Several group related items the research reported as not found.

  1. Certificate of need and licensure

  2. Site, title and zoning

    The parcel identifier from the county assessor, a written zoning determination that a surgery center and a medical office are allowed on it [^23,24], and the appraisal, survey and sale history of the land.

    A state count station and annual average daily traffic for the corridor, the nearest interchange, the FEMA flood zone and panel, and a Phase I environmental site assessment.

  4. Utility service, fees and taxes

    Signed commitments from the founding surgeons for a base volume of cases, the specialty mix, a payer-mix study with allowed amounts for the commercial, Medicare and TennCare panels, and any seasonality in elective volume. The research found none of these.

  6. Competitor detail

    The operating room count for the Vanderbilt hospital surgery center, operator websites for the two directory-only centers, the status of the eye surgery center and the unverified registry entry, year opened for each competitor, posted procedure prices, county assessor records for each, and any other Wilson County surgery center application in 2025 or 2026 [^8].

    A funded reserve for the Year 1 shortfall between operating income and debt service, held until the stabilized year is reached.
