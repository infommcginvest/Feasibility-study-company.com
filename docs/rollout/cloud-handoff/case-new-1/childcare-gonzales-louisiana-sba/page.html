This page shows the work on a hypothetical project: a new, purpose-built center in Gonzales, the commercial heart of Ascension Parish, financed with an SBA 7(a) start-up loan. Every market figure is public and dated. Every model figure is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed further down, each of which carries either a source or the word assumption.

The site is generic by design. The town, the parish and the corridor are real; the parcel, the sponsor and the project are not. That keeps the study about method, and it means no figure here belongs to any particular owner's deal.

{{case:glance}}

The market answer is yes. Gonzales counted 13,930 residents in the Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 estimates, up 15.2% from an estimates base of 12,089 in April 2020 [^1]. The parish around it grew from 126,499 at the 2020 Census to 135,105 in July 2025 [^4]. Heavy industry is arriving along the Geismar to Gonzales corridor, and the state's own waiting list for subsidized child care is thousands of children long [^9,10]. No dated source shows licensed supply growing at that pace, and the local planning record contains no sign of a new center in the pipeline [^18,19].

A city that is small but not still

Gonzales is a modest place by population and a busy one by growth. It added 1,841 people over the estimates period (13,930 less 12,089), 15.2% on that base [^1], and it packs 1,337.9 residents into each square mile of the city [^1]. Next door, Prairieville held 33,197 people at the 2020 Census [^5], about 2.7 times the 2020 Gonzales count of 12,231 [^1], and the families who live there and in Dutchtown and Geismar are part of the same child care trade area. The parish count shows the same direction on a larger scale: five annual estimates from 126,975 in 2020 to 134,027 in 2024, then 135,105 in July 2025 [^4]. The parish gained between 1,564 and 2,038 residents in each of the four years from 2020 to 2024, and 8,606 over the whole span from the 2020 Census to July 2025 (135,105 less 126,499). The line rises every year.

The weaker half of the picture is income. Gonzales had a median household income of $45,875 in the 2024 American Community Survey, and a median age of 38.9 years [^2]. That income is far below what a new-build center usually needs its families to earn, and the gap shapes the tuition the model can assume. Parish-wide, households are far better off, as the federal register figures above show, but a center on the Gonzales side of the parish draws first from the city and its neighbors, so the study carries tuition inside the range local operators publish rather than a suburban price.

Plants, payrolls and the parents who staff them

The parish is in the middle of an industrial build-out, and the employers are specific. Senator John Kennedy's office listed a $5.8 billion Hyundai steel plant, announced in March 2025 and expected to support more than 1,300 direct jobs, and a $4 billion ammonia plant called Blue Point One, which broke ground on August 26, 2026 [^6]. In February 2025 the state announced a $69 million Gonzales facility for an industrial controls company, with 175 direct jobs over ten years and a $12.5 million payroll [^7]. At Geismar, Chevron's renewable diesel unit is growing from 90 million to 340 million gallons a year of capacity, a gain of 250 million; the plant manager told a local business paper that the site has more than 160 Chevron employees and about 350 workers in all, and that construction will put more than 1,500 workers on site over roughly two and a half years [^8].

These are not child care numbers, and the study does not turn them into enrolment. What they establish is direction: plant operators, contractors and the suppliers around them are adding payrolls to the Geismar to Gonzales corridor, and shift workers need care that opens early and closes late. A center that takes early hours and a long day is serving that workforce. It is also competing with those employers for staff, which the operating-expense section returns to.

Public money and a waiting list

Louisiana's Child Care Assistance Program, known as CCAP, pays part of tuition for eligible working families. At the end of calendar 2025 it supported 16,871 children in voucher seats, while 5,371 families waited for assistance, according to a state agency report summarized in February 2026 [^10]. A later report from the Louisiana Policy Institute for Children put the statewide waiting list at nearly 8,000 children when it was released on October 13, 2026 [^9]. Eligibility reaches families earning up to 85% of state median income, which is $6,786 a month in gross income for a family of four, effective February 1, 2025 [^11]. The state also moved toward paying providers in advance rather than in arrears in 2025 [^32].

A waiting list cuts two ways for a lender. It proves unmet need, and it shows that subsidy money is rationed: a center that depends on CCAP children takes on the risk that vouchers do not arrive. A provider must hold a Type III license, the category for centers that receive public funds, to take CCAP children [^35]. The model assumes private-pay tuition and carries no subsidy upside; the lender should ask what share of enrolment the sponsor expects from vouchers and how many families on the waiting list live within the center's reach. The Ascension parish count of that waiting list was not found.

The Ascension Early Childhood Ready Start Network, the parish's quality and coordination body, counts 28 early learning centers, 18 school-based sites and one Head Start center in its 2026-2027 blueprint, and says the parish awarded seats and workforce scholarships through the state matching fund in 2024 [^12]. Those programs are small, but they show local government treating child care as infrastructure.

Most of what is known about them comes from directories that republish state licensing records, which carry no publication date for their capacities. For that reason this study prints no directory capacity, rate or review score. Four locations run by three operators could be tied to a page the operator itself publishes, and those are the rows below. The twelve that stay out of the table

The remaining centers include Little Lads and Lassies on LA 621, which the Ready Start Network profiles [^12], Kaleidoscope The Learning Center near Geismar, Dutchtown Child Development Center, three Adventureland Kids sites, Seven Little Blessings Learning Center on LA 44, La Petite Papillon Learning Center on LA 44, Grace Learning Center, Genesis Learning Center, Baby Your Baby Childcare and Tonya's Kids and Co. None of their own sites was available to the study, and no county assessor record was read for any of them. The directories give them different capacities, license types and addresses, and in two cases the same center appears with conflicting street numbers. The lender should require a field survey that confirms each center's current license, its licensed capacity, its posted tuition and whether it has a waiting list.

One quality signal exists in the public record. The Ready Start Network scores participating sites on a seven-point scale and publishes a profile for Little Lads, but the scores shown are for the 2020-21 and 2021-22 school years [^12], too old for a 2026 underwriting file, so the study prints none of them. The network's blueprint is the better evidence for the supply side: 28 early learning centers and 18 school-based sites in the 2026-2027 plan [^12]. That is the supply count a lender can trust, and it is a count of participants in a network, not of every licensed center.

What a recent sale says about price

The one price evidence in the file is a real estate sale. In April 2025 the 7,400 sq ft Dutchtown center changed hands for $860,000 as Golden Rule took over from the previous operator; the same building sold for $1.1 million in 2017 [^13]. That is a used building sold with its operating use, and it sold for less than the 2017 price. It is not a template for construction cost. Its use here is narrower: it shows that existing centers in the cluster trade at prices a new building cannot match, so a buyer deciding between acquiring a center and building one starts with a large cost gap. A purchase is a change of ownership, which the program tests under separate rules; this case does not price that route.

Pipeline

The research found no planned or permitted child care center in the trade area for 2025 and 2026. The Ascension Parish Planning Commission's April 8, 2026 agenda covered a consent agenda, family partitions, a flag-lot code and a high-impact development ordinance, with no child care item [^18], and its May 13, 2026 meeting approved three family-partition plats [^19]. Those summaries are third-party meeting digests and building permit records for child care use were not found, so the absence is a lender condition, not a conclusion.

The subject is a hypothetical parcel of about two acres on the LA 621 corridor in Gonzales, zoned for commercial use. Two things need confirming before a lender commits: whether the parcel sits inside the City of Gonzales or in unincorporated Ascension Parish, and what zoning district applies. The jurisdiction decides which approvals apply. The research did not establish either, and the model does not guess.

Zoning and approvals

For land outside the city, the Ascension Parish Unified Land Development Code governs, with zoning tables the parish adopted on February 18, 2021 [^20]. Whether a child care center is allowed by right or only by conditional use in the commercial districts was not found, and neither was a typical approval timeline. The state licensing steps follow the local ones. A center needs parish or city zoning approval and a certificate of occupancy, plus approval from the State Fire Marshal and the Office of Public Health, before the Louisiana Department of Education will license it [^33]. New or renovated space needs those approvals before children occupy it [^23]. The sequence matters to the loan, because a center earns nothing until the license issues, and the working-capital line in the budget pays for the wait.

License fees and classes

Louisiana charges child care centers an annual license fee by licensed capacity: $25 for 15 children or fewer, $100 for 16 to 50, $175 for 51 to 100 and $250 for 101 or more, with a $25 application fee, under the April 2025 version of Bulletin 137 [^22]. The fees are small; the license class is not. Type I centers take no public funds, Type II centers take federal food program money only, and Type III centers receive state or federal funds and are the only class that can accept CCAP children [^35]. The subject needs to decide its class before design, because a Type III center also needs academic approval.

Louisiana is an active 7(a) state. One input comes straight from a source: the SBA guaranty share. Everything else is an assumption, and each carries its reasoning in the table. The research gave the model benchmarks for wage and scale but no cost, ratio or occupancy figures for Gonzales, which is why so many lines are allowances.   1. Enrolment ramps slower than modeled

    The model assumes {{case:v.in_occ}} of capacity in Year 3. The research found no competitor occupancy and no waiting-list count for the parish, so the ramp is untested. A pre-enrolment list is the nearest thing to evidence.

  2. Subsidy dependence

    Nearly 8,000 Louisiana children were waiting for CCAP help in October 2026 [^9], and 5,371 families were waiting at the end of 2025 [^10]. A center that fills with voucher families inherits that program's pace. If the waiting list does not clear, the subsidy share of enrolment does not arrive.

  3. Labor cost and turnover

    Advertised assistant pay of $13.00 to $15.00 an hour [^17] competes with industrial employers hiring in the same corridor [^6,7,8]. Each dollar of hourly wage moves payroll in a business where payroll is most of the cost.

  4. The building costs more than assumed

    No bid exists. At the restructured equity share, a cost overrun lands mostly on the sponsor, and at the proposed share it lands on a loan that already fails.

  5. Licensing delay

    The center earns nothing until the state licenses it, and licensing follows zoning, fire marshal and public health approvals [^33]. A delay burns the working-capital reserve before the first child enrols.

  6. Flood and insurance

    The parcel's flood zone was not found. The parish is spending public money on flood protection and elevating a highway [^6], which is a reminder that water is a live issue for any site in the area.

  7. A new competitor opens first

    No center is in the pipeline in the sources reviewed [^18,19], but the cluster has operators with several locations, and a successful operator can add capacity faster than a first-time owner can build.

  8. Industrial projects slow or move

    The demand case rests partly on announced plants and expansions [^6,7,8]. Announcements are not employment, and a project can slip or change. The study uses only projects with a dated public source.
