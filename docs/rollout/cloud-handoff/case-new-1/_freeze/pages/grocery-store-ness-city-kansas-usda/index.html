Grocery Store Feasibility Study Case: A New Store in Ness City, Kansas, with a USDA B&I Loan

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published October 29, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Ness County counted 2,617 residents on July 1, 2025, down 2.4% since April 2020 [1], and a regional chain already runs the county's only full-line grocery in Ness City [11][23]. A second, newly built store of 6,000 square feet reaches $1,902,058 of sales in Year 3 but ($67,159) of net operating income, so at the modelled USDA B&I terms the model finds the loan unsupported, as proposed and as restructured.

Study at a Glance

A second grocery in a county of 2,617 people, tested against a USDA loan.

Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders, and this page shows what one looks like for a rural grocery. The subject is a hypothetical new store of 6,000 square feet on about 2 acres in Ness City, the seat of Ness County, Kansas, financed with a USDA Business and Industry guaranteed loan. If you are weighing a store like this one, you can contact Feasibility Study Company before you commit to land.

The market evidence below is real and dated, drawn from public sources. The site, the sponsor and the numbers in the model are hypothetical, and every model input that no source supports is labelled an assumption. For the wider picture, see our pages on grocery store feasibility, Kansas markets and the USDA Rural Development programs.

Market Conclusion

One full-line grocer, a shrinking county and a loan the sales cannot carry.

Ness County is the kind of market in which a grocery already exists and a second one has to take its customers. The county's population estimate was 2,617 on July 1, 2025, against 2,682 on the April 2020 base, a decline of 2.4% while Kansas as a whole grew 1.3% [1]. Nearly three in ten residents, 29.5%, are 65 or older [1]. A resident base like that buys groceries steadily and has little growth to hand to a new entrant.

The supply side is thin but not empty. The research found one full-line supermarket in the county, run by a family-owned regional chain that offers online ordering for its Ness City store [11], and dollar stores that accept food assistance benefits [12][23]. A directory of benefit-authorized retailers lists a handful of other food sellers in the county, mostly convenience stores and a meat shop [23]. No planned or under-construction food retail project turned up for 2025 or 2026, and the county reported zero residential building permits for 2025 [1].

What the research could not find matters as much. It found no retail building or pad that could be confirmed as available in Ness City, no zoning district for any site, no flood determination, no electric or gas utility, no store size or sales for any competitor and no lender with a B&I loan in the county. Each of those gaps becomes a condition at the end of this page, not a number.

The model turns the evidence into a sales estimate by taking the county's residents, a placeholder food-at-home spend per resident and a share that the new store captures. In Year 3, the first stable year, that gives sales of $1,902,058 and net operating income of ($67,159), a margin of -3.5%. The project costs $2,653,148, and the sponsor's proposed $1,989,861 loan carries annual debt service of $199,728. Coverage is -0.34x against a 1.25x floor. The engine could not find an equity share that fixes this, because the store loses money before it pays any debt service. The finding is a negative determination: the market as measured does not support this loan, and the page shows what would have to be different for it to do so.

Demand

A small, older, steady county built on health care, oil and farmland.

Residents and households. The 2020 Census counted 2,687 people in Ness County and 1,329 in Ness City, where 595 households lived [1][2]. The July 2025 estimate of 2,617 sits below that census count [1]. The five-year survey for 2020 to 2024 puts the county at 1,161 households averaging 2.23 people, and the Census Bureau's 2025 housing estimate is 1,542 units [1].

Age and income. The median age is 45.1 years in the five-year survey for 2024 [3], and 29.5% of residents are 65 or older [1]. Median household income is $68,616 and per capita income is $45,085 in the 2020 to 2024 survey [1]. Food is a larger share of an older household's budget than of a younger one, and these households buy it close to home. The mean commute is 15.6 minutes [1], which means most working residents live and shop in or near Ness City.

Language and background. Hispanic or Latino residents are 12.9% of the county in the 2025 estimate, and 14.2% of residents speak a language other than English at home [1]. The model gives that no extra revenue, because the research found no purchasing data to size it.

Jobs. Private employers in the county had 807 employees at 122 establishments in 2023, and employment fell 2.9% between 2022 and 2023 [1]. Kansas names Beutler Inc, Grisell Memorial Hospital and Ness County Hospital among its major employers [4]. The unemployment rate was 2.7% in 2024 [4]. Health care and social assistance took in $25,930,000 in 2022 [1], and the hospital district alone levied $3,046,449.62 in property tax for 2025, 23.13% of the county's ad valorem total [6]. These are employers whose staff buy lunch and groceries locally; they are also the reason a deli counter appears in the model.

The commodity base. Oil and gas property carried $23,251,523 of taxable value in 2025, 29.57% of the county's total, and agricultural land carried $16,549,938, or 21.05% [6]. Half the tax base therefore rises and falls with crude prices and crop returns. The risk to a grocer is indirect: income in a county like this follows those cycles, and a store with fixed payroll and debt service feels a downturn quickly.

Retail spending. The 2022 Economic Census recorded $12,031,000 of retail sales for the whole county, or $4,528 per resident [1]. That figure covers every store type, and it is small enough to say that the grocery market is also small. The model's food-at-home assumption sits above what that per-resident total suggests for food alone, because it counts the whole food budget and not only what stays in the county, and the inputs table says so.

Competitor Census

One full-line grocer and a ring of dollar stores and convenience stores.

The competitive set in Ness County is a full-line supermarket, national dollar-store formats, a specialty meat seller and convenience stores in Ness City and Ransom [23]. The research defines the trade area as Ness County, an administrative proxy and not a drive-time polygon, because the county is the smallest geography with both the 2025 estimates and the five-year survey in one primary table [1]. Only competitors with a web page of their own, confirmed by the research, appear in the table below.

Both rows come from the operator's or the brand's own pages. The Ness City Jamboree Foods store is one of several run by a family-owned regional chain in northwest Kansas, and the chain's own site lists the store and offers online ordering for it [11]. The Family Dollar store on West Sycamore Street lists food assistance benefits as accepted and sells refrigerated foods, canned goods and quick meals [12]. A Dollar General store on Eagle Drive appears in the benefit-retailer directory built on the federal SNAP database [23], but the chain's own store page refused an automated request when this study tried it, so the store is not a row in the table. The same applies to the meat shop, the convenience stores and a wholesale grocer whose operating status the research could not verify. They are named here and left unconfirmed.

What the table shows. The only competitor that sells a full grocery basket is Jamboree. Its operator is a chain, and a chain buys at a scale that one new store cannot match [11]. The dollar stores sell food in a narrow range and take the fill-in trip, which leaves the full weekly shop to the supermarket. A new store would have to win that weekly shop from Jamboree, because the dollar stores do not hold it to begin with.

What the table cannot show. The research could not retrieve the county assessor's parcel, tax or acreage record for any competitor, because the state's property records portal blocked automated access. It found no square footage, no opening year it could confirm and no posted price for any of them. The directories it did find disagree about the Jamboree store's history, and the building at that address was listed under a different grocery name at some point. This page treats the history as unverified and uses none of it. The size of the incumbent matters, since a large store in a county of 2,617 people [1] means either high share or low sales per square foot, and the assessor's records settle it. They head the lender conditions.

Dollar-store pressure. The dollar stores accept food assistance benefits [12][23], so they compete for the household that shops on benefits. The research did not size that group, and the model gives it no separate treatment.

Pipeline. No planned or under-construction grocery, dollar-store or other food-retail project was found in Ness County or in the nearby towns of Hays, WaKeeney, Dighton and La Crosse for 2025 or 2026. The research did not search planning-commission agendas in full, so this is a gap in the search and not proof that nothing is coming. With zero residential building permits reported for 2025 [1], the county shows no sign of growth-driven retail.

Site

A generic pad at the US 283 and K-96 junction, with most site facts still open.

The hypothetical site is about 2 acres of commercial land in Ness City, on the frontage of US 283 or K-96, which meet in town [5]. US 283 runs north to I-70 and US 40 at WaKeeney, and the research did not find the distance [28]. No particular parcel is assumed. The research looked for a retail building or pad listed in Ness City and could not confirm one, so the study uses no listing, parcel or owner, and the land figure is a round placeholder.

Zoning. No Ness City zoning map, district table or approval timeline was retrieved. Whether a grocery is permitted by right on the generic pad, needs a conditional use or needs a rezoning is therefore a lender condition, as are setbacks, parking ratios and signage rules. The commercial assessment ratio, by contrast, is a matter of state law: Kansas assesses commercial real property at a fixed share of its value under the state constitution, and vacant lots at a lower share [9]. The research prints no edition date for that text, so this page prints neither share as a market figure and the model carries the commercial ratio as an assumption.

Utilities. The city operates a public water system governed by Chapter XV of its code of ordinances, which includes cross-connection rules in section 15-231 [8]. The sewer provider was not confirmed. The electric and gas utilities were not found. Refrigeration makes electric service the largest utility question for a grocery, so will-serve letters and tap, connection and impact fees are lender conditions. The research found no fee schedule, and the model carries an allowance only.

Flood and insurance. No FEMA flood zone was determined for any site, and no wind, hail or flood insurance conditions were found. Both go on the list. The lender will want a bound quote that covers wind, hail and flood.

Traffic. The state's 2019 counts, reproduced on a highway reference site, show US 283 carrying 2,450 vehicles a day between the south city limits and K-96, 38.8% of them trucks, and 3,120 a day between K-96 and the north city limits, 10.9% trucks [5]. Outside town the road carries 1,580 vehicles a day from mile post 100 to Ness City and 1,430 from Ness City to mile post 116, with truck shares of 28.5% and 31.5% [5]. The research found no 2024 or 2025 count and no count-station identifier. The high truck share means that much of the through traffic is freight. There is no evidence that it stops for groceries, and the model gives it no revenue.

Property tax. The tax line is the best-documented cost in the model. The county's 2025 average mill levy was 167.502 mills on $78,626,942 of taxable value, producing $13,169,019 of ad valorem tax [6]. Commercial real property made up $8,167,824 of that value, 10.39% of the county's total [6]. The levy for the Ness City tax unit specifically was not found; the Department of Administration publishes county levy sheets that give it [24], and the lender should pull the unit's levy. The school district's own levies for 2025 were 20.000 mills general and 18.224 mills supplemental general [7]. The model applies the county average to land, building and site work at cost, a cautious basis, since the appraiser's value will not equal cost.

Sales tax. Kansas stopped charging its state sales tax on food and food ingredients on January 1, 2025, while the full local sales tax on food still applies [10]. The research found conflicting reports on whether Ness City's local rate is a city or a county levy, and it recommends confirming the rate against the Department of Revenue's published rate list, so this page prints none. It matters to a grocer because a shopper compares shelf prices to the incumbent's after tax, and the local rate applies equally to both.

Revenue is $1,181,836 in Year 1, $1,574,272 in Year 2 and $1,902,058 in Year 3, the first stable year. The build has four steps: the county's residents, the food-at-home spending each resident directs to stores, the share the new store captures, and a split between the grocery aisles and the deli. It is a demand build, not a sales-per-square-foot build, because the research holds no sales or size for any Ness County competitor.

Residents and trend. The model starts from 2,617 residents [1] and lets the number change by -0.5% a year, the pace of the 2020 to 2025 decline [1]. Year 1 is three years after the 2025 estimate, so the model allows time for approvals and a build.

Food spending per resident. The model carries $2,700 a resident and a price growth of 2.0% a year. No source in the research gives a Ness County food-at-home figure, so this is the least supported demand input and the first thing a household survey should replace. It may overstate what stays in the county, since part of a household's food budget goes to stores in larger towns.

Share of spending. The store captures 25.0% of the county's food spending by Year 3, rising from a lower share in Years 1 and 2. That means roughly one dollar in four, in a county where the incumbent has the customers, the chain's buying power and online ordering [11]. The research holds no market-share evidence. The share is an assumption that the break-even and sensitivity sections test.

Deli. Prepared food is 8.0% of sales, at a higher margin than the aisles, for school, hospital and oilfield lunch trade. The research found no sales split, and a lender will ask for the sponsor's deli plan before crediting the higher margin.

Margins. The model uses a gross margin of 24.0% on grocery, meat, produce and dairy and 50.0% on deli. A single independent store buying through a wholesaler has less purchasing scale than a regional chain, so the grocery margin is a middle-of-the-road figure and the wholesaler's cost schedule should replace it. The sensitivity grid moves it up and down by a fifth.

Payroll starts at $270,000 and grows 3.0% a year. It pays about eight full-time equivalents, enough to staff a manager, a meat cutter, deli, cashiers and stocking for evening and Sunday hours. The county's unemployment rate of 2.7% [4] suggests a tight labor pool. Utilities start at $60,000 because refrigeration never stops. Card and EBT processing, supplies and repairs and advertising and administration are shares of sales. Insurance starts at $30,000 and is a placeholder; the research found no insurance terms, and a bound quote must replace it.

Property tax applies an assumed assessment ratio of 25.0% [9] and the county average levy of 167.502 mills [6] to land, building and site work at cost, and grows 2.0% a year. It is one of the larger lines in the table. The USDA annual retention fee of 0.55% is shown as a pass-through cost on the guaranteed balance, which the model lowers each year to approximate amortization [15]. The terms sheet does not print the base, so the lender must confirm it. The replacement reserve of $15,000 a year keeps compressors and cases from becoming new debt.

Total project cost is $2,653,148, or $442 per square foot of store. The hard-cost inputs are placeholders: $210 per square foot for a steel retail building, $500,000 for refrigeration, fixtures and checkout, and $175,000 of site work. The research found no Ness County construction cost index and no bid, and the contractor's price and the vendors' quotes are lender conditions.

Land is carried at $20,000 an acre for about 2 acres. The research held no closed land sale or assessed land value for Ness City, and it could not confirm any retail listing, so the study uses no asking price from anywhere. An appraisal with closed sales replaces the placeholder. Land is a small share of the budget, so a miss on land moves the answer less than a miss on sales.

The USDA guarantee fee of 3.5% is added to project cost and shown as a use. The budget also includes $200,000 of opening inventory, about three weeks of cost of goods, and $125,000 of pre-opening payroll and working capital. Whether inventory and working capital may sit inside a guaranteed B&I loan, and what lien a wholesaler takes on inventory, are lender conditions.

Refrigeration and fixtures are about a third of the hard cost and wear out well before the building, so the model blends to 20 years..

The guarantee. For fiscal 2026 the Business and Industry program guarantees 85% of a loan under $5,000,000 and 80% of a loan from $5,000,000 to $25,000,000, with an initial fee of 3.0%, an annual retention fee of 0.55% and a 0.50% fee for a guarantee issued before construction is complete [15]. A reduced-fee category charges 1.0% and 0.50% but guarantees only 80% [15]. The 85% tier replaced 80% in a notice published on March 9, 2026, effective October 1, 2025, and USDA announced it on March 27 [15][19]. The loan of $1,989,861 is well inside the 85% tier.

Eligibility. The terms sheet behind this page prints no eligible or ineligible purposes for grocery stores. The only exclusion it records is wind and solar projects, from August 2025 [27]. The research took the program as the fit for a privately owned grocery and Community Facilities as the route for a public body or nonprofit [13][25]. The project site must lie in a rural area, meaning outside a city or town of more than 50,000 people, with the site and not the headquarters governing [19]. Ness City counted 1,329 people in the 2020 Census [2], well under the line. A city under 25,000 earns 5 priority points under the scoring rule [16]. The research did not retrieve USDA's eligibility map result for any Ness City parcel, so a determination for the actual site is a lender condition.

Rate and term. The lender and borrower negotiate the rate, fixed or variable, and the term runs to the useful life of the project or collateral, up to 40 years or the state-statute limit, whichever is less [14][17]. The model carries 8.00%, which is Prime of 7.00% on October 9, 2026 [26] plus 1.00 point for a start-up with no operating history, and it amortizes over 20 years because refrigeration and fixtures wear out well before a building does.

What the terms sheet leaves open. The USDA terms in the research print no equity minimum and no coverage ratio for B&I, and no separate maximum term for real estate or equipment. The study therefore tests two lender-style parameters and labels both as assumptions: 25.0% equity and a 1.25x coverage floor in Year 3. Neither is a USDA requirement. The regulation asks for projections through at least two years at full capacity or stable operations [16], which is why Year 3, the first stable year, is the test year. The lender's credit evaluation must include a written evaluation of the feasibility study [18].

The feasibility study. A guaranteed loan of more than $1,000,000 to a new business requires a study prepared by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to USDA [16]. The loan of $1,989,861 is over that line, and for a loan of $1,000,000 or less USDA may still require one if the lender's analysis is insufficient [16]. This page shows the market and financial sections that such a study contains; USDA sets the scope case by case.

The program's own risks. The fiscal 2027 budget request proposes no Business and Industry program level, and the fiscal 2025 program obligated a record $2,111,702,000 [20]. In May 2026 USDA revoked the approved-lender status of ten lenders whose portfolios held about $620 million in delinquent loans, roughly 47% of Rural Development's delinquent loans, and said more than 750 lenders remained [21]. A trade-association study found that B&I loans in Opportunity Zones rose 59.29% between 2018 and 2025, and that the number of loans fell from 309 in fiscal 2015 to 176 in fiscal 2024 while the average loan rose from $2.75 million to $8.1 million [22]. A grocery loan of this size sits far below that average. The research found no B&I lender or loan in Ness County for 2024 to 2026, and Kansas applications are taken on a rolling basis [14]. Finding a lender that is both approved and willing to make a loan this small is a task of the engagement, not a given.

Risks

What could break the case, beyond the finding itself.

  1. An unmeasured sales base

    No source gives Ness County's food-at-home spending, the incumbent's sales or its size. The model's demand build is a placeholder, and the determination moves with it. The household survey is not optional.

  2. A shrinking county

    The population fell 2.4% from April 2020 to July 2025, and zero residential building permits were reported for 2025 [1]. Every year of decline lowers the food budget that the store competes for.

  3. A chain incumbent

    The full-line competitor belongs to a regional chain with online ordering [11]. It can match prices or hours in response to a new store, and a single independent cannot.

  4. Commodity exposure

    Oil and gas supply 29.57% of the county's taxable value and farmland 21.05% [6]. A downturn in either cuts incomes, and a store with fixed payroll and debt service feels it at once.

  5. Tax on cost

    The model taxes land and improvements at cost at the county average levy of 167.502 mills [6]. An appraised value below cost or a lower unit levy would help; a higher one would hurt.

  6. Construction cost and equipment

    The research found no local cost index or equipment quote. Refrigeration is a large share of a grocery budget, and an overrun comes straight out of loan capacity.

  7. Program funding and lender base

    The fiscal 2027 request proposes no B&I program level [20], and ten lenders lost approved status in May 2026 [21]. A delayed appropriation or a lender exit can stall the guarantee after land is bought.

  8. Rural eligibility and labor

    The eligibility map result for the actual parcel was not retrieved, and the county's 2.7% unemployment rate in 2024 [4] makes staffing a long evening schedule harder.

Lender Conditions

Open items to close before underwriting.

Every item the research could not find becomes a condition. They are listed in the order a lender would want them closed.

  1. Site. A retail building or pad that is actually available in Ness City, with an appraisal using closed sales, a survey and a title commitment. The research confirmed no listing.
  2. Zoning and approvals. The zoning district, whether a grocery is permitted by right, the approval type and timeline, setbacks and parking, from the city's zoning map and ordinance.
  3. Competitor records. Store size, parcel number, acreage, assessed value and tax for the incumbent and the dollar stores from the county appraiser's records, plus a drive-by survey and a sample of posted prices on the same day.
  4. Food spending. A household survey or card-spend study of where Ness County residents buy food and how much leaves the county.
  5. Pipeline. A review of city and county meeting agendas and the permit log for new food retail in Ness City and the nearby towns for 2025 and 2026.
  6. Rural eligibility. USDA's eligibility determination for the exact parcel, printed and placed in the file, and the status of any Opportunity Zone or priority-point criteria.
  7. Utilities. Will-serve letters for water, sewer, electric and gas, electrical load for refrigeration, and tap, connection and impact fees under Chapter XV and the providers' schedules [8].
  8. Flood, traffic and access. A FEMA flood zone determination, a current state traffic count for the frontage and the driveway permit, since the only counts found date from 2019 [5].
  9. Construction and equipment. Contractor bids for the building and site work, vendor quotes for refrigeration and fixtures, and a wholesaler's cost schedule and lien terms.
  10. Taxes. The combined levy for the Ness City tax unit from the county levy sheets, the appraiser's expected value, and the local sales tax rate on food confirmed against the state's rate list [24][10].
  11. Insurance. A bound quote for property, spoilage and liability that includes wind, hail and flood cover.
  12. Licenses. Kansas retail sales tax registration and food establishment licensing, neither of which the research retrieved.
  13. Lender and program. A USDA-approved lender with Business and Industry experience that will make a loan of this size, its credit memo on equity and coverage, and the independent feasibility study that USDA requires above $1,000,000 for a new business [16].
  14. Alternative structures. A decision on whether to price a purchase or lease of an existing building, or a community-ownership route under Community Facilities [25].
Ness City Grocery Questions

Questions lenders ask about this Ness City grocery case.

Is a grocery store eligible for a USDA Business and Industry loan?

The USDA terms sheet behind this page prints no eligible or ineligible purposes for grocery stores, and the only B&I exclusion it records is wind and solar projects, from August 2025 [27]. The research read the program as the fit for a privately owned grocery, with Community Facilities reserved for public bodies and nonprofits [13][25]. The site must also lie in a rural area, meaning outside a city or town of more than 50,000 people [19]. The 2020 Census counted 1,329 people in Ness City [2]. A lender confirms eligibility; this page does not.

Does USDA require a feasibility study for this project?

For a guaranteed loan of more than $1,000,000 to a new business, 7 CFR 5001.306 requires a study prepared by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to the Agency [16]. For $1,000,000 or less, USDA may still ask for one if the lender's analysis is insufficient [16]. The lender's credit evaluation must include a written evaluation of the study [18].

Why does the model find that this loan does not work?

The county is small and shrinking, and the incumbent already serves it. The county's 2,617 residents spend a limited amount on food at home, a new store captures a share of that, and the gross profit on that share does not cover payroll, a 167.502-mill property tax levy and debt service on a newly built store [1][6]. The page shows the break-even share and the sensitivity grid so a reader can see how far the inputs would have to move.

Would buying the existing store or reusing a building change the answer?

Possibly, but it is a different case. An acquisition has a seller's history and a building that is already paid for in part, and a conversion carries a lower build cost. This page tests only a new building. Both alternatives would need their own study and would bring their own questions about price and records.

Could a community-owned store use a different USDA program?

The research notes that Community Facilities guaranteed loans serve public bodies and nonprofits, and that the program carried an 80% guarantee for loans approved in fiscal 2026 [15][25]. A municipality or nonprofit owner could be evaluated there instead of under B&I. That is a different ownership model with different governance questions, and this page does not model it.

What would a full study add to this worked case?

It would put the model on a real parcel, with drive-time demographics, a store-level read of the incumbent's size and sales, a will-serve letter from each utility, a contractor's bid, a wholesaler's cost schedule and a household survey of where Ness County residents buy food. Those replace the placeholders in the inputs table, and the answer could move in either direction.
