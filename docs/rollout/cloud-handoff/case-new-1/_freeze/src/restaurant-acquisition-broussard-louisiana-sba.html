{{case:glance}}

Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility and market studies for owners, developers and their lenders, and this page shows the method on a restaurant purchase. Request a methodology briefing if you are weighing a similar deal. The case belongs to our restaurant and foodservice guide, sits in Louisiana and follows the lending rules in our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice area.

Broussard is the rare Louisiana town where the demand case is not the hard part. The city counted 13,447 residents at the April 2020 estimates base and 15,799 at July 1, 2025, a gain of 17.5% while the state shrank by 0.9% [^1,2]. Its households earn a median $116,179, nearly twice the state's $60,756 [^1,2]. Eighty-three percent of homes are owner-occupied, and the median home is valued at $310,200 [^1]. Households like these are the core of a dining room's trade, and the 2022 Economic Census recorded $65,405,000 of accommodation and food services sales inside the city limits [^1].

What the town does not offer is empty ground. Five full-service restaurants are confirmed on their own pages, from old-line Creole and steakhouse rooms to a neighborhood bar and kitchen, and two more names appear in a September 2026 Tripadvisor roundup but stay unconfirmed [^6,8,9,10,11,27]. The pipeline holds a Cajun-Asian seafood house with a $1.45 million building permit on Ambassador Caffery Parkway [^12,13]. A buyer of an existing dining room is therefore betting that a known address, a trained kitchen and a liquor license are worth more than the newer rooms' fresh paint.

Here is the credit question. The buyer pays {{case:v.in_price}} for the land, the building, the kitchen and the going business, a figure tied to the seller's illustrative earnings, and wants an SBA 7(a) loan with only 10% down. Because the deal is an initial acquisition, SOP 50 10 8.1 grades it on what the seller already earned, with a floor of 1.25x. Nobody has published statements for a Broussard restaurant, so the page builds a two-year seller history out of the model's own economics and marks it illustrative every time it appears. Against an illustrative FY2025 EBITDA of {{case:v.hist_ebitda}}, the minimum-injection request is covered {{case:v.hist_dscr_proposed}}, short of the floor.

Raising the buyer's cash is the cure the engine finds: {{case:v.equity_pct}} down leaves {{case:v.loan_total}} of debt and brings the same year to {{case:v.hist_dscr}}. A pass on that test is not the end of the analysis, since the test looks only at the past and the parkway is changing. King's Seafood broke ground in late March 2026 on the same road [^12], and none of its trade is in the seller's FY2025. Our first-year projection absorbs it and covers the restructured loan {{case:v.dscr_y1}}: clear of SBA's general 7(a) standard of 1.15x, short of the acquisition floor.

Our reading: Broussard can support an operating dining room, and it can support this purchase once the buyer puts in the restructured equity, but only if 2026 interim statements show the guest count held through the end of the interchange work and the new neighbor's arrival. A steeper slide than the one projected should lower the price before it raises the equity a second time. Readers comparing deal types can set this case beside the Opelika gas station purchase, which runs the same rule on a different business.

Three readings matter for a dining room. First, growth is recent and large, and it is a family kind of growth: 28.4% of residents are under 18 against 22.7% statewide, and households average 2.81 persons against 2.49 [^1,2]. Family tables tend to mean larger parties and earlier dinners, which suits a Cajun menu better than a bar-led format. Second, incomes are high. A median household income of $116,179 and per capita income of $48,339 put the typical Broussard household well above Louisiana's [^1,2], and the labor force participation rate of 77.1% against 58.8% points to earned income, which fits DOTD's 2010 note that residents commute to Lafayette [^1,2,3]. Third, Broussard sells more at retail per resident than the state does, $27,416 against $19,351 in 2022 [^1,2], which shows spending staying in town.

The same table carries the main caution. Broussard is not a retirement or tourist market: only 9.7% of residents are 65 or over [^1], so the weekday senior lunch trade that carries many small-town dining rooms is thin. Sales depend on working households, which makes the weekday dinner and the weekend the restaurant's real business. The city's own economic development office publishes a higher population estimate than the Census does; the case uses the Census figure and treats the gap as a reason to verify local demand rather than assume it.

The register lines above place the city inside a parish that already runs thick with restaurants: the parish carries about a third more restaurants per resident than Louisiana as a whole. That density helps a buyer because the parish eats out, and it hurts because every new room is competing for the same table. Parish incomes sit well below Broussard's own, so the city is a high-income pocket inside a parish whose typical household earns much less.

The access evidence is mixed and dated. The last Louisiana DOTD counts the research found are from 2010: 40,247 vehicles a day on US 90 south of Broussard and 47,492 on Ambassador Caffery Parkway (LA 3073) [^3]. DOTD described US 90 that year as the road residents use to commute from Broussard to Lafayette [^3]. Current counts with station identifiers were not found and are a lender condition below. Since then DOTD has been rebuilding the US 90 and Ambassador Caffery interchange, a $136.52 million project whose February 21, 2024 release estimated completion by spring 2026 [^4]. Access to a restaurant on the parkway was disrupted in the meantime, including an overnight closure of LA 3073 between the US 90 lanes in April 2025 [^5]. Whether the finished interchange helps or hurts a given site depends on where its entrance sits relative to the new ramps, which the study treats as a site question and not a corridor-wide gain.

Two demand drivers the research could not size are crawfish season and festival weekends. Both are real in kind for Acadiana restaurants, but no source quantified them for Broussard, so the model carries no seasonal premium and a lender condition asks for monthly sales from the seller.

The census shows a lunch tier, a dinner tier and one pattern. The lunch-plate tier at Prejean's sits below the dinner entrees at LT's, and the dinner tier is where the upscale rooms compete. Nash's holds the fine-dining position, and the family-style steakhouses fill the middle. A buyer needs to know which of these positions the target occupies, because a mid-priced Cajun room competes with several of them at once.

The pipeline matters more than the census. King's Seafood, a Cajun-Asian seafood restaurant that is a third unit after New Iberia and Abbeville, began site work in late March 2026 at the corner of Ambassador Caffery Parkway and South Bernard Road, on a site once announced for a New Orleans concept that was never built [^12]. The building permit is $1.45 million [^13]. No opening date is confirmed. A limited-service wave follows: Fish Box Sushi signed a lease in a new 12,000 square foot center with seven restaurant, coffee and quick-service spaces [^14], and a Tropical Smoothie Cafe is coming to Albertson Parkway in a former Taco Bell building [^15]. Those are quick-service formats and the research names no operating quick-service restaurant inside the city, so they are cited here as pipeline and the case draws no quick-service conclusions from them.

Prejean's arrival shows both sides of the same fact. A new full-service name found room in Broussard, so entry is possible. The same arrival took tables from the rooms already there, which is exactly the kind of change a seller's two-year history may not show. A buyer should ask what happened to the target's sales in the twelve months after a new full-service competitor opened, and again in the year King's opens [^6,12].

Picture an independent full-service Cajun and seafood restaurant, owned with its building, somewhere along the Ambassador Caffery and US 90 corridor inside Broussard. The model assumes roughly four and a half thousand square feet, about one and a half acres and about 110 seats. Those round numbers are placeholders and describe no actual restaurant, and the address, parcel, parish and menu concept are left open on purpose. With an existing business the live question is not whether the use could be permitted but whether it keeps its permissions when the owner changes.

Zoning

The City of Broussard publishes its zoning ordinance through its permitting and licensing page [^16]. No district was found for any restaurant parcel, and no by-right or conditional status. A zoning verification letter that names the district, confirms the restaurant and bar use, and states rebuild rights after a casualty is a lender condition. A restaurant that serves alcohol may also depend on separation rules from schools and churches that the research did not retrieve.

Utilities and taxes

  • Water, sewer, electric and gas: no provider was confirmed for any parcel on the corridor, and impact or connection fees were not found. An operating restaurant already holds its connections; the question matters if the buyer expands the kitchen.
  • Sales tax: the rate depends on the parcel. The table lists 9.50% inside Lafayette Parish, made of the 5.00% state rate, 2.00% for the parish school board and 2.50% for the city [^17]. Parcels inside the Ambassador Caffery Extension economic development subdistrict pay 10.50%, because it adds 1.00%, and the part of Broussard in St. Martin Parish pays 11.00%, all effective October 1, 2025 [^17]. The state rate rose from 4.45% to 5% on January 1, 2025 [^18]. Sales tax is collected from guests and is not a restaurant expense, but it is part of the check a guest compares with other rooms.
  • Corporate income tax: Louisiana moved to a flat 5.5% rate for periods beginning on or after January 1, 2025 [^19]. The model stops at EBITDA, so the rate does not enter the coverage test.
  • Property tax: the Lafayette Parish Assessor's 2025 report shows Broussard with no municipal general alimony millage, against 3.6 mills in Youngsville, 4.66 in Carencro and 4.32 in Scott [^20]. The parish and school levies that apply to the parcel and the assessment ratio were not found. The model carries a placeholder of {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of the real estate allocation, and the tax bill is a lender condition. For scale, the parish collected $288,314,896.36 in property tax for tax year 2025 [^20].

Flood, wind and environment

The flood zone is undetermined and so are the site's wind and flood insurance costs. DOTD notes that the US 90 and Ambassador Caffery corridor carries hurricane evacuation traffic [^3], which is a reminder that Acadiana restaurants are underwritten for storm closures. The Phase I environmental screening is smaller for a restaurant than for a fuel site but not optional: grease interceptors, any former on-site fuel tank and the age of the building's systems are all diligence items.

{{case:revenue}}

Guests. The model carries {{case:v.in_guests}} guests in Year 3 for a room of about 110 seats; the inputs table shows the daily load behind that, under two turns of the dining room on an average day, which is a plausible load for a full-service Cajun restaurant that does lunch and dinner. Year 1 sits a little below the illustrative FY2025 volume because King's Seafood is expected to open during the year [^12], and growth after that is slight. The city grew far faster than that [^1], but a table-service restaurant cannot grow faster than its seats and its hours, so the model lets price do most of the work.

The check. Food sales are {{case:v.in_food_check}} per guest and beverage and bar sales {{case:v.in_bev_check}} in Year 1, growing {{case:v.in_check_growth}} a year. The food figure is a blend of lunch and dinner. The competitors' own menus show a lower lunch tier and a higher dinner tier [^7,8], and the model places the check between them. The beverage figure averages every guest, including those who order water, and assumes the restaurant holds a standard liquor license that transfers with the business.

What is left out. Catering, takeout, a second dining room and delivery platforms are not modeled, though most Acadiana restaurants run at least one of them. The research did not evidence their volume for any Broussard restaurant, and a lender should not underwrite income it cannot trace. If the seller's records show catering or private events, they belong in the history, and the model's guests and check would need to be adjusted to match.

How the price is split decides three things at once. The model puts {{case:v.in_re_alloc}} of the {{case:v.in_price}} on land and building and the remainder on kitchen equipment, fixtures, licenses and goodwill. Nothing in the research prices land or construction in Broussard, so the split is a round placeholder for the appraiser to replace. Every dollar moved out of real estate and into goodwill amortizes over the shorter business-portion term and not the longer real estate term, which raises the payment.

  • Business Purchase Price. In SBA's words it is the purchase-agreement price less the appraised value of owner-occupied commercial real estate. On this allocation that figure exceeds $350,000, the ceiling for a lender valuing the business in-house, so an outside valuation is part of the file.

  • Valuation cost as equity. The out-of-pocket cost of the business valuation and QoE counts toward the injection, so a share of the closing line is equity already paid.

Smaller lines follow. Inventory of {{case:v.in_inventory}} is counted at closing, the working capital reserve is {{case:v.in_working_capital}}, and {{case:v.in_closing}} covers the appraisal, valuation, screening and legal work plus the move of the liquor, health and occupational licenses to the buyer. A debt ceiling applies as well: total acquisition debt, including seller debt not on full standby, is capped at the business valuation; any excess must be equity. The restructured {{case:v.loan_total}} funds part of the {{case:v.in_price}} price together with inventory, working capital, closing costs and the fee, and the buyer's equity covers the rest. Several buyer-side rules apply too. The injection is 10% of project cost at minimum. A standby seller note, other standby debt and minority investors together may fund no more than half of it. The rule in Appendix 15 reads: the initial-acquisition floor is 1.25x on historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two. As proposed. The made-up seller earned {{case:v.in_seller_ebitda_fy2024}} in FY2024 and {{case:v.in_seller_ebitda_fy2025}} in FY2025; the page flags both as invented. On the restructured loan, Year 3 NOI only just covers debt service once guests drop to {{case:v.breakeven_value}}, which is {{case:v.cushion_pct}} under plan. A dining room staffs to its opening hours, not to its covers, so that margin of safety is slim. The real cushion is thinner than the table. {{case:sensitivity}}

Coverage in Year 3 ranges from {{case:v.sens_min}} to {{case:v.sens_max}} across the grid, and the negative corner is a year in which NOI itself turns negative. The drivers are chosen because they are where a single dining room on this corridor is exposed. Guests carry the competitive risk: King's Seafood, the limited-service wave and every other room in town fish in the same pool of households [^12,14]. Food cost carries the commodity risk: a seafood menu is priced off shrimp, crab and fish whose market can move faster than a printed menu.

The useful reading is the diagonal. A restaurant that loses a tenth of its guests while its food cost rises by one grid step at the same time, which is what a bad shrimp season and a new competitor look like together, falls well below the level where NOI covers debt service on the restructured loan. A restaurant that holds volume and cost clears the floor with room. The lender's real exposure is a combination, and the lender should test both at once.
