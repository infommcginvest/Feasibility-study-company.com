In Wyoming, Feasibility Study Company prepares independent studies of whether a project will work. The client is the owner or developer behind the project, and the reader is whoever will lend on it: a bank making an SBA 7(a) or 504 loan or seeking a USDA Rural Development guarantee, the sponsor of an EB-5 raise, or a conventional credit committee. Wyoming had 588.753 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 in the Census estimate FRED carries, against 586.722 thousand a year earlier [^16], and the public record a Wyoming feasibility study starts from pulls in three directions at once. This page sets out each one with its publisher and its date.

The first direction is southeast. Laramie County, which holds Cheyenne, drew the largest net migration of any county in the state in the Census year to July 2025 [^2], and the county's economic development group counted 10 locations with operating data centers there in June 2026 [^1]. A gas-powered data center campus, a large wind project and a rodeo headquarters move are all on the county's books, and the county has added a workforce housing requirement for large industrial projects, as the Cheyenne section below describes.

The second direction is down. Wyoming's gas fields produced less in 2025 than in 2024, the Workforce Services county files show covered jobs falling in Campbell and Sweetwater counties, and NAHB's reading of the Bureau of Economic Analysis release counted Wyoming among six states whose real output fell in the second quarter of 2026, with mining the leading drag [^17]. A project whose customers are crews, mine payrolls or the households that depend on them is underwritten against that record, not against a statewide average.

The third is the summer. Yellowstone and Grand Teton bring millions of visits through northwest Wyoming each year, and the state's own tourism figures put overnight visitors at 8.8 million in 2025 [^6]. Hotels, campgrounds, RV parks, restaurants and fuel stops in the park gateway towns live on that season, and a study for one of them shows the winter months as well as July.

The page follows that order: payrolls, then minerals, then visitors, then the tax and rule questions that cut across all three, then seven county markets from Cheyenne to Laramie, and the lending programs last. No public source found for this page gives a statewide count of hotel rooms, apartment vacancy, licensed care beds or commercial rents, so where a market section says a figure was not found, the study collects it for the site.

BLS's Wyoming summary for September 2026, a preliminary month, counts 295.5 thousand nonfarm jobs on a seasonally adjusted basis, a little more than a year before, and 276.4 thousand employed residents [^7]. Government accounts for 71.3 thousand jobs, trade, transportation and utilities for 52.4 thousand and education and health services for 29.7 thousand [^7]. BLS prints a twelve-month decline for the first two of those and a small gain for the third. For a study the sector lines matter more than the total: with public payrolls of that size, a county seat, a university town or a capital can carry a market that has little private industry, and a cut in public jobs reaches the stores and apartments that live on those salaries.

Workforce Services counts the same jobs without seasonal adjustment. Its August 2025 release put nonfarm employment at 302,100 [^18], and its June 2026 figure, as Wyoming Public Media reported on July 30, 2026, was 304,100, up from June 2025 [^19]. The adjusted BLS series and the unadjusted state series are two views of one payroll, and a demand model quotes one of them with its label rather than setting a summer month against an adjusted one.

Openings, closings and the fourth quarter of 2025

BLS's Business Employment Dynamics release of July 31, 2026 looks under the payroll total. In the three months to December 2025, Wyoming's private establishments added 19,197 jobs through openings and expansions and lost 17,023 through closings and contractions, for a net gain of +2,174, after a net loss of -1,362 the quarter before [^20]. Establishments that opened in the quarter accounted for 4,143 of the jobs gained, and establishments that closed for 2,668 of those lost [^20]. The release publishes five industry sectors: leisure and hospitality netted 858 jobs, professional and business services a net gain of 157 jobs, and retail trade a net loss of 63 jobs [^20].

The churn behind those nets is the point for an operator's budget. Gross gains and losses of that size against the payroll counted above mean that a new hotel, store or clinic hires out of a labor pool where openings and closings run constantly, and a staffing plan states how it will fill each position in a quarter when other employers are hiring as well.

Covered employment by county, 2024 and early 2025

The Department of Workforce Services' quarterly Employment and Payroll releases are the county view. From the fourth quarter of 2023 to the fourth quarter of 2024, covered employment statewide rose by +1,805 jobs and covered payroll by +$194.8 million [^15]. From the first quarter of 2024 to the first quarter of 2025 the statewide gain was +717 jobs, with employment rising in 11 counties and falling in 12 [^14]. These are the latest county releases found; Workforce Services' 2026 quarterly files were not located, so a study cites each figure with its two quarters and does not present it as current. The county lines appear in the market sections: gains in Laramie and Sheridan counties, losses in Campbell and Sweetwater.

The index and the output series tell the same story from two sides: a state economy that is holding level while its mining base gives ground.

EIA's annual table for Wyoming, released on September 30, 2026, counts in millions of cubic feet. It gives gross natural gas withdrawals of 1,197,527 for 2025 and 1,343,290 for 2024, and marketed production of 938,131 for 2025 against 1,052,320 for 2024 [^3]. Property tax on mineral production is a large part of what Wyoming collects, as the tax section shows, so a smaller gas year reaches county budgets as well as the demand for crew lodging, fuel and equipment service in the gas basins.

The state's sales and use tax figures show the same turn. Economic Analysis Division figures, as K2 Radio reported them on November 4, 2025, had mining sales and use tax collections, which include oil and gas extraction, lower in fiscal 2025 than the year before [^4], and the News Letter Journal's account of the May 2026 indicators report had mining collections in March 2026 down 12.3% from March 2025 [^22]. Sales tax on mining purchases is a reading of field activity, and it was still falling in early 2026.

Coal carries dates of its own. WyoFile reported on October 23, 2026 that the Kemmerer mine in Lincoln County is set to close at the end of 2029, and that the southwest Wyoming soda ash plants that burn its coal are looking for another fuel [^24]. In the Powder River Basin, the federal government approved a mining plan change at the Antelope Mine in August 2025 that adds 14.5 million tons of coal and extends its life by about half a year into 2037, as Wyoming Public Media reported [^25]. A lender reading a project in Campbell, Converse, Lincoln or Sweetwater County will want to know which mine or plant its customers depend on and how long that operation is scheduled to run.

New energy projects are announced in the same counties. The Wyoming Energy Authority reported in December 2025 that the Governor approved Large Project Energy Matching Funds for BWXT's planned nuclear fuel fabrication facility in Gillette [^26]. In Fremont County, a data center at Lost Cabin is the first phase of a plan put at $66 billion, with on-site gas generation that does not feed the grid, as The Ranger reported through WyoFile on October 7, 2026 [^27]. And on October 26, 2026 the state signed a memorandum with the federal government meant to shorten federal permitting for energy and industrial projects, according to WyoFile [^28]. An announced project is evidence of intent, not of payroll; a study counts the jobs once construction has a date and a contractor.

The range is the other base. The NASS state overview counts 1,160,000 cattle and calves in Wyoming on January 1, 2026, of them 621,000 beef cows [^29]. Feed stores, livestock auction barns, veterinary practices and the main-street businesses of ranch counties draw on that herd, and a study for one of them takes the inventory for its own county from the same NASS series rather than the state total.

The Wyoming Office of Tourism's figures for 2025, as the News Letter Journal relayed them on April 24, 2026, count 8.8 million overnight visitors, $291.8 million of state and local taxes generated by visitor spending and $1.47 billion of earnings for travel industry workers [^6]. The same report says 47% of overnight visitors stayed in hotels, motels or short-term vacation rentals [^6]; it does not say where the others stayed, so a campground or RV park study cannot read its own share from it. For 2024 Sheridan Media reported, from the same office, 8.7 million visitors to Wyoming [^30]. The 2024 figure counts visitors and the 2025 figure counts overnight visitors, so the two are not a growth series and a study does not read one against the other.

Lodging tax is the nearest thing to a statewide room count. Wyoming collected $67.4 million of statewide lodging tax in fiscal 2025, as K2 Radio reported Economic Analysis Division figures on November 4, 2025 [^4]. The statewide tax applies to hotels, RV sites, rentals and other short stays, with part of it going to the Office of Tourism and part staying in the county, and Wyoming Public Media reported in August 2025 that only a few of the state's counties did not add a local lodging tax on top [^31]. A lodging study therefore states both the state rate and the local rate, if any, for the county where the property stands, and it uses the county's own collections as its demand check.

Wyoming's property tax is the cost line with a vote pending. Proposition 1, a citizen initiative that reached the ballot with 30,251 petition signatures, would halve the residential property tax on qualifying home structures; the Legislative Service Office projected an initial revenue loss of $109.4 million, as Cowboy State Daily reported on October 28, 2026 [^8]. The same report says the Legislature had already enacted an annual cap on property tax growth and a cut on taxes billed on the first part of a home's value [^8]. The vote falls on November 3, 2026, and this page does not anticipate its result.

The measure reaches homes, but its effect on a commercial project runs through the tax base. Department of Revenue data in the same report put oil production at 22.5% of the state's property tax in 2024, natural gas at 18.4%, coal at 7.8% and industrial property at 6.9%, with residential property at about 29% [^8]. With mineral output lower in 2025, and a residential cut on the ballot, counties and school districts are weighing where revenue will come from, and economists quoted in the report raised an income tax as one answer [^8]. A study dated before the result states the mill levy it uses and the date of the levy sheet; a study dated after it states which law it was priced under.

Sales tax gives a second reading of county demand. In the Economic Analysis Division's fiscal 2025 figures, as K2 Radio reported them on November 13, 2025, collections fell in Campbell County by 8.2% and in Sweetwater County by 11.6%, and rose in Natrona County by 3.1% [^37]. A retail, restaurant or fuel project uses its county's line as a check on the spending it assumes, alongside the payroll record.

Labor is the one operating cost that carries dated public figures, and only for two metro areas: BLS's May 2025 occupational surveys for the Cheyenne and Casper metros give mean wages by occupation group, which the market sections print. Everything else a budget needs is missing from the public primary record: construction cost indices for any Wyoming market, Department of Insurance rate filings or an average commercial or homeowners premium, wage determinations for state-assisted projects and impact fee schedules. A Wyoming study prices each of those from a dated quote for the site, a contractor's bid and the county's own fee sheet.

FEMA's press release of November 14, 2024 announced a major disaster declaration, DR-4845, for wildfires burning from August 21 to 31, 2024, with Public Assistance in Campbell and Johnson counties and hazard mitigation available statewide [^38]. For a project in or near Gillette that declaration is the recent hazard of record, and a study reads the parcel's own fire history and the insurer's assessment of it.

Drought brought SBA's economic injury loans. SBA's release of February 9, 2026 reminded small businesses and private nonprofits in Campbell, Converse, Crook, Fremont, Lincoln, Niobrara, Park, Sublette, Teton and Weston counties that applications were due March 9, 2026, for loans of up to $2 million [^39]. The deadline has passed, but an operator in those counties may already carry such a loan, and a lender's debt schedule for an acquisition there lists it.

Several things a hazard section would normally cite were not reachable: FEMA's 2025 and 2026 declarations for Wyoming (the agency's pages could not be read), the state hazard mitigation plan's figures and a dated count of 2025 wildfire acres. A Wyoming study takes flood status from the parcel's flood map panel, wildfire exposure from the insurer and the county, and winter road closures on I-80 and the mountain passes from the state transportation department's records for the corridor its customers use.

Hotels, RV parks and short stays

Wyoming's statewide lodging tax applies to hotel rooms, RV and campground sites, rentals and other short stays, and a county may add a local lodging tax of its own [^31]. Wyoming Public Media reported in August 2025 that Teton County, home of Jackson, was weighing such a tax as one of the counties without one [^31]. A hotel, glamping site or campground pro forma therefore carries the state tax and the county tax in force on its opening date, and a lodging study in Teton County states whether the local tax has been adopted by then. Short-term rental rules come from towns and counties; no statewide short-term rental statute passed from 2024 to 2026 was found.

Data centers and large energy loads

Data center rules in Wyoming are being written county by county. Cheyenne LEADS, the Cheyenne area's economic development group, lists state incentives for data centers above a capital investment threshold, along with the city's noise limits at the property line [^40]. Laramie County now has a workforce housing requirement for large industrial projects, which WyoFile reported on October 9, 2026 [^41]. The Cheyenne city council rejected a petition for a one-year data center moratorium in May 2026, and the Wyoming Outdoor Council held statewide forums on data center rules through the fall, as Wyoming Public Media reported on October 28, 2026 [^42]. In Natrona County, the county and a developer were weighing the industrial park exemption instead of the state Industrial Siting Council's review for a proposed computing complex east of Casper [^43].

Even a project with no server hall in it is affected. A hotel or apartment plan near one of these campuses states which construction schedule its demand depends on, and a site in Laramie County that falls under the workforce housing requirement reads it before it budgets.

What the record does not hold

The research did not find, with bill numbers and dates, any zoning reform, certificate of need change or liquor licence rule change enacted in Wyoming from 2024 to 2026, nor the state's licensing rules for child care centers or assisted living in a dated primary source. A senior housing, child care, restaurant or bar project in Wyoming therefore takes its licensing and its licence availability from the agency or the city clerk on the filing date, and the study cites that letter or schedule.

Realtor.com's figures, as FRED relays them, put Wyoming's median home listing price at $496,500 in September 2026, with 2,589 active listings [^44,45]. The state's all-transactions house price index from FHFA, which sets the first quarter of 1980 at 100, read 526.96 for the second quarter of 2026 [^46]. The Census Bureau's permit survey counted 132 units of new private housing authorized in Wyoming in September 2026; FRED's page does not show the same month of 2025 [^47].

Those three series describe owner housing. They are the context for an apartment, build-to-rent or workforce housing study, because a renter who can buy at the listing median is a renter a new building can lose, but they do not measure the rental market itself. The Wyoming Community Development Authority's 2026 rental vacancy survey could not be opened, and no broker report on Wyoming apartments, industrial, office, retail or self-storage space was found. Hotel occupancy and room rates are not published in text form by the Office of Tourism or the city visitor bureaus; Cheyenne's appear only as chart images.

For most projects, then, supply in Wyoming is counted by the study itself: the county assessor's records, the city's certificates of occupancy, a drive of the competitive set and the operators' own figures where they will give them. The county listing counts in the Cheyenne and Casper sections are the nearest public substitute.

Laramie County had 102.938 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 in the Census estimate FRED carries, against 101.687 thousand a year earlier [^48]. Over the year to July 2025 its net migration ranked first among Wyoming counties, and so did its growth rate, as the News Letter Journal reported the Census county estimates [^2]. The metro's payroll did not grow with its population in 2026. The quarterly county files point the other way for 2024 and early 2025. Laramie County's covered employment rose by +635 jobs from the fourth quarter of 2023 to the fourth quarter of 2024 [^15] and by +834 jobs from the first quarter of 2024 to the first quarter of 2025, a gain in which construction added more than 600 jobs [^14]. The release does not name the projects behind that gain. Whatever its source, construction is a temporary payroll: a crew hotel or short-stay rental that rents to it has demand that ends when the building does.

Wages

BLS's occupational survey for the Cheyenne metro, released on August 5, 2026, gives an all-occupation mean of $30.33 an hour in May 2025 [^50]. Management occupations averaged $54.72 an hour and healthcare practitioners and technical occupations $55.50, the highest-paid major group in the release; food preparation and serving workers averaged $15.43 [^50]. Transportation and material moving occupations held 5,170 jobs, about one in ten of the metro's total in the release [^50], in a city where Interstates 25 and 80 meet. A restaurant or hotel budget in Cheyenne uses the food service line, and a clinic budget uses the healthcare line, each with its May 2025 date.

Data centers and the projects around them

Cheyenne LEADS counted 10 locations with operating data centers in Cheyenne and Laramie County on June 4, 2026, with 5 more under construction and 9 planned or in due diligence [^1]; it gave no megawatts or investment totals. The largest single proposal has had a changing record. Laramie County commissioners unanimously approved a gas-powered campus by Crusoe and Tallgrass of 1.8 gigawatts in January 2026, as Wyoming Public Media reported [^51]; Bloomberg Law reported in June 2026 that Crusoe had paused the Wyoming site [^52]; and in October 2026 WyoFile reported Tallgrass developing a campus near Cheyenne with Google, planned at 2.7 gigawatts [^53]. The Cheyenne LEADS data center page reports that data centers use a small share of the city's municipal water supply [^40].

Other projects are adding to the county's construction load. The state Industrial Siting Council permitted the 650-megawatt Laramie Range Wind project in October 2026, after the county commission denied the original plan in September 2025, as Cowboy State Daily reported [^54]. And the Professional Rodeo Cowboys Association is moving its headquarters and hall of fame to a campus in Cheyenne, with construction planned for 2027 and 2028 and a ten-year economic impact estimated at roughly $253 million, according to Cowboy State Daily on October 25, 2026 [^55].

For a lender, the question each of those raises is timing. A hotel, RV park or apartment building sized to construction crews needs the schedule for the projects it depends on, and the county's workforce housing requirement for large industrial projects [^41] may house some of those crews on the developer's own land.

Frontier Days and housing

Cheyenne Frontier Days is the county's best-documented event. Its 2024 report, as KGAB relayed it, counted 263,640 tickets sold and approximately 660 jobs supported, full and part time, and put visitor spending attributable to the event at $50.4 million [^56]. An event of that size packs lodging demand into its July run, and a hotel study shows the rest of the year as plainly as the rodeo.

The county's for-sale market had a median listing price of $473,975 in September 2026, with 377 listings active, in Realtor.com's figures relayed by FRED [^57,58]. City building permit counts, apartment vacancy and hotel occupancy for Cheyenne were not found as text; a multifamily or lodging study gathers them from the city and from a survey of the competitive set.

Natrona County had 80.526 thousand residents on July 1, 2025, up from 80.271 thousand a year earlier in FRED's Census series [^59], and net migration of 275 people over the year to July 2025 [^2]. The Casper metro is the county alone. BLS's September 2026 summary, preliminary and unadjusted, counts 39.9 thousand nonfarm jobs, lower than a year earlier, with trade, transportation and utilities at 9.2 thousand [^60].

Casper's occupational survey shows what kind of town it is. Casper's all-occupation mean wage in May 2025 was $28.93 an hour [^5]. Installation, maintenance and repair occupations held 2,450 jobs, with a mean of $30.75 an hour [^5]. The location quotient for mobile heavy equipment mechanics, other than engine specialists, was 4.26, meaning the metro employs them at several times the national rate, and electrical power-line installers and repairers averaged $100,820 a year [^5]. Those figures describe a service base for oil fields, mines, pipelines and power lines across central Wyoming. An equipment dealer, truck repair shop, parts warehouse or field-services yard has a deep labor pool here and a customer base that rises and falls with drilling and mining.

The airport is the brighter series. Casper/Natrona County International Airport reported 103,763 total enplanements for 2025, the first year above 100,000 in a decade [^61]. For a hotel near the airport or downtown, that count is a measure of business and visitor travel into central Wyoming, and a study sets the hotel's year against it.

Natrona County's for-sale housing had a median listing price of $401,875 in September 2026, and 229 listings active [^62,63]. A proposed computing complex east of Casper, and the county's consideration of the industrial park exemption for it, is described under the rules section [^43]; its size and timing are not settled, and a study near the Hat Six Road area treats it as a possibility. Hotel occupancy, building permits, broker reports and traffic counts for Casper were not found in a public text source.

Campbell County had 48.145 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 in FRED's Census series [^64]. Its covered employment fell by -453 jobs from the first quarter of 2024 to the first quarter of 2025, with mining losing more than 300 jobs, in the Workforce Services release of August 8, 2025 [^14]. WyoFile, reporting the Gillette News Record's review of county records on September 28, 2026, found that 39 parcels holding Gillette's biggest apartment complexes are owned by out-of-state companies, with only 5 of the 20 largest complexes owned by companies filed in Gillette [^65]. No rents or vacancy figures were printed.

What a study in Gillette has to source is most of the market. Rents and vacancy come from a survey of the complexes and their managers. Hotel occupancy, the city's sales tax collections, county assessed valuation and Northeast Wyoming Regional Airport's passenger counts were not found. The demand side starts from the mines: which ones the project's customers work at, what their plans say about production, and how the BWXT nuclear fuel facility the state is helping to fund [^26] fits that schedule. The 2024 wildfire declaration covering Campbell County, and the county's place on the SBA drought list, are in the hazards section.

Sweetwater County, which holds both Rock Springs and Green River, had 41.267 thousand residents on July 1, 2025, against 41.350 thousand a year earlier, in FRED's Census series [^66]. Covered employment in the county fell by -575 jobs (-2.7%) from the fourth quarter of 2023 to the fourth quarter of 2024 [^15].

The county's base is trona mining and soda ash processing, and a study here has to source the plants' own plans: the fuel change the Kemmerer closure forces on the plants that burn its coal [^24], their employment and their expansion schedules. City population estimates for Rock Springs and Green River, hotel occupancy, lodging tax collections, building permits and the airport's passenger counts were not found. Green River's downtown has one documented project, the renovation of the 1921 Tomahawk Hotel, which Cowboy State Daily reported in October 2026 [^67]; a single renovation shows interest, not a market, and a lodging or retail study surveys the corridor itself.

This section reads Teton County, Wyoming alone; the Census Bureau's Jackson area also takes in Teton County, Idaho, which no figure here includes. Teton County, Wyoming had 23.333 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 in FRED's Census series, against 23.345 thousand a year earlier [^68]. It is small and it is crowded with visitors. The Park Service counted 3,800,648 recreation visits to Grand Teton National Park in 2025 [^69]. The Jackson Hole News&Guide's Market Watch column of September 2, 2026 put Grand Teton's July 2026 visitation at 773,094 and its fiscal year to July 2026 at 3,806,677, relaying Park Service counts [^13].

The airport set records in the same column. Jackson Hole Airport boarded 83,388 passengers in July 2026, an all-time monthly high, and 598,923 in the fiscal year ending July 2026 [^13]. Air arrivals matter more here than anywhere else in Wyoming because the county's visitors include many who fly in for a week rather than drive through, and a lodging or vacation rental study in Jackson sets its year against the airport's monthly series.

Lodging tax is the dated money record. The Jackson Hole Travel and Tourism Board's annual report, as Buckrail reported it in February 2024, gave Teton County lodging tax revenue of $10,095,202.90 for July 2022 to June 2023 and counted 7,890 tourism-related jobs in the county [^12]. Those figures are older than the rest of this section, and a study asks the board for the latest fiscal year before using them. Teton County was among the counties without a local lodging tax in August 2025 [^31], so a lodging pro forma here checks whether one has been adopted.

The home market is in a different class from the rest of the state. A broker-sponsored report on Buckrail counted $980 million of Teton County sales in the first nine months of 2025 and 301 properties listed in late October 2025, against 256 a year earlier [^70]. For an employer that is a labor question as much as a housing one: a hotel, restaurant or care project in Jackson states where its staff will live and, if the answer is outside the county, budgets for housing or transport as part of its labor cost. Hotel occupancy, the town's permit counts and its short-term rental rules with dates were not found; a study gathers them from the town, the county and the operators.

Sheridan County had 33.241 thousand residents on July 1, 2025, up from 32.933 thousand a year earlier in FRED's Census series [^71], and net migration of 338 people in the year to July 2025 [^2]. Covered employment grew in both of the latest county releases: by +224 jobs (1.6%) from the fourth quarter of 2023 to the fourth quarter of 2024 [^15], and by +281 jobs from the first quarter of 2024 to the first quarter of 2025, with gains in local government, leisure and hospitality, retail, manufacturing and professional and business services [^14].

Gains spread across that many industries make Sheridan a different case from Gillette or Rock Springs: its demand does not hang on one mine or plant. What a study must still source is the market itself. Hotel occupancy, the county's lodging tax collections, building permits, home prices for the city and Sheridan County Airport's passenger counts were not found as dated text, and the county's travel and tourism reports were not available as readable text. A lodging, senior housing or retail project here starts from the city's and county's own records and a survey of its competitors.

Albany County had 38.558 thousand residents on July 1, 2025, against 38.545 thousand a year earlier, in FRED's Census series [^72], and its covered employment rose by +99 jobs from the first quarter of 2024 to the first quarter of 2025 [^14].

The University of Wyoming is the county's anchor, and almost everything a study of Laramie needs has to be sourced from it and from the city. The university's enrollment for fall 2026, the county's unemployment rate in a 2026 release, hotel occupancy and lodging tax collections, building permits, short-term rental rules, broker reports and Laramie Regional Airport's passenger counts were not found. A student housing, hotel or restaurant study here asks the university for its enrollment and housing plans in writing and surveys the competitive set itself.

Request a methodology briefing

Working as a Wyoming feasibility study consultant, Feasibility Study Company prepares lender-grade studies for loans under SBA's 7(a) and 504 programs, for USDA Rural Development guarantees, for EB-5 offerings and for conventional bank credit. What a lender wants from a Wyoming feasibility study company differs by program, and the public record for each program in this state is uneven.

SBA 7(a) and 504

SBA's regulation on studies reads 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^73]. Since October 1, 2026 lenders have worked from SOP 50 10 8.1 [^74]. A 504 project that is new, or a single-purpose building such as a hotel or a car wash, puts in 15% equity, and one that is both puts in 20% [^74]. A Wyoming motel, lodge or car wash meets the higher tiers more often than not, so the equity line in its sources and uses is set from the SOP before the study tests the debt. Those tests are walked through in the practice area for SBA 7(a) and 504 lending. Fiscal 2025 results for the SBA's Wyoming District Office were not found in an SBA release.

USDA Rural Development

Many Wyoming towns could be eligible for USDA's Business and Industry guarantees and its Community Facilities programs, subject to the parcel test on USDA's eligibility map. The Wyoming Rural Development office's page returned an error, so its fiscal 2025 obligations are not on this page. A Wyoming sponsor checks the parcel before naming the program; how the programs judge a project is covered under USDA Rural Development.

Banks and the conventional market

Community and regional banks carry much of Wyoming's commercial lending, but a dated statewide deposit total was not available: the FDIC's 2025 summary record for Wyoming states no unit for its deposit field, so no total is printed here. The bank's own credit policy and its appraiser's view of the market are what a conventional study answers; the tests are summarized under conventional and institutional lending.

EB-5

The record holds no Wyoming regional center and no EB-5 project figure. An EB-5 offering for a Wyoming resort or hotel would rest its job count on the same county payroll, wage and visitor records this page holds; the program's own tests are on the EB-5 page.

The state's SSBCI money

Wyoming applied in January 2022 for a federal State Small Business Credit Initiative allocation of just over $58 million over nine years [^75]. In March 2023 the Wyoming Business Council said it would use up to $58.4 million of those federal funds for its Wyoming Venture Capital program, through a funds strategy of $23.4 million and a direct investment strategy [^76]. That is equity for young companies, not a loan participation for a real estate project, and a Wyoming hotel or plant does not count on it. The state's other development money flows to public infrastructure: the State Loan and Investment Board approved Business Ready Community grants in June 2026 of $5,701,748 for the Seven Trails Commerce Center in Douglas and $3,179,648 for roads and utilities serving new homes in Wright [^77]. A project on a site those grants serve cites them as the reason the site is ready.
