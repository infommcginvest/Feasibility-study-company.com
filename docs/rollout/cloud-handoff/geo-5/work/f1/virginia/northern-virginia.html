Feasibility Study Company writes independent, lender-grade feasibility studies for projects in Fairfax, Arlington, Loudoun and Prince William counties and the city of Alexandria, for borrowers using USDA Rural Development, SBA 7(a) or 504, EB-5 or conventional loans. The first question a Northern Virginia feasibility study has to settle here is which of the five payrolls stands behind the subject, because in the year to March 2026 they did not move together.

For March 2026, the Virginia county employment and wages release from BLS counts 622,779 jobs in Fairfax County's covered establishments, 171,969 in Arlington County and 79,023 in the city of Alexandria, and in each of the three the count stood below March 2025 [^1]. Loudoun County's 206,071 and Prince William County's 146,398 were both above their year-earlier levels [^1]. The three jurisdictions on or near the Potomac lost ground; the two counties farther out gained it. The Virginia state page carries the statewide federal workforce record that sits behind that year, and this page keeps to the five jurisdiction counts.

Pay sorts the five differently from headcount. Average weekly wages for the first quarter were $2,901 in Arlington County, $2,451 in Fairfax County, $1,944 in Alexandria and $1,292 in Prince William County, and the release shows each of them higher than a year before (it prints Loudoun's figure too) [^1]. These are wages paid by establishments located in each jurisdiction, so they describe the jobs, not the people who live nearby. An Arlington office tower's payroll is drawn from commuters across the region. A restaurant, childcare center or fitness club sells to residents, and for that side of the ledger a study turns to household income by place of residence, which the table below gives.

The resident labor count comes from a wider frame. The BLS Northern Virginia table puts employed residents at 1,647,917 in September 2026, a preliminary figure, not seasonally adjusted, for a region that also takes in Fauquier, Stafford and Spotsylvania counties and the city of Fredericksburg [^8]. That total cannot be laid against the five-jurisdiction job counts above as though the two shared a boundary. At the county level, FFXnow reported 23,749 jobless residents in Fairfax County for January 2026, preliminary, more than the county had in January 2025 [^9]. A consumer project in Fairfax County should carry that softer start to 2026 in its ramp, not only the wage level.

New construction is the dated signal it does hold. Census permit series relayed by the St. Louis Fed record 2,862 new private housing structures authorized in Fairfax County in 2025, 1,340 in Prince William County and 1,309 in Arlington County [^11,7,12]. The series is titled in structures while its unit label reads units, so a study that counts apartments should confirm against the Census Bureau's own permit files which of the two it is reading. Alexandria's series printed an implausibly small 2025 value and is not used, and Loudoun's is not shown here; for both, the study goes to the Census files directly.

Asking prices show where the household money sits. Realtor.com's median listing price for September 2026 was $782,450 in Loudoun County, $724,900 in Fairfax County, $619,648 in Arlington County, $599,995 in Prince William County and $534,950 in the city of Alexandria [^6,13,14,5,15]. A listing median is what sellers asked for the homes on the market that month; the survey values in the table above are what owners said their homes were worth across five years. The two answer different questions, and a study keeps them apart rather than reading a growth rate from one to the other.

For a project that depends on new rooftops, the useful reading is the neighborhood, not the county. Prince William's 1,340 permitted structures [^7] and its $599,995 listing median [^5,6] describe the county a new rooftop project there sells into, and a for-sale, build-to-rent or neighborhood retail study there has to count the approved lots and recorded plats within the subject's drive time, not the county total. Counting at that scale is the subject of the research note the trade area is the study.

Two airports anchor the region's visitor and business-travel demand, and both sit inside this page's five jurisdictions. Washington Dulles International, which lies in Loudoun and Fairfax counties, carried 29.01 million passengers in 2025, a record, and 10.53 million of them were international travelers, another record [^2]. Ronald Reagan Washington National in Arlington County carried 24.89 million, fewer than in 2024 [^2]. The airports authority puts the two together at more than 53.9 million [^2].

The two airports sit in different hotel and service markets. Around Dulles, on the Route 28 and Dulles Toll Road corridor, an international count of that size is a segment a hotel study can name and test. Around Reagan National, in Crystal City, Pentagon City and Old Town Alexandria, the airport sits beside the same office districts whose payrolls the county release shows shrinking, so the study tests weekday business demand against those counts. A record at one airport and a decline at the other is a reason to read the subject's own corridor, not the regional total.

For a hotel study, the passenger counts explain demand segments; they do not size a property's share. The subject's competitive set, its trailing twelve months of occupancy and rate, and the rooms opening nearby do that work, and our note on reading the STR report shows how the competitive set is drawn.

Road access is the gap. The research found no Virginia Department of Transportation traffic counts for the I-66, I-95, Route 1, Route 7, Route 28 or Dulles corridors, so this page prints none. A fuel, quick-service or car wash project on any of those roads needs the VDOT count for the segment that passes the site, its year, and a statement of how far the counting station sits from the driveway.

Office and industrial supply here is published by brokers, and the two used on this page, CBRE and Cushman & Wakefield, each draw their own Northern Virginia boundary. Neither boundary is the five jurisdictions of this page, and they are not the same as each other. Their figures for the same quarter therefore differ, and this page states each broker's number in its own sentence, never averaged, ranked or set against the other.

Office: CBRE's reading

CBRE reports net absorption of +355,000 square feet for the third quarter of 2026 and positive absorption of 941,000 square feet across the first three quarters of the year [^4]. Its overall average asking rent was $37.80 per square foot, and the page does not say whether that rent is quoted full service or on another basis [^4]. A study that uses the CBRE rent has to establish the basis before it compares the figure with a lease the subject has signed.

Office: Cushman & Wakefield's reading

Cushman & Wakefield puts the average full-service asking rent at $36.45 per square foot for Q3 2026 and breaks it out by jurisdiction: $40.63 in Arlington County, $36.76 in the city of Alexandria, $35.44 in Fairfax County and $28.87 in Loudoun County [^3]. It records net absorption of +413,800 square feet for the quarter and +377,800 in Alexandria, a city figure it attributes to the National Science Foundation occupying space at 401 Dulany Street [^3]. Prince William County carries no rent or absorption figure in either broker's report.

That single move is the lesson for a lender. One federal tenant can drive a jurisdiction's quarter, and the next quarter can return space as quickly. An office acquisition, an office-to-residential conversion or a ground-floor retail plan in Alexandria or Arlington should rest on leasing by tenants other than the one large occupier, on effective rents rather than asking rents (the gap is explained in a research note, effective rent vs. asking rent), and on an absorption record traced tenant by tenant, the method in our absorption forensics note. Where conversion is on the table, the office repurposing note covers what a lender tests.

Industrial: CBRE's reading

CBRE's industrial report gives an average asking rent of $18.10 per square foot for Q3 2026 and 283,000 square feet completed in the quarter, of which about 61% was still available [^16]. The same report lists one project under construction without printing its size [^16], so a study cannot read the pipeline from it and has to confirm what is being built near the subject from permits.

Industrial: Cushman & Wakefield's reading

Cushman & Wakefield prints an overall industrial asking rent of $18.73 per square foot for Q3 2026 and net absorption of +168,600 square feet in the quarter [^17]. A flex, contractor-yard or last-mile project quotes that rent only with the broker's name and definition beside it.

Data centers: the dated count is missing

The statewide data center tax picture is on the Virginia page.

To start a study, send three things: what is to be built, bought or refinanced; which of the five jurisdictions holds the site, or the submarket if the parcel is not fixed yet; and where the money is coming from, whether an SBA lender, USDA, EB-5 investors or a bank's own balance sheet. The reply sets out the framework the study will follow and the deliverable the program expects.

Request a methodology briefing

Each of the five jurisdictions writes its own zoning, permits its own buildings and taxes its own property. Alexandria is an independent city, governed apart from any county. Fairfax County also surrounds or borders independent cities that this page does not cover, the City of Fairfax and Falls Church among them, and Prince William County has Manassas and Manassas Park inside its outline. A parcel with a Fairfax or Manassas mailing address can sit in a city with different rules from the county around it, which is why a Northern Virginia feasibility study consultant confirms the governing jurisdiction from the parcel record before reading a single figure for it.

The research gathered for this page holds none of those rulebooks. It found no zoning or short-term rental ordinance for any of the five, and no building permit or certificate of occupancy report from any of them. Each of those is a stated gap: the study obtains the current zoning district and permitted uses from the jurisdiction's own code, the short-term rental rule from its ordinance where a lodging or furnished-rental project depends on it, and the permit and occupancy record from the jurisdiction's permit office, each with the date it was read.

Those rules decide feasibility more often than the market does in a built-up place. On a small infill parcel in Arlington or Alexandria, the zoning district sets the floor area and parking the pro forma can count; on a Loudoun or Prince William greenfield site, the approval path and the timing of road and utility work set when revenue can begin. The site budget and schedule in the study come from those documents, not from an allowance.

No lender ranking, SBA district total or certified development company production record for these five jurisdictions turned up in the research. A sponsor choosing between lenders asks each one for its own recent production in the subject's asset class, and a 504 sponsor asks the development company for the same.

The program rules are fixed and dated. SBA's loan conditions say: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^18]. Coverage on a 7(a) loan has a standard floor of 1.15x, set in SOP 50 10 8.1 [^19]. Whether a lender asks for a study on a given loan is the lender's call, and what that study must show for each program is laid out under our SBA lending work.

Conventional and institutional lenders on office, industrial and hotel loans here will want the broker discipline described above, one named report per statement, and a downside case tied to the jurisdiction's own payroll; the conventional and institutional practice page covers that work. An EB-5 project has to document its job creation and, where it claims a targeted employment area, the designation for the specific site; the EB-5 practice page explains both. As a Northern Virginia feasibility study company, we write the study to the program the capital comes from, and the parcel decides which programs are open.

The research for this page holds no USDA rural eligibility reading, no Opportunity Zone tract list and no HUBZone designation for any part of the five jurisdictions, so the page asserts none. A sponsor considering a USDA Business and Industry guarantee checks the exact address against USDA's eligibility map before the application is planned; the steps after a site qualifies sit with our USDA Rural Development work. Opportunity Zone and HUBZone status are confirmed the same way, tract by tract, with the date of the map read.

A sponsor whose search runs beyond these five jurisdictions will find other Virginia markets as sections of the state page: Richmond, Hampton Roads and Charlottesville each carry their own dated figures there.

The case library has no project in Fairfax, Arlington, Loudoun, Prince William or Alexandria. The cases it does hold show how a study carries a program's rules through a pro forma: equity sized to the program tier, debt service coverage tested in the year the rule names, and a sensitivity table on the input the deal depends on most. A Northern Virginia hotel, office conversion or flex project would follow the same structure, with the inputs taken from the jurisdiction-level figures on this page. The full set is on the case studies page.
