The studies Feasibility Study Company prepares in Alaska are independent and lender-grade, written for projects that an EB-5 offering, a bank, an SBA 7(a) or 504 lender or a USDA Rural Development program is asked to finance, and each one is written for two readers at once: the owner or developer who proposes the project and the lender who has to approve it. An Alaska feasibility study consultant starts somewhere a study in most states would not, with the Department of Revenue's oil forecast. North Slope crude and the Permanent Fund feed the state's unrestricted general fund, and a hotel, clinic, apartment building or supply yard whose customers draw public paychecks or work on state contracts depends on that fund whether or not it ever sells to the oil industry.

The Spring 2026 Revenue Forecast, under a cover letter dated March 13, 2026, puts the average Alaska North Slope price at $75.26 per barrel for fiscal 2026 and production at an average of 459.2 thousand barrels per day for fiscal 2026 and 518.5 thousand barrels per day for fiscal 2027 [^1]. New production is starting: Santos announced first oil from Pikka on May 17, 2026, as Alaska Business reported [^2]. The Energy Information Administration, working on a different basis in November 2025, forecast Alaska crude production of 477,000 barrels per day in calendar 2026, the most since 2018 [^15]. The state figure is a fiscal-year forecast and EIA's a calendar-year one, published in different seasons; a study quotes each with its own basis and date and does not merge them.

The same forecast carries the treasury. Unrestricted general fund revenue is forecast at 6,491.9 million dollars for fiscal 2026 and 6,725.3 million dollars for fiscal 2027, and the transfer from the Permanent Fund into the general fund, the percent-of-market-value draw, at 3,798.9 million dollars for fiscal 2026 [^1]. The Fund itself was valued at $87.6 billion on January 31, 2026 [^1]. Mining adds its own line, with unrestricted mining revenue forecast at 102.0 million dollars for fiscal 2027 [^1].

Price is the line that moves first. Alaska North Slope crude averaged $69.48 per barrel in February 2026, as Alaska Public Media reported in March 2026 from the Department of Revenue, in an article on forecasters' expectation that disruption from the war with Iran would linger for months [^16]. A forecast written in one season can be overtaken in the next, and the department publishes a new one each spring and fall, so a study that leans on state spending names the edition it used and the price assumption inside it.

The practical consequence for a borrower is narrow and specific. A Juneau apartment project leased to public employees, an Anchorage hotel that books state and industry travel, or a Kenai Peninsula fabrication shop serving North Slope contractors carries some exposure to this forecast. The study identifies which line of the forecast the project's demand rests on, prints it with its date, and tests the project at a weaker reading of that line.

BLS counted 340.7 thousand seasonally adjusted nonfarm jobs in Alaska in September 2026, preliminary, and printed a change of +1.2% over twelve months [^17], and a seasonally adjusted civilian labor force of 365.2 thousand [^5]. The Department of Labor and Workforce Development's own release for the same month reports jobs up 0.1% from September 2025 [^18]. The two are different series from different publishers, each with its own basis, and a study that needs a statewide job count picks one, names it and does not average or reconcile them.

The sector lines say more about a given project than the total does. In BLS's seasonally adjusted September 2026 figures, all preliminary, construction held 17.7 thousand jobs (-5.3%), government 76.8 thousand (-3.0%) and manufacturing 13.9 thousand (+14.9%), each change measured over twelve months as printed [^5]. The department's Alaska Economic Trends for August 2026 carries an article on the federal jobs Alaska lost in 2025 [^19], and federal employers sit inside the government line.

Each line points at different property types. The government count is the one a project in Juneau, or one leasing to agencies in Anchorage or Fairbanks, cites for its tenant base. The construction count is both a demand signal for builders' supply yards and the labor pool a project's own contractor hires from, so a schedule for a 2027 start is written with it in view. The manufacturing line takes in seafood processing along with other plants, and a processing or industrial study looks past the statewide change to the employer and the borough before using it.

Business Employment Dynamics adds the churn behind the totals. From September to December 2025, Alaska's private sector recorded 30,293 gross job gains at opening and expanding establishments and 23,914 gross job losses at closing and contracting ones, seasonally adjusted, for a net change of +6,379 [^20]. The state demographer estimated 738,737 Alaska residents on July 1, 2025, up +1,649 (+0.2%) from a year earlier [^4]. SBA 7(a) and 504

SBA's own regulation settles the point: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^51]. In Alaska a lender asks for one most often where the cash flow has not been earned yet: a new lodge sized for the cruise season, a start-up processor, a clinic in a borough without a comparable. Since October 1, 2026, lenders have followed SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates [^52]. That procedure sets debt service coverage for a standard 7(a) loan at 1.15x; 504 equity is 10%, or 15% if the borrower's business is new or its building is special-purpose [^52].
