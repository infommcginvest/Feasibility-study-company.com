For a Vermont project, Feasibility Study Company prepares an independent feasibility study that serves two readers: whoever builds or buys the property, and the bank, SBA lender or USDA program that must approve its loan. In Vermont the first question in that file is about people to staff the project, and the evidence starts there. The Department of Labor's September 2026 report puts the seasonally adjusted civilian labor force at 337,877, with 329,128 employed and 8,749 unemployed, and its comparison column gives 347,408 for September 2025 [^1]. In April 2026 the Department had already reported that Vermont's labor force decreased over the year to February [^14]. Vermont Business Magazine, relaying the September release, gave the labor force participation rate as 62.1% [^15].

BLS publishes the same measure without seasonal adjustment: 337,142 for September 2026, marked preliminary, and 347,040 for September 2025 [^16]. The two agencies' numbers are not a disagreement. One series removes the seasonal swing in Vermont hiring and the other keeps it, so a study names which one it quotes and stays with it from the market chapter to the staffing plan.

Payrolls are counted by employer rather than by household. BLS's state table gives Vermont 311.2 thousand nonfarm jobs, seasonally adjusted, in September 2025 and 310.8 thousand in August 2026 [^17]. That table's September 2026 column falls outside this page's research and is not quoted; a study pulls the newest column from the current release on the day it is written and names the month it used.

By sector, BLS's Vermont summary for September 2026, preliminary and seasonally adjusted, counts 65.5 thousand jobs in education and health services, 56.8 thousand in government, 31.4 thousand in professional and business services and 27.9 thousand in manufacturing [^18]. Those four lines sort the demand cases a lender will see. Health and education carry medical office, senior housing and the housing that hospital and college staff rent. Government payroll anchors Montpelier and its county. Manufacturing brings supplier, warehouse and workforce housing projects behind it.

Underneath the totals, BLS's Business Employment Dynamics series recorded 19,658 gross job losses and 16,739 gross job gains at Vermont's private employers between June and September 2025 [^18]. Those are flows, not levels: they show how much hiring and shedding runs beneath a payroll that moves little from month to month, and that churn is what a new employer competes in when it recruits a kitchen, a front desk or a production line.

The county wage file is the one a budget uses. BLS's release of September 4, 2026, whose tables carry the note that the data are preliminary, counts 33,374 establishments in Vermont in the first quarter of 2026, covered employment of 304,200 in March 2026, which BLS reports as a decrease over the year, and an average weekly wage of $1,322, which it reports as an increase [^2]. Output gives the scale. BEA's all-industry gross domestic product for Vermont was $49,657.5 million in 2025, in current dollars, as FRED relays it [^19]. The research behind this page did not find the state's own real growth figure for 2026, the Joint Fiscal Office's consensus forecast or a university business forecast, so a study that projects demand forward states the growth rate it assumed and where that rate came from.

Flooding is the hazard a Vermont lender asks about first, and the record runs through two summers. FEMA's notice for DR-4720-VT, the major disaster declared on July 14, 2023 for flooding from July 7 to 17, designated Caledonia, Chittenden, Lamoille, Orange, Orleans, Rutland, Washington, Windham and Windsor counties for Individual Assistance, with Public Assistance for emergency work in every county [^20]. A year later DR-4810-VT, for the storms of July 9 to 11, 2024, designated Addison, Caledonia, Chittenden, Essex, Lamoille, Orleans and Washington for Individual and Public Assistance [^21]. Chittenden, Caledonia, Lamoille, Orleans and Washington appear on both notices.

The state's one-year report on the 2023 flood, as Vermont Business Magazine relayed it on July 11, 2024, counted $25,987,190.49 of Individual Assistance approved through FEMA and SBA, and it named Chittenden, Lamoille, Rutland, Washington, Windham and Windsor as the declaration's counties [^22]. The FEMA notice is the record of designation; the report's shorter list is what the magazine printed. For a site in Caledonia, Orange or Orleans the notice is the one to cite.

Recovery money then came through HUD's disaster block grant, administered by the Agency of Commerce and Community Development. Its November 2025 awards page shows $49,991,445 approved across housing, infrastructure, mitigation and planning [^4]. On October 15, 2026 the Governor announced the final award, which put Vermont's CDBG-DR funding for the 2023 flood at $67,845,000 across 28 projects [^5]. The November 2025 page lists awards in Barre, Montpelier and Brattleboro among others, and the market sections name them where they fall.

For businesses and farms, VEDA opened a Disaster Recovery Loan Fund in August 2025 to lend to those affected by declared disasters [^23]. A borrower that drew on it carries that loan into any new financing, and a lender reading the study wants it in the debt schedule.

Three consequences follow for a study. First, grant-funded housing is new supply: an apartment study in Barre, Montpelier or another awarded town counts those homes in its pipeline before it sets an absorption pace. Second, a site in a designated county is read against its town's flood map, its buyout record and the elevation of its access road before any revenue line is drawn, since a property that loses its road loses its trade for as long as the road is out. Third, insurance follows from the first two; the section on costs explains why it is priced from a quote.

What the research could not reach is also part of the file. FEMA's declaration pages with dollar totals refused the connection, and no state hazard mitigation plan figure, wildfire count or winter storm loss series with a date was found. A Vermont feasibility study does not fill those gaps with an average; it reads the flood zone of the parcel and the declarations its county appears in.

The Vermont Economic Development Authority is the state's own lender to business, agriculture and, since July 2026, housing. Its release of April 1, 2026 reports investments in 32 projects in ten counties from January to March 2026, the third quarter of its fiscal year, and itemizes most of them by borrower and town [^10]. For the fiscal year 2025, Vermont Business Magazine relayed VEDA's annual meeting report: $61.5 million of loans closed and 1,748 jobs created or retained at the companies financed [^11]. From July 1, 2026 VEDA may also lend to commercial housing projects under S.328 [^24], which gives an apartment sponsor a state lender to bring beside the bank.

Vermont's federal State Small Business Credit Initiative funding was announced on July 18, 2022 at $57.9 million, divided between venture capital and a loan participation program that VEDA administers [^12]. That announcement dates from 2022 and is the allocation of record; how much of the participation money remains uncommitted was not found. A study that counts on a participation carries the lender's written confirmation that one is available for this loan.

The State Treasurer invests a share of the state's cash in Vermont housing and business through a local investment program, and its February 2026 round, unveiled with local leaders in Burlington, put money into housing across the state [^25]. Where a project's capital stack includes a Treasurer's loan, the study runs the coverage test on that loan's terms as written, not on a market rate.

The federal shutdown of October 2025 touched Vermont lending directly. SBA's state analysis of October 21, 2025 estimated that $1,586,902 of SBA loan proceeds were blocked in Vermont for each week the shutdown lasted [^13]. It is SBA's estimate of delayed loans, not an approval count. For a sponsor whose closing slipped that autumn, the study's construction interest, its opening date and the first year's debt service are restated from the actual closing.

Visitors are the other demand base beside Vermont's own households. Vermont Business Magazine, relaying the Department of Tourism and Marketing and its Tourism Economics study, reported 16 million visitors to Vermont in 2024 and 31,780 direct jobs in the visitor economy [^3]. Ski Vermont counted 339,046 cross-country skier visits at its member areas in the 2025 to 2026 season [^29], a figure that bears most on the inns and lodges near touring centers.

The visitor count sizes a market; it does not fill a hotel. The Agency of Commerce and Community Development's tourism research page carries hotel occupancy and average daily rate figures, but the page is undated, so they are not printed here. The Department of Taxes publishes meals and rooms tax statistics monthly, and the dollar receipts sit in spreadsheets this research could not read. A hotel or inn study therefore buys a dated competitive-set report for its own town and season, and states its occupancy and rate assumptions against that report rather than against a statewide average.

Two features of Vermont lodging shape that study. In the ski towns and along the foliage routes demand gathers in winter and autumn, and a debt service schedule has to carry the months between. And short-term rentals compete directly with inns and hotels in the resort towns, where Stowe's rental count and cap, in the resort section below, are the local record this research found.

For the coming winter, Vail Resorts set November 21 as the target opening day for Stowe, Okemo and Mount Snow, as Vermont Business Magazine reported on October 26, 2026 [^30]. A resort-area restaurant or shop study begins its first-season revenue from the actual opening date, not the target.

Vermont's land use permit, Act 250, decides how long a project waits and what it may build. Act 181 of 2024 changes where it applies. The Land Use Review Board's FAQ of April 10, 2026, as Vermont Business Magazine relayed it, describes a road-construction trigger taking effect on July 1, 2026 and Tier 3 jurisdiction taking effect on December 31, 2026, each unless the Legislature delays it [^31]. A study for a project that needs a new road or sits in an area the tiers reach states which jurisdiction it assumed and on what date.

Housing near transit has a separate path. In October 2026 the Board's transit-area map expanded the areas in which a housing project near transit is exempt from Act 250, with the Chittenden County map enlarged; the notice printed no effective date [^32]. For an apartment sponsor in the Burlington area the exemption bears on the schedule first: a project inside the map does not go through Act 250 review, so the study's pre-development period and carrying cost depend on which side of the line the parcel falls.

Short-term rentals are regulated town by town. Stowe's selectboard adopted a cap in July 2026 [^8], and VTDigger's January 2026 reporting describes a state surcharge on stand-alone short-term rentals [^9]. A lodging study in a resort town reads its own town's ordinance and registration rules before it counts competing rentals, because a cap limits future supply and a registry, where a town keeps one, counts the existing stock.

VEDA's June 2026 notice on housing lending also names S.327, the bill on commercial property assessed clean energy financing [^24]. Where a sponsor plans to fund efficiency or renewable work through such an assessment, the study carries the assessment's annual payment in the coverage test and states where its lien sits.

Vermont's certificate of need rules for health care facilities, its liquor licence rules, child care licensing capacity and any statewide short-term rental registry were not found. GasBuddy's survey, relayed by Vermont Business Magazine, put the average price of regular gasoline in Vermont at $4.34 a gallon on October 26, 2026 [^33]. A pump price ages within days. A convenience store or travel center study quotes the week it was written, sets fuel margin per gallon rather than price as the revenue driver, and tests the result at a different price week.

Labor is priced from the county wage file. The first-quarter 2026 county averages sit in the market sections, and a staffing plan carries its own county's figure, raised to the opening year by an escalation rate the study states. Where an occupation's hourly rate matters more than the average, as with nurses in a care facility or cooks in a restaurant, the study cites the BLS occupational wage for the nearest area; for the Burlington metro, BLS's mean hourly wage across occupations is in that section.

Construction cost is the line with the least public support. No Rider Levett Bucknall, Cumming, Mortenson or Turner index covering Burlington or Vermont was found, and no prevailing wage determination was reached. A study prices construction from a contractor's dated estimate for the project and says so, and it carries a contingency sized to how far the design has progressed.

Insurance follows the same rule. The Department of Financial Regulation's consumer advisory carried no homeowners or commercial property rate figures, and no carrier withdrawal or approved rate change for Vermont property was found. In a state where the flood notices above name so many counties, cover is priced from a quote for the parcel, with the flood zone and the elevation certificate in hand, and the study shows the premium as a line of its own.

The housing series are the deepest statewide supply record. FHFA's all-transactions index for Vermont read 902.53 for 2026 Q2 (1980:Q1=100), as FRED relays it [^34]. Connecticut Public, carrying VTDigger and Vermont Public reporting, gave a median sale price for primary homes in Vermont of about $353,000 in 2024 [^35], and the Burlington Free Press, reporting Realtor.com, gave a statewide median list price of $525,000 for July 2025 [^36]. A sale price and an asking price measure different things at different times, so a study quotes each by name and does not read one as a change from the other.

Those series matter to more than housing. A senior housing project draws residents who sell a home to move in, so the price of that home sets what the community can charge for entry; a workforce housing project is argued from the gap between what local wages carry and what homes cost.

Agriculture is the rural lending base. USDA's 2025 State Agriculture Overview for Vermont gives milk production of 2,513,000,000 pounds [^37], and its 2026 edition gives maple syrup production of 3,091,000 gallons [^38]. The 2026 page labels its figures by edition only, so the maple total is cited to that edition and not to a harvest year. Dairy processing, feed and farm supply, maple sugarhouses and their packing plants are the projects these figures support, and a USDA-backed loan for one of them starts its demand case here.

For commercial property there is no statewide inventory in the research. No broker quarterly from CBRE, Cushman and Wakefield, Colliers, JLL or Marcus and Millichap was found for Vermont apartments, industrial, office, retail or self-storage; the regional brokers' commentary pages refused the connection; and no count of senior housing units, licensed beds, hotel rooms or child care slots was reached. Each study counts the competing supply in its own trade area, names the properties and gives the date of the count.

Chittenden County's covered employment was 100,700 in March 2026, a decrease over the year in BLS's telling, and its average weekly wage in the first quarter was $1,521, an increase; both come from the release BLS marks preliminary [^2]. A staffing budget for a Burlington hotel or care facility carries that county wage, not the state average.

Three price series describe the owner market. On FHFA's all-transactions measure the metro index stood at 422.61 in the second quarter of 2026 on its 1995:Q1 base [^7], and its annual index for Chittenden County read 322.44 for 2025 on a 2000 base [^42]. Realtor.com's median listing price for the county was $577,925 in September 2026, as FRED relays it [^6], and $614,900 in July 2025, as the Burlington Free Press reported [^36]. The two indexes have different bases and areas, and the two list prices are single months a year apart in a seasonal market, so none of them is read as a trend on its own.

Supply is measured by permits. In FRED's copy of the Census Bureau's building permit series, Chittenden County authorized 1,026 new private housing structures in 2025 [^43]. The series title counts structures while its unit label says units, and the difference is large where apartment buildings are permitted, so a study states which it read before it sets the figure against household growth.

The county also has a large place in the visitor economy: Chittenden County drew 24.5% of statewide visitor spending in 2024 in the Tourism Economics study as Vermont Business Magazine relayed it [^3]. On October 26, 2026 regular gasoline averaged $4.40 a gallon across 100 Burlington stations in GasBuddy's survey [^33].

The county's employers include manufacturers whose ownership is changing: OMRON agreed on October 10, 2026 to acquire OnLogic, a South Burlington maker of industrial computers [^44]. A study whose demand rests on one employer's payroll reads that employer's own announcements, not just the county count. The transit-area exemption in the rules section reaches Chittenden County first.

Stowe's own record in this research concerns short-term rentals. For a lodge, inn or condominium project the cap changes the supply argument.
