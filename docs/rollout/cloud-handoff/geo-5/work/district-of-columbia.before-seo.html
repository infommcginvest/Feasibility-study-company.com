Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility studies for projects in Washington, DC: independent, lender-grade analyses written for the owner or developer who commissions them and for the bank, SBA lender or other capital source that has to approve the loan. The District's record changed faster in 2025 and 2026 than most state records did, and a reviewer judges a District study first on whether its figures are current and whether each one describes the place the project sells to. The first of those records is the payroll.

The Office of Revenue Analysis, reading the November 2025 employment report, counted 739,200 jobs in the District, a preliminary figure it described as the lowest since the year after the COVID-19 lows; 169,000 of them were federal government jobs [^1]. The same analysis ties the sharpest federal losses to the Deferred Resignation Program, whose departures fell between September and October 2025 [^1]. For a city whose office stock, restaurants and hotels were built around a federal workforce, that is the starting fact of almost any demand case.

The Department of Employment Services publishes its own monthly count of jobs located in the District. Its release for August 2025 put the count at 751,000 [^14]. The release for June 2026 gave 721,800 [^15], and the release for August 2026 gave 713,600 [^16]. A later release can restate a month, so a study quotes each count with the release it came from rather than a level copied from a summary, and it keeps to one series from start to finish.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics gives two more readings. Its Economy at a Glance page for the District shows 719.9 thousand nonfarm jobs for September 2026, seasonally adjusted and preliminary [^17]. Its county wage program, which counts jobs covered by unemployment insurance rather than estimating from a survey, gives 715.8 thousand jobs for March 2026 at 51.4 thousand establishments, and an average weekly wage of $2,725 for the first quarter of 2026 [^3]. The four measures differ in method, adjustment and month, which is why they are listed by release below and not set against each other.

By sector, the September 2026 release counts 160,900 jobs in professional and business services, 125,500 in education and health services and 17,600 in information, each not seasonally adjusted [^18]. The sector a project leans on decides which of these lines its study watches. An office conversion, a Downtown lunch restaurant or a business hotel reads professional and business services, the line that holds the District's law, consulting and contracting firms. A medical office, a school or a child care center reads education and health services, which in the District includes the universities and hospital systems. A data or media tenant reads information.

Residents are counted apart from jobs. The same September 2026 release puts the District's resident civilian labor force at 402,900 and its employed residents at 380,400 [^18]. Those are people who live in the District, wherever they work; the job counts above are positions located in the District, whoever fills them and wherever that person lives. The distinction decides which series a study uses. A grocery, a child care center or an apartment building sells to residents, so its demand case starts from the resident series and the household figures in the register table below. An office building, a hotel or a restaurant that lives on weekday trade draws on the job count and on the commuters behind it, many of whom live in the Maryland and Virginia counties covered in the Washington metro section.

The register's household measures come from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey, in its five-year estimates covering 2020 to 2024, and from BEA's personal income account for 2024. The District is one county-equivalent as well as the seat of government, so its state and county values are the same numbers, and the four city submarkets on this page have no register value of their own. The Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 population estimate for the District was not read from a dated release for this page; a study takes it from the Census table and names the vintage.

The two income measures in the table are not the same thing. The survey's per capita income counts money income reported by residents over five years; BEA's per capita personal income adds items the survey does not ask about and covers a single calendar year. A retail or restaurant study that sizes spending from income says which one it used. The tenure counts are the base a multifamily study starts from, and the five-year gross rent median runs well behind the current asking rents a broker reports, so it describes the existing stock rather than what a new building can charge.

Cushman & Wakefield's MarketBeat for the third quarter of 2026 puts citywide office vacancy, all classes, at 23.5% and the full-service average asking rent at $55.15 per sf [^2]. Its own net absorption for the quarter was negative [^2]. CBRE's figures for the same quarter report 360,154 sf of positive absorption across the city, with 218,400 sf absorbed by Class A buildings [^7]. The two firms draw their inventories differently and measure absorption in their own ways, and CBRE's page does not print a vacancy level, so the readings do not form a single series and cannot be netted against each other.

For a lender the vacancy rate frames the rest of the file. A conversion study reads it to argue that a building has no better future as office; a ground-floor retail lease-up reads it to estimate weekday foot traffic; a hotel that counts on business travel reads it as a proxy for how many offices send visitors. In each case the study cites one firm's vacancy, rent and absorption together and says which firm it chose, rather than pairing one broker's positive absorption with another broker's vacancy rate.

The quarter's absorption by submarket for the Central Business District and the East End is in the Downtown section. Submarket vacancy and asking rent were not printed on the page read for this one, so a study for a particular building asks the broker for the submarket table that matches its citywide figure. Citywide space under construction, office cap rates and the square footage of federal leases ended or scheduled to end were not found in a dated, readable source either. Each is gathered from the broker or from the General Services Administration's lease records and carried with its date.

Two questions follow from the payroll record above. The first is tenancy: a building whose rent roll depends on a federal agency or on contractors to one carries a rollover risk that a vacancy average does not show, and a study lists each such lease with its expiry. The second is the exit: a lender underwriting an office acquisition in 2026 asks what the building is worth if it cannot be re-leased as office, and in the District the answer usually runs through the conversion program in the next section.

The District's policy answer to empty offices is a tax abatement for turning them into housing. By January 2026 ten conversions had advanced under the Housing in Downtown abatement since March 2024, producing 2,563 housing units, as Commercial Observer reported from the Mayor's announcement [^8]. In the same month two further projects, at 2121 Virginia Avenue NW in Foggy Bottom and 899 Maine Avenue SW near The Wharf, were conditionally awarded abatements for 811 homes, as Hoodline relayed the Mayor's release [^9,8]. Commercial Observer gives the abatement term as 20 years and describes an annual program cap that rises in steps to fiscal 2028 [^8].

In a District of Columbia feasibility study the abatement is a revenue line with a status attached. Because the program works within an annual cap, a project not yet awarded competes for a share of a fixed amount, and a pro forma that assumes the abatement states whether the award is conditional, final or only applied for, with its date. A lender will read a conditional award as a condition of the loan, not as income already in hand.

The record also contains single-building financings, among them the C-PACE package for The Geneva on Connecticut Avenue NW, which the Mayor's office called the District's largest office-to-residential conversion when construction began in January 2026 [^9,8]. Single transactions are not printed here as market figures. What the record does not give is the count of conversions completed and leased as of October 2026 or the abatement's dollar value for each project; both were not found, and a study of a converted building takes them from the developer's award letter and the District's own records.

A conversion study answers questions a ground-up apartment study does not. Can the floor plate, the window line and the core yield units that lease at the rents in the Washington metro section? How long will a vacant office sit while permits are drawn, and who carries it? What does the tenant purchase law in the next section do to a sale of the finished building? The DMPED count of 73,000+ housing units added in the District since 2015, a share of them affordable [^21], is the long-run supply each converted building joins.

Apartments and the RENTAL Act

The RENTAL Act, B26-0164, passed the Council on September 17, 2025 [^13] and was approved again on October 21, 2025 after a technical error [^22]. In Holland & Knight's summary of the bill as passed, it changes the Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act on points an apartment buyer underwrites. A building whose permanent certificate of occupancy is less than 15 years old is exempt from a TOPA offer of sale, though notice of the transfer is still required. Tenant organizations get cooling-off periods of 45 days in buildings of five or more units and 22 days in buildings of two to four units [^13]. The Washington Informer reported that an amendment exempting buildings of four or fewer units passed at the October vote [^22].

The date on which the law took effect after congressional review was not confirmed in the research for this page. A study written for an apartment purchase therefore states which TOPA regime it assumed, the old one or the one the Council passed, and the lender's counsel confirms it. The exemption matters most to a newer building, where it can shorten a sale by weeks; the cooling-off periods matter to an older building with an organized tenant association.

On July 10, 2026 the Mayor proposed two more changes. A Housing Investment Protection Act of 2026 would set up a purchase opportunity under which the District could buy newer buildings within a fixed window, and an Illegal Occupancy Enforcement Amendment Act of 2026 would let a short-term guest who stays unlawfully be removed as a trespasser [^21]. Both were proposals, not law, on the date of the release. An apartment study cites them as pending, and a hotel or short-term rental study notes the second.

Hotels, restaurants and retail

Hotels and other transient accommodations pay a combined sales and use tax of 15.95%, up from 14.95%, for the period from April 1, 2023 to March 30, 2027 set by the Office of Tax and Revenue's notice of February 13, 2023 [^10]. A hotel pro forma that runs past March 2027, as almost every one does, states the rate it assumes after that date and the authority for it.

The general sales tax increase that had been scheduled was postponed through September 30, 2027, according to the Chief Financial Officer's February 2026 revenue estimates [^23]. A restaurant, a shop or a grocery budgets the rate in force for its opening year and names the date on which the postponement ends.

Property tax and the rules not found

The Office of Tax and Revenue's real property rate page prints the commercial classes by assessed value but carries no tax year, so its rates are not printed here. A study takes the rate from the tax bill or the assessment notice for the year it models. Short-term rental registration, liquor licensing, child care licensing and any certificate of need for a health facility were not found in a dated source for this page. A study for a hotel bar, a restaurant, a child care center, a clinic or a senior housing project opens those files itself and cites the agency and the date it read them.

The labor line has a dated anchor: the District's average weekly wage of $2,725 for the first quarter of 2026 from the BLS county wage release [^3]. The District's federal declaration of 2026 was not a storm. A sewer line collapse led to a presidential emergency declaration for the District, EM-3643-DC, on February 20, 2026, with direct federal assistance under Public Assistance and an incident period beginning January 19, 2026, as Safeguard Properties relayed the FEMA declaration [^25]. The FEMA page itself did not load for the research, and the relay is a secondary source.

For a project the event bears on two inputs. Sewer capacity and the timing of a new connection are not things a site plan can assume, and a utility's capital program after a failure can change its schedules and charges. A study for a building that needs a new or larger connection asks DC Water for the capacity position on the parcel and dates the answer.

No major disaster declaration for the District in 2025 or 2026 was found. Flood zone acreage, figures from the District's hazard mitigation plan and dated counts of extreme heat days were not found either. A study therefore reads the FEMA flood panel for the parcel itself. That check matters most along the Anacostia and the Potomac, where the Capitol Riverfront, the Southwest Waterfront and parts of the wards east of the river meet the water, and it decides whether a lender will require flood insurance and what it costs.

Destination DC counted 27.2 million visitors to Washington, DC in 2025, who spent $11.9 billion, and put the jobs supported by that spending at 114,013 [^4]. The bureau's release calls 2025 a year of slight growth in visitation and a record for economic impact. For a hotel, a restaurant or an attraction those are the citywide demand totals; a study then narrows them to the submarket and the segment, business, leisure or group, that the subject competes for.

The bureau's research page also counts hotel rooms citywide and the rooms in the construction pipeline, but the page carries no publication date, so those counts are not printed here. Monthly hotel performance for 2026 was not found in a dated, readable release, and a January 2026 comparison would in any case be set against the inauguration month of 2025, which no study should treat as an ordinary base. The submarket readings the record holds are in the Downtown and NoMa sections.

The quarter's office record splits inside Downtown. In the third quarter of 2026 the Central Business District recorded net absorption of -42,500 sf, which Cushman & Wakefield attributes to Class B move-outs, while the East End absorbed 70,400 sf, led by Class A space [^2]. Taken together with the citywide figures in the office section, the two numbers are consistent with tenants leaving older buildings in the core while the newest buildings east of it gain occupancy.

That split is the argument a Downtown study has to settle for its own building. A Class A tower can point to the East End's quarter; a Class B building in the CBD faces the quarter that went the other way, and its owner is the one weighing the conversion program. A lender reads the class of the subject first and the citywide rate second.

The DowntownDC Business Improvement District's report on 2024 put hotel revenue in its area at $788 million, nearly one-third of the District's total, and projected Downtown's net fiscal impact on the city for fiscal 2025 at $645 million [^29]. Those describe 2024 and a projection made before the federal job losses in the economy section. A study of a Downtown hotel or restaurant uses them for the scale of the district and updates the operating figures from the BID's later report and the hotel's own STR competitive set.

Downtown is also the area the abatement in the conversions section is named for, and the Class B space behind the CBD's negative quarter is the kind of stock an owner weighs for conversion. A conversion study for a Downtown site reads both sections: the office record to show the building's office future is weak, and the apartment record to show the residential future is better.

BLS's Economy at a Glance page for the metro gives total nonfarm employment of 3,292.0 thousand for September 2026, a preliminary count without seasonal adjustment, with 676.6 thousand in government, 769.4 thousand in professional and business services, 318.4 thousand in leisure and hospitality and 78.5 thousand in information [^33]. WJLA, reporting BLS data on July 21, 2026, gave metro-wide federal employment as 375,800 to 312,500 from the start of 2025 to May 2026, its lowest level in about three decades [^6].

The metro counts matter to a District project because its customers cross the District line every working day. An office tenant draws staff from the whole metro; a hotel's weekday guests come to meet firms counted in the professional and business services line; a restaurant near a Metro station feeds commuters who live in Maryland or Virginia. When the metro's government and professional services payrolls shrink, a District study that relies only on resident counts misses part of its demand, and one that relies only on job counts misses where those workers spend the rest of the week.

Apartments: two brokers, two geographies

Apartment figures for Washington come from two brokers that draw the market differently. Matthews' Washington D.C. multifamily report for the second quarter of 2026 does not state its geography, whether the District, the metro or a wider region, so its figures are quoted here as Matthews' Washington figures and nothing narrower: an average rent of $2,227, -0.72% over the year; net absorption of 4,613 units in the quarter; 7,698 units delivered over the trailing twelve months, a pace the report shows slowing from 2024; and $8.24 billion in trailing twelve-month sales volume, up 44.5% [^11]. Yardi Matrix, which reports at the metro level, counted 4,101 units added in the first five months of 2026 [^34] and an average advertised asking rent of $2,191 for December 2025 [^12].

Matthews' $2,227 is also the dollar figure Yardi Matrix gives for its metro asking rent through May 2026 [^34], so the two reports should not be read as two independent confirmations of one rent. Neither describes the District alone. A District apartment study uses them for the direction of the regional market, deliveries slowing and sales volume returning, and builds its rent conclusion from buildings in the subject's own submarket, inside the District line, with the date each rent was read.

SBA's rule on studies is 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^35]. For District borrowers, as everywhere, SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates now governs both loan programs; it came into force on October 1, 2026 [^36]. Its coverage floor for a standard 7(a) loan is 1.15x, and a 504 borrower in business for two years or less, or one whose building is special-purpose, puts in 15% of the project cost [^36]. A District hotel or a new restaurant meets both tests on projections, which is where a study's market figures enter the credit file. For the way each test is carried through a pro forma, see SBA 7(a) and 504 lending.

SBA's fiscal 2025 results for its Washington-area district office were not found for this page, and no loan-level total for District addresses is printed here. A study that needs a District lending count takes it from SBA's loan-level data and states the fiscal years it covers.

The District's own capital is mostly housing and energy capital. DMPED's July 2026 release reports what the Housing Production Trust Fund has invested under the current Mayor [^21]; the Housing in Downtown abatement works through the property tax [^8]; DC Green Bank has financed residential solar together with City First Enterprises and Evergreen Energy Investments [^37]; and C-PACE carried part of the financing for The Geneva conversion [^8]. The District's SSBCI allocation, DC Green Bank's 2025 lending totals, CDFI awards and FDIC deposit totals were not found in a dated source.

USDA Rural Development's business guarantees are written for rural areas, and the research for this page found no program activity in the District. A sponsor whose site lies in an outer county of the metro reads that county's eligibility for itself; the USDA Rural Development page explains the program's tests. A bank or institutional lender reads the same vacancy, absorption and rent figures as an SBA lender but sizes the loan on its own coverage and leverage terms, as the page on conventional and institutional lending describes. An EB-5 offering counts the jobs a project creates, and the District's payroll and wage records in the economy section are where that job model starts.

Whichever of these readers holds the file, the study is one independent document. Feasibility Study Company, the District of Columbia feasibility study company behind this page, prepares lender-grade studies whether the capital is a 7(a) loan, a 504 debenture, a guarantee from USDA Rural Development, an EB-5 offering or a conventional loan, and the dated evidence on this page is the material each of them is built from.

The case studies include hotels financed under SBA programs and apartment buildings financed by agency lenders, the two property types most of this page's District records bear on. A District version of either would keep the case's structure and change three inputs: the payroll series from the economy section instead of a state count, a broker's submarket table instead of a statewide rate, and the building's TOPA status and abatement award instead of another jurisdiction's rules.
