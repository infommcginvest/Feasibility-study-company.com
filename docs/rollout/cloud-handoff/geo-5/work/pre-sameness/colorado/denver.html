Owners who look for a Denver feasibility study company usually need one document that their lender's credit officer will accept. Feasibility Study Company prepares independent, lender-grade feasibility studies for Denver-area projects, whether the money is a conventional bank loan, an SBA 7(a) or 504 loan, a USDA Rural Development guarantee or an EB-5 raise. In this metro the evidence starts with the county payroll, because the five counties this page covers moved in different directions over the year to March 2026.

In the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Colorado county tables for early 2026, Denver County showed 551,034 covered jobs in March 2026, down 1.3% from a year earlier, with an average weekly wage of $2,354, up 4.4% [^4]. Arapahoe County held 339,426 jobs, down 0.5%, at $1,929 a week, up 4.7%. Adams County held 242,964, down 0.8%, at $1,484, up 3.8%, and Jefferson County 240,128, down 1.5%, at $1,753, up 7.6% [^4]. Douglas County went the other way, with 147,131 jobs, up 1.0%, at $1,913 a week, up 5.0% [^4]. The release marks the Denver County count preliminary.

The split matters more to a lender than any metro total. A project whose customers or tenants work in Denver County draws on a county where covered employment fell while pay rose. A project in Castle Rock, Parker or Highlands Ranch sits in Douglas County, where the count grew. Rising pay with falling headcount cuts two ways in a pro forma: payroll cost per worker climbs for a hotel, restaurant or care operator, and the income behind a rent check or a retail ticket rises for the workers who kept their jobs. A study should carry both effects from the county the subject actually draws on, rather than blend the five.

Across the whole Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area, the Bureau's occupational survey put the mean hourly wage for all occupations at $39.28 in May 2025 [^12]. The latest metro output figure the research holds is $311,876.352 million of gross domestic product for 2023, from a FRED series that carries the label discontinued [^13]. It describes 2023 and gives the economy's scale; it is not a reading of growth since.

Visitors are the second leg of the base. Visit Denver counted a record 37.6 million domestic visitors to Denver in 2025, 20.1 million of them overnight and 17.6 million day visitors, and 73,200 tourism-supported jobs across the Denver metro area [^5]. The visitor counts are for Denver and the job count for the wider metro area, as the bureau reports them. The statewide tourism, population and state budget figures sit on our Colorado market overview, which this page does not repeat.

The Census Bureau's American Community Survey reads the household side on the register's geography, the ten-county metro area of Adams, Arapahoe, Broomfield, Clear Creek, Denver, Douglas, Elbert, Gilpin, Jefferson and Park counties. That area is wider than the five counties in the payroll table, so the two tables are read separately and never combined.

CBRE's downtown Denver report for Q3 2026 puts total vacancy at 40.1%, up 150 basis points on the quarter, with direct vacancy at 38.1% [^1]. Downtown net absorption was negative 483,000 square feet, and the average direct asking rent was $41.00 per square foot, full service gross, down 2.2% from a year earlier [^1]. A single move-out, TIAA leaving 293,000 square feet at 1670 Broadway, made up 61% of that quarter's downtown loss [^1].

CBRE's report for metro Denver shows the suburbs moving the other way in the same quarter: net absorption of positive 320,000 square feet outside downtown, which left metro net absorption at negative 161,000 square feet [^6]. The firm put the office construction pipeline at 461,000 square feet, down 36.4%, and placed almost all of it in Cherry Creek [^6].

Cushman & Wakefield measures the market on its own inventory and submarket lines. Its Q3 2026 MarketBeat puts the overall metro asking rent at $33.80 per square foot, full service gross, and counts 439,100 square feet under construction in Cherry Creek across four projects; it reports no metro-wide construction total [^9]. The two firms' figures are not mixed on this page. Each statement above uses one firm's series and names it, and a study should do the same, since a vacancy rate from one firm and an absorption figure from the other do not describe the same buildings.

For a lender these numbers sort office projects into three kinds. A downtown acquisition or refinance carries a lease-up assumption that has to hold in a district where occupied space was still shrinking in Q3 2026, so the study works from the building's own rent roll, its rollover dates and the tenants actually touring, not from a metro vacancy rate. A downtown conversion to housing leans on the same renter demand as every new apartment building in the metro, so it is tested against the multifamily figures below as well as against its floor plates and code path, as our note on office repurposing sets out. A suburban office or medical office project sits in the part of the market that absorbed space, but one positive quarter is not a trend; the study shows the subject's own submarket over several quarters. In Cherry Creek the first question is new supply rather than old vacancy, because nearly all of the space under construction is there.

Apartments: a thinner pipeline, rents not yet turned

CBRE's Denver multifamily figures for Q3 2026 show an average rent of $1,776 per unit, up 0.6% on the quarter and down 2.9% from a year earlier [^3]. Over the 12 months to Q3 2026 CBRE counted net absorption of 7,346 units and completions of 6,481 units, with the completion total down 12.0% from its Q2 2026 reading [^3]. Its average sale price was $231,212 per unit, down 2.3% from $236,593 the quarter before [^3].

Matthews Real Estate Investment Services, whose report charts credit CoStar, reads the quarter before. It put Denver multifamily vacancy at 5.74% in Q2 2026, down from 6.84% in Q1, counted net absorption of 5,902 units in the quarter, up 58.0% from a year earlier, and found 15,493 units under construction, the lowest in at least five years, with a cap rate of 5.36% [^2]. The Matthews quarter and property universe are not CBRE's, so the two firms' absorption counts are not read as one series.

The read for a multifamily loan is that the construction wave that held rents down is thinning, but rents had not turned on a yearly basis by Q3 2026. A pro forma that grows rent from its first year assumes a turn the CBRE series had not shown. A Denver feasibility study for an apartment project builds its lease-up from absorption in the subject's own submarket, states whether it relies on effective or asking rent (the gap between the two is the subject of a research note), and shows an exit value from the Matthews cap rate beside CBRE's per-unit pricing, each held to its own quarter.

Industrial: rising rents and a sales rebound

Cushman & Wakefield's Q3 2026 industrial MarketBeat puts the metro overall asking rent at $10.23 per square foot, triple net, up $0.26 on the quarter, and industrial sales volume at $364.7 million, up 80.5% from a year earlier [^10]. Matthews, reading Q2 2026 and only industrial and flex buildings of 5K to 200K SF, counted approximately 1.89 million square feet under construction in that small-bay segment [^11].

For an owner-user building financed under SBA 504, the small-bay count is the relevant competition, because a contractor's shop or a light manufacturer shops that segment rather than a large distribution building. A rising asking rent strengthens the lease-or-own comparison an owner-user study makes. The study still prices the subject against small-bay rents in its own submarket, which the metro averages do not give.

Retail: investment up, rent flat

CBRE's Q3 2026 retail figures put the average net asking rent at $20.28 per square foot, up 0.5% from a year earlier, and retail investment sales at $293.2 million, up 95.7% [^18]. This page carries no retail vacancy or availability figure, so a retail or quick-service restaurant study counts the vacant and planned space in its own trade area and reads its sales from the households there, as our note that the trade area is the study describes.

Denver's broker reports cut the metro along the lines a site study should use. Downtown is the district where vacancy rose in Q3 2026; Cherry Creek is where nearly all new office space is being built; and the suburbs, where CBRE recorded positive office absorption in the same quarter [^6], spread south along I-25 through Arapahoe County into Douglas County, west into Jefferson County and north and east into Adams County. A project's competition and its demand both come from the submarket it sits in, so a study names that submarket before it quotes any metro number.

The research holds no Colorado Department of Transportation count for any Denver corridor, so the page prints none. A fuel, convenience store, express car wash or quick-service site on I-25, I-70, I-225, C-470 or E-470 needs the state's count for the station nearest the parcel, with its year and its distance from the site stated. Two features of these roads change how a count reads. E-470 is a toll road, so its mainline volume includes drivers who reach a corner only by exiting and paying. And where the nearest station sits on a different road from the frontage, the study has to explain why the count still describes the site.

Much of what a Denver feasibility study consultant checks before the market work begins is jurisdictional. Denver is both a city and a county, so one government zones, permits and taxes a parcel there. In the other four counties the rules come from the city the parcel sits in, such as Aurora, Lakewood, Centennial, Thornton, Englewood or Castle Rock, or from the county where the land is unincorporated. Aurora reaches into Arapahoe, Adams and Douglas counties, so the county line alone does not settle which code applies.

The research found no Denver short-term rental or zoning change with an effective date, and no impact fee or tap fee schedule for any city in the five counties, so the page states none. Colorado's recent housing laws on density near transit, parking minimums and accessory dwellings reach local codes across the metro; their terms are set out on our Colorado page, and the study confirms with the city's planning staff how the subject's jurisdiction has applied them. Water and sewer service on the Front Range is provided by a patchwork of city utilities and special districts, so the study attaches a written will-serve letter and the tap fees quoted for the subject, rather than a per-unit allowance.

Investment sales are the clearest sign of lender and buyer appetite in the record: both the industrial and the retail sales totals in the supply section rose sharply over the year in Q3 2026. The record behind this page does not name the metro's most active SBA lenders or certified development companies, and the Census register carries no SBA loan count for the Denver metro area, so the page prints no lender ranking. A 504 sponsor should ask the development company it plans to use for that company's own Denver production record.

The SBA's rule on studies is 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^19]. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, a standard 7(a) loan is tested against a 1.15x debt service coverage floor, and a 504 project by a business two years old or less, or one for a special-purpose building, brings 15% equity [^20]. In Denver the coverage test is where the county payroll split shows up: a business whose customers come from a county that lost covered jobs needs its revenue case cut back before coverage is tested. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice sets out what an SBA lender asks the study to prove.

Apartment, office and industrial loans above SBA size go to banks, life companies, agencies and debt funds, whose committees test a study's absorption and rent against the broker series on this page (see conventional and institutional lending). An EB-5 raise for a Denver hotel or mixed-use project adds a job count to the study; the method is in counting EB-5 jobs.

None of the published cases is sited in these five counties. What the published worked cases do show is the order a Denver study would follow: a market finding first, then a loan size, then a coverage test and the conditions a lender attaches. Here the inputs would be the county payroll line, one broker's series for the property type, and the pipeline in the subject's submarket, each carried with its own quarter.

  1. The county behind the demand

    Which of the five counties the subject's customers or tenants come from, with that county's covered jobs and weekly wage, since Douglas grew while Denver, Arapahoe, Adams and Jefferson fell.

  2. One broker per office statement

    Vacancy, absorption and rent taken from a single named publisher for a single quarter, never a CBRE rate beside a Cushman & Wakefield count.

  3. Downtown lease-up from the rent roll

    For a downtown office loan or conversion, the building's own rollover dates and tenant prospects, tested against the downtown series rather than the suburban one.

  4. The Cherry Creek pipeline

    For an office project in Cherry Creek, the competing buildings under construction and their delivery quarters.

  5. Year-one apartment rent

    The starting rent against CBRE's year-over-year decline, and the quarter in which the pro forma begins rent growth, with the evidence for it.

  6. Two apartment quarters kept apart

    Matthews' Q2 2026 readings and CBRE's Q3 2026 readings cited separately, each with its own universe.

  7. Hotel performance from a dated report

    Occupancy, rate and RevPAR for the competitive set from a dated STR report, since no Denver hotel release sits behind this page.

  8. Jurisdiction, water and fees

    The city or county that zones the parcel, how it applies the state housing laws, a will-serve letter and the tap and impact fees quoted for the subject.

  9. The area behind every figure

    Whether each number in the study describes one county, the ten-county metro area or a broker's own metro Denver, labeled as such.
