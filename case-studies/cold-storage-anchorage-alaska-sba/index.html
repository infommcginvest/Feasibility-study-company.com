Cold Storage Feasibility Study Case: Anchorage, Alaska, SBA 504

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published November 4, 2026 · Data as of November 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Anchorage handled a record 3.82 million tonnes of air cargo in 2025 [8], yet the Census register lists no refrigerated warehousing establishment in the county. Cold storage is on SBA's special purpose list, so a new operator starts at the top equity tier, and even the 15.0% the sponsor proposes leaves Year 2 coverage at 0.81x; the floor is reached at 40.5%. Feasibility Study Company prepares cold storage feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A cold storage feasibility study consultant or company for an Anchorage, Alaska project would produce four things, and this page shows them for one hypothetical building financed with an SBA 504 loan: a market read, a supply count, a priced build and a coverage test. The subject is generic: an owner-operated refrigerated building of about 20,000 square feet on about 3.0 acres in the airport-area industrial corridor, with about 4,200 pallet positions, a dock, and two refrigerated trucks for deliveries to grocery and food service accounts. It is meant to serve three flows the market talks about: air cargo, seafood and the grocery supply chain. Market figures are dated public records. Market Conclusion

Anchorage is a real cold chain location. The public record cannot show how much of it a new building can win.

The location answer is yes. Ted Stevens Anchorage International Airport moved a record 3.82 million tonnes of cargo in 2025 [8], and the Don Young Port of Alaska moved a record 5.7 million tonnes [8]. A municipality of 289,221 residents [1] with 183.4 thousand nonfarm jobs [3] buys food every week, and it is the supply point for much of the rest of the state.

The volume answer is not available. The research found no dated figure for seafood landings or processing in Southcentral, none for the grocery tonnage that moves through Anchorage warehouses, and none for how much air cargo is stored locally and how much simply changes aircraft. Those three gaps are the center of the demand case, so this page turns each of them into a lender condition and does not paper over them with a number.

The supply answer is odd. The Census Bureau's County Business Patterns series, read through the site's market register, lists no refrigerated warehousing establishment in the Municipality of Anchorage or in the metro area (see the supply table below). That is either an empty market or a disclosure gap, and the page explains why the second reading is the more likely one. The SBA's loan records add a second fact: the program has financed no cold storage building in Alaska in the ten approval years to 2025, against a national count that is well above zero. A lender and a Community Development Corporation here would be pricing a building type they have not seen locally.

The financing answer follows. Under a 15.0% equity proposal the project costs $13,232,657, the debenture is $4,631,430 and Year 2 coverage is 0.81x against a floor of 1.15x. Restructured, the sponsor brings $5,352,390, 40.5% of cost, and coverage reaches 1.15x in Year 2 and 1.74x at stabilization. A building that earns well once full needs a large contribution to survive the fill.

Demand

A record cargo airport, a stable food-buying base and three volume gaps.

Cold storage demand follows product that must stay cold between a producer and a plate. Three flows matter in Anchorage: freight that passes through the airport, fish that comes out of Alaska waters, and the food that feeds the people who live and visit here. Air cargo

The cargo record is the headline and also the trap. Two reports give slightly different totals for 2025: 3.82 million tonnes in an Alaska Business account of the Anchorage Economic Development Corporation's 2026 forecast [8], and more than 3.85 million metric tons in Alaska Public Media's account of an airport system report [9]. Both are secondary relays, neither opened the underlying report, and they are two accounts of the same 2025 total. The more useful signal is direction. Cargo at the Anchorage and Fairbanks airports together ran 11% ahead of the same period a year earlier in the first half of 2026 [9], and Anchorage had handled nearly 2 million metric tons by June [9].

What none of the reports say is how much of that freight is stored in Anchorage. Aircraft that stop here to refuel or change crews carry cargo that never enters a local building. Perishable freight that does stop needs a refrigerated dock and short-term holding, which is a different service from a month of pallet storage. The research holds no share, no perishable tonnage and no handler volume, so the model does not take its occupancy from the cargo total. It takes it from a ramp assumption and asks the lender to see signed agreements first. The transportation sector gives a mild corroboration: the Economic Development Corporation forecast 450 added transportation jobs in 2026 after 550 in 2025 [8].

Seafood

The research could not retrieve a dated harvest, landing or processing-volume figure for Southcentral Alaska. The state fish and game harvest page returned an error, and the one seafood search in the research was for Kodiak and found nothing dated. The port record cargo of 5.7 million tonnes in 2025 [8] shows a busy marine gateway, but it covers all cargo and says nothing about frozen fish. A seafood-led storage business would be built on contracts with processors and exporters, and none of those are in the public record this study can use. The model therefore does not rely on seafood volumes. Frozen seafood accounts are one of the customer types a lender should see under contract.

The grocery supply chain and the people it feeds

The consumer base is stable rather than growing. The municipality had 289,221 residents on July 1, 2025 [1], inside a state of 738,737 [12]. The state added 1,649 people (0.2%) between 2024 and 2025: natural increase of 3,389 outweighed a net loss of 1,740 people to other states [12]. Population is therefore flat, and a new warehouse cannot count on growth to fill it. It has to win share from existing arrangements.

Household spending power looks mixed. Anchorage covered employment stood at 145,080 in March 2026, down 1.6% from a year earlier as printed, while the average weekly wage was $1,579, up 5.3% over the year [4]. The county held 8,754 establishments in the first quarter of 2026 [4]. Prices rose 3.3% over twelve months to August 2026 on an index of 282.910 [6]. A local consumer confidence reading was 40.5 out of 100 in July 2026 [7]. Every Alaska resident who applied could expect a 2026 dividend of $1,200, a $1,000 base plus $200 of energy relief, and nearly 600,000 people applied [18], which is a seasonal injection of cash into grocery spending. The military is a further buyer: the Economic Development Corporation expects about $2 billion of near-term investment at Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson and about 2,700 new personnel and families [7].

Visitors add a second, seasonal food market. Municipal tourism tax collections passed $57.5 million in 2025 [10], and Southcentral cruise capacity of 492,000 in 2025 was projected to rise to 526,000 in 2026 [10]. Hotels, restaurants and cruise provisioners buy chilled and frozen food in the same months. A tourism-heavy customer mix, however, concentrates a building's occupancy in a few months, and the model's annual occupancy averages over that swing.

Housing and tourism add texture. The municipality reported about 400 new dwelling units permitted in 2025 and 330 in the first half of 2026, and it puts the housing need tied to JBER at 2,500 homes over five years [11]. The average Anchorage home price was $481,000 in 2026 [7]. Statewide, a record tourism season brought 33,000 more visitors (1.1%), yet winter visitors numbered fewer than 400,000, down 5.5% [22]. Winter is the season in which a building depends on residents and not on visitors.

Headwinds

The labor market is soft. Anchorage nonfarm employment of 183.4 thousand in September 2026 was 1.0% below a year earlier, and the civilian labor force of 200.5 thousand sat below the 204.0 thousand of April [3]. The municipality's unemployment rate was 3.4% by the state's measure and 3.6% by the federal one in September, preliminary and from different vintages [2][3], against 4.3% for the state as a whole [13]. Statewide, the federal series shows seasonally adjusted nonfarm employment of 340.7 thousand, up 1.2% over twelve months [14], while the state's own count, not adjusted, rose by only 300 (0.1%), with federal employment down 1,300 and private employment up 2,200 [13]. Anchorage federal employment fell 8.4% in the first half of 2026, against a statewide decline of 11.7% [7]. One bright spot is that the same state release reports transportation, warehousing and utilities up 900 jobs [13]. Anchorage nonfarm employment was 185.3 thousand in August and the unemployment rate 3.1% [3]. Private employers added 30,293 jobs and lost 23,914 in the last quarter of 2025, a net gain of 6,379, after a net loss of 6,477 in the third quarter [15], so the state economy churns even when the headline is flat.

The state economy produced $73,634.8 million of output in 2025, up from $69,130.8 million in 2024 [17]. That is the base on which the project's customers operate, and it depends heavily on energy. The pipeline project now under discussion would need a peak workforce of about 6,600 workers for construction alone [16]. If it proceeds it would lift food demand for construction camps and compete with a cold storage operator for skilled labor and contractors; if it does not, the state loses a source of growth. The study treats it as an upside that a lender should not underwrite.

How to read the zero

The register counts every industry cell that has an establishment, and it treats a cell that is absent as a true zero. On that rule Anchorage holds no refrigerated warehousing establishment, and neither does the metro area. We do not believe the market is empty. A city that handles a record volume of air cargo and feeds the state's largest population is unlikely to have no refrigerated storage at all, and the Census Bureau withholds establishment cells where publishing them could disclose a single firm's data. The register's own state-level cell for Alaska is flagged for that reason: it carries a lower bound of zero with some establishments possibly withheld. The honest reading is that the published data show none, that withheld cells may hide some, and that private operators, grocery chains' own distribution centers and seafood processors with their own cold rooms may not appear as a separate class at all.

That last point matters most to the subject. Much cold storage in a market like this one sits inside larger operations: processors, grocers and freight handlers that store their own product. Their capacity competes with a third-party building without appearing in a competitor list. A new owner-operated building must win customers who either rent from an incumbent or build their own, and the census a lender requires should name which.

The industrial base and the customers

The proxy count of industrial and warehouse establishments in the table includes ordinary dry warehouses. It shows how small the local logistics stock is, and it suggests that a refrigerated building would be a rare specialty among them. On the customer side, the table counts grocery store establishments for the municipality and the metro area, with the paid employees of the municipality's grocery stores. A modest number of grocery buyers is both good and bad news for a delivery line: it concentrates the sales effort on a few accounts, and it makes one lost account a large share of revenue.

Site

The airport-area corridor suits the use. The parcel, power, flood and seismic facts are all unknown.

The assumed site covers about 3.0 acres in the industrial area near Ted Stevens Anchorage International Airport, and its parcel, zoning and utility territory are still to be confirmed. The research turned up no parcel identifier, flood zone, seismic class, electric provider, permafrost or soil report, or traffic count, so what follows describes the rules that bear on the site and the cost they could add.

Zoning and approvals

The municipality reported in July 2026 that commercial zoning review now takes 20 business days, down from more than 30 [11]. That is a process change stated in a press release, with no effective date printed, and it covers review time and not the building permit. A refrigerated warehouse is an industrial use; whether it is permitted by right on the parcel, and what the refrigerant storage and noise rules say, are questions for a written zoning determination. The model carries a soft-cost allowance of $650,000 that includes permits.

Power, gas and resilience

Power is the cost that separates a cold building from a dry one, and the research found no commercial electric tariff and no confirmation of which utility serves the corridor. The model assumes 40 kilowatt hours per square foot per year at $0.22 a kilowatt hour, both labelled assumptions. Gas matters too: the local gas utility said in October 2026 that it could fall short of supply for 18 days in the winter of 2026 to 2027 [20]. The report concerns gas supply and not electricity, and the research does not tie the two. It does show that an energy shock is a real planning scenario in Southcentral, and a lender should ask about backup generation, fuel storage and the product-loss clause in the insurance policy.

Hazards

The research found no dated earthquake, permafrost or flood-zone figure for Anchorage. A refrigerated building is heavy, depends on continuous power and holds product whose value is a multiple of the building's, so a structural and seismic report, a geotechnical report and a flood determination are all conditions of closing. Winter conditions affect the truck yard and the frost protection of the slab; the model carries them in site work.

Freight routes

A federal grant announced in October 2026 would revive a rail link to Port MacKenzie, across Knik Arm from Anchorage: $249 million of federal money, $62 million of local share and an earliest completion of 2029 [21]. It is one project, the research does not describe its cargo, and it could change how freight reaches Southcentral. A lender should ask whether it would add to or divert the flows a building in Anchorage depends on.

Land

The research holds no Anchorage industrial land evidence the study can print. The model carries land at a round $300,000 an acre as a planning figure, and an appraisal replaces it. A Phase I environmental assessment should be ordered before the site contract is signed, because an industrial parcel near an airport may carry a history of fuel or de-icing use.

Labor

The Anchorage mean hourly wage for all occupations was $36.14 in May 2025, and the transportation and material moving group held 17,980 jobs, the group a cold warehouse draws on [5]. The pool is large, but the airport and the military compete for it, and the pipeline project would add demand for construction labor if it proceeds [16].

Occupancy

The model opens the building at 80.0% of its positions in the stabilized year, passing through a thin first year. That is the assumption on which everything else turns. The air cargo record, the grocery base and the seafood economy are reasons to believe that demand exists, but the study cannot trace any of them to this building. The ramp assumes the operator signs anchor customers before opening and fills the remainder over three years. In Year 3 the three lines produce $3,360,840 of revenue, rising to $3,601,080 by Year 5.

Rates and the delivery line

The storage charge is $42 per occupied position per month and the handling charge $24 per pallet moved, held flat for five years. The delivery line charges $105 a stop and grows to 8,200 stops in the stabilized year. Delivery adds revenue and also adds drivers, trucks and fuel, so it is a smaller margin than storage. It is in the model because the brief for the subject is an owner-operated building that distributes as well as stores, and because a delivery line makes the operator a service provider to grocers and not just a landlord of cold space.

The 504 program funds a project in three layers, and the rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [23]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the project is both [23]. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built, and its example list names Amusement parks, Bowling alleys, Car wash businesses, Cemeteries, Cold storage facilities where more than 50% of total square footage is equipped for refrigeration, Dormitories, Farms, including livestock and dairy facilities, Funeral homes with crematoriums, Gas stations, Golf courses, Hospitals, surgery centers, urgent care centers, and other health or medical facilities, Hotels, motels, and other lodging facilities, Marinas, Mines, Nursing homes, including assisted living facilities, Oil wells, Quarries, including gravel pits, Railroads, Sanitary landfills, Service centers (e.g., oil and lube, brake, or transmission centers) with pits and in-ground lifts, Sports arenas, Swimming pools, Tennis clubs, Theaters and auditoriums, Wineries [23]. A new operator in a refrigerated building therefore starts at the top tier.

The sponsor in this case proposes 15.0%, which is the middle tier and below the required 20.0%. The debenture of $4,631,430 would sit under the program limit at that equity, so the size test passes; the equity tier and the coverage test fail. Restructured to 40.5%, the debenture falls to $1,255,499 and the bank lends $6,607,889, 50.0% of cost. Maturities of 10, 20 or 25 years are available, set by the larger asset category. Real estate makes up most of the proceeds in this budget, which leads the model to the longest debenture and an equal bank amortization.

The owner-occupancy test is not a constraint on this subject. Occupancy by the operating borrower has to reach 60% of a building it constructs and 51% of one it buys [23]. An owner-operated cold storage building is occupied by its operator in full, which is one reason the structure suits the program. A lender will still want the operating company and the real estate holder identified and, if they are separate entities, the lease between them.

The debenture rate of 6.90% is a planning assumption, since the research holds no 2026 pricing and the sale of the debenture fixes the rate. The bank rate is 7.50%: Prime was 7.00% in the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [25], plus half a point for a start-up special purpose building, and the third-party spread is capped by SBA at 6% over New York Prime [23]. The model charges the FY2027 upfront fee, 0.50% of the debenture, and adds it to project cost [24]. The notice's waiver line reads: both 504 fees waived for the same three categories, including 504 refinance loans [24]. Its food supply chain category lists refrigerated warehousing and storage. The model does not take the waiver, because it depends on the operating company's classification code, and a lender or CDC would need to confirm it. If it applies, project cost falls by the fee. The jobs standard is one job per $95,000 of debenture for loans approved on or after October 1, 2025 [23]. The CDC tests the staffing plan against the restructured debenture, and that count is a lender condition.

A second financing route exists in the state. The Alaska Small Business Development Center reports a state allocation of $54M to loan guarantees under the State Small Business Credit Initiative, a program running ten years from 2022 [19]. The research could not tell whether the guarantees are available to a building of this kind, and a lender should ask. Whether a study is called for is left to the lender: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [26]. On a ground-up special purpose project by a start-up borrower in a state with no local cold storage loan record, a lender may well ask for one, and our cold storage hub explains how the studies are built.

Risks

What could break the Anchorage projection.

  1. The anchor customers do not sign

    The occupancy ramp is an assumption and no volume record in the public data reaches this building. If processors, grocers and freight handlers keep storing their own product or renting from an incumbent, the building fills slowly and fails the Year 2 test.

  2. The supply picture is wrong

    The register shows no refrigerated warehousing establishment in the county, but withheld cells and in-house cold rooms may hide competitors. A named census could show a market with more capacity than the data suggest.

  3. Air cargo is a transit market

    The cargo record of 3.82 million tonnes [8] is a total for the airport and not a measure of stored freight. If most of it never enters a building, the cargo leg of the demand case is thin.

  4. A flat population and a soft labor market

    The state grew by 1,649 people in 2025 [12], Anchorage jobs were down 1.0% over the year [3], and federal jobs in the municipality fell 8.4% in the first half of 2026 [7]. A grocery customer base that is not growing gives a new building no tailwind.

  5. Energy cost and reliability

    Power is the largest cost after payroll, and the model's rate is an assumption. A gas utility warned of a possible 18-day shortfall in the coming winter [20]. A cold building without backup power risks product loss and insurance disputes.

  6. Construction cost and labor

    No Anchorage cost index was found, and a pipeline project would draw about 6,600 workers at its peak [16]. If it proceeds, bids for a refrigerated building could run above the assumption, and every dollar of overrun adds debt the building can only just carry.

  7. A first-of-kind loan file

    Alaska recorded no cold storage loan under either SBA program in the ten approval years to 2025. A lender and a CDC without local precedent may ask for more equity, a longer reserve or a different structure than the engine produces.

Lender Conditions

Conditions before commitment.

The items below are questions the public record left open, and several bundle things the research reported as not found.

  1. Named competitor census

    A list of named competitors with their websites or assessor records, covering third-party cold storage, grocery and seafood in-house capacity, and freight handlers with refrigerated docks. The register's zero count is not a substitute.

  2. Signed customers

    Letters of intent or storage agreements covering the anchor share of positions in the first year, from at least the grocery, seafood and air cargo customer groups, with rates and terms. This replaces the occupancy and storage charge assumptions.

  3. Volume evidence

    Dated seafood landing and processing volumes for Southcentral Alaska, grocery tonnage moved through Anchorage, and the share of airport cargo that is stored locally, each from a primary source. The research found none.

  4. Site, title and zoning

    The municipal assessor's parcel number, a written zoning letter for a refrigerated warehouse, a survey and a Phase I environmental assessment.

  5. Power and resilience

    A utility rate letter and service capacity confirmation, a refrigeration engineer's load estimate, a backup power plan with fuel storage, and an insurance quote that includes spoilage cover.

  6. Seismic and geotechnical

    A structural and seismic report, a geotechnical report and a flood determination for the parcel; the research found no dated figure for any.

  7. Construction cost

    A contractor's bid for the insulated shell and a refrigeration vendor's quote, with a statement of how the pipeline workforce demand affects the schedule [16].

  8. Program mechanics

    The CDC's identity and rate quote, the operating company's classification code and whether the 504 fee waiver in the FY2027 notice applies [24], the structure of the operating company and real estate holder, and confirmation that the building meets the special purpose property definition.

  9. Operating costs

    Wage quotes from the trade area for warehouse and driver roles, the municipal mill rate and assessment ratio for commercial property, and a quote for the refrigerated trucks.

Anchorage Questions

What lenders ask about the Anchorage cold storage case.

Does Anchorage have enough demand for a new cold storage building?

The location has the ingredients: a record 3.82 million tonnes of air cargo in 2025 [8], a municipality of 289,221 residents [1] and a busy port [8]. What the public record lacks is any dated figure for seafood volumes, grocery tonnage or the share of airport cargo that is stored locally. A feasibility study therefore turns those three gaps into signed-customer conditions and does not claim a demand number the data cannot support.

Why does the supply table show zero cold storage establishments?

The register's County Business Patterns series publishes no refrigerated warehousing cell for the municipality or the metro area, and it treats an absent cell as zero. The Census Bureau withholds cells that could disclose a single firm, and in-house cold rooms at processors and grocers may not appear as a class. The zero is a published-data reading and not proof that no one competes, so the lender conditions ask for a named census with each competitor's own website or assessor record.

Is cold storage special purpose property under the SBA 504 rules?

SBA's example list of special purpose property names Amusement parks, Bowling alleys, Car wash businesses, Cemeteries, Cold storage facilities where more than 50% of total square footage is equipped for refrigeration, Dormitories, Farms, including livestock and dairy facilities, Funeral homes with crematoriums, Gas stations, Golf courses, Hospitals, surgery centers, urgent care centers, and other health or medical facilities, Hotels, motels, and other lodging facilities, Marinas, Mines, Nursing homes, including assisted living facilities, Oil wells, Quarries, including gravel pits, Railroads, Sanitary landfills, Service centers (e.g., oil and lube, brake, or transmission centers) with pits and in-ground lifts, Sports arenas, Swimming pools, Tennis clubs, Theaters and auditoriums, Wineries [23]. A new business in special purpose property starts at the top equity tier of 20% [23]. The case engine then raises the contribution further if Year 2 coverage falls short of the floor.

Has SBA financed cold storage in Alaska before?

Not in the ten approval years to 2025. The register records no 504 loan and no 7(a) loan to a cold storage business in Alaska in that window, while the nation recorded loans in both programs. That makes the file a first of its kind locally, which a lender and a Community Development Corporation will weigh.

Is a feasibility study mandatory for a cold storage loan?

Not as a blanket rule: the regulation reads 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [26], and the research note on that wording explains why "may" falls short of "must". A ground-up special purpose building with a start-up borrower is the kind of file where a lender asks, and a study that counts the field and tests coverage answers the request early.

What is the biggest risk to an Anchorage cold storage loan?

Filling the building. Occupancy and the storage charge are assumptions in this case, and the sensitivity table shows Year 2 coverage moving from well below the floor to well above it across their ranges. Energy supply is the second risk: a gas utility warned in October 2026 of a possible 18-day shortfall in winter [20].
