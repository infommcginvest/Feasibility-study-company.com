Urgent Care Feasibility Study Case: Elizabethtown, Kentucky, SBA 7(a)

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published October 29, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Elizabethtown grew 12.9% between April 2020 and July 2025 [1], enough to support another urgent care clinic on Ring Road, but three of the four sites confirmed in the city belong to two health systems [13][14][15]. A 7(a) loan with 10.0% equity covers Year 2 debt service only 0.57x; the model supports $1,826,910 of debt at 54.4% equity.

Study at a Glance

A new walk-in clinic on the Ring Road corridor, tested against the SBA 7(a) rules.

Feasibility Study Company prepares studies like this one for owners, developers and their lenders: a dated market read, a competitor census, a priced build and a coverage test that says how much debt a clinic can carry. This page runs that work for one hypothetical project, a new independent urgent care clinic in Elizabethtown, Kentucky, financed with an SBA 7(a) loan. Ask us to run the same work on your site.

The subject is generic: a single-story clinic of about 4,500 square feet with on-site X-ray, on about 1.25 acres of commercial land along Ring Road. Every market figure is public and dated. Every model figure below is computed by the case engine from the inputs listed near the end, each of which carries either a source or the word assumption. The study belongs to our urgent care practice, the town sits in Kentucky, and the program rules are laid out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

Market Conclusion

Elizabethtown can use another clinic. The loan can carry about half of what was asked.

The market answer is a qualified yes. Elizabethtown counted 35,448 residents on July 1, 2025, up from an estimates base of 31,388 in April 2020, a gain of 12.9% [1]. Hardin County, the larger area around it, grew only 2.5% over the same span, to 113,482 [1]. The city is where the growth is, and the Ring Road corridor is where the city's retail and clinics are gathering. Fort Knox sits north of town with a large payroll, and the county holds a large veteran population [1][3][4].

The competitive answer is less comfortable. Four urgent care sites in the city could be confirmed on an operator's own page, and three of the four belong to two health systems, Baptist Health and Norton Healthcare [13][14][15]. The fourth is an independent regional chain [16]. A further Baptist Health clinic at the west end of Ring Road has been announced, but its own page could not be confirmed for this study. A large veterans outpatient clinic is also being built on the same corridor [9][12]. It will not run a walk-in service, but it will absorb part of the veteran primary care that otherwise reaches a clinic like the subject.

The financing answer follows from both. A newcomer has to buy its volume in a market where the established brands have a hospital and its referral network behind them [19]. The model ramps the clinic to 38 walk-in visits a day by Year 3, a modest share of the county, yet the first two years lose money or barely cover it while fixed staffing runs from the first day. Under the sponsor's 10.0% equity proposal the project costs $4,057,711 and asks for a $3,651,940 loan at 10.00%. Coverage in the Year 2 test is 0.57x against a floor of 1.15x [28]. The proposal fails.

Restructured, the clinic carries $1,826,910 of debt and the sponsor puts in $2,179,472, 54.4% of project cost. That structure meets the floor in Year 2 and reaches 2.06x at stabilization. It meets the program's tests on these numbers, but it is a different deal from the one proposed. The sensitivity table later on this page shows how thin the Year 2 margin is: a modest shortfall in visits or in collected revenue per visit undoes it.

Demand

A growing city, a military county and a payer base the research could not see.

Urgent care demand follows people, not traffic: households within a short drive, workers who need care on a lunch hour or after a shift, and families without a same-day appointment elsewhere. Elizabethtown's base has four parts: a city adding residents quickly, a county with a large veteran population, the largest military installation in the region, and a sizable health care economy, whose 2022 Economic Census receipts appear in the table. A city that is filling in

The city grew far faster than the county around it, which barely moved over the same five years [1]. A household in town averages 2.31 persons, against 2.44 for the county [1], a smaller household than the county's. One in four residents of the city is under 18 [1], and children are a dependable source of walk-in visits: ear infections, sprains and strep arrive on evenings and weekends, when a pediatrician's office is closed. At the other end, 14.9% of city residents and 17.2% of county residents are 65 or older [1], a group that also uses same-day care but is more likely to be steered to a primary care office or a hospital.

Income is middle-market. The city's median household income of $60,760 sits below the county's $67,647 [1], and the per capita figures follow the same order, $35,019 against $37,638 [1]. For a clinic whose revenue depends on what insurers allow, income matters less than coverage. The research found Census series for uninsured and poverty rates, but it labels their vintage only as the latest available, so this study does not print them. The payer mix of the trade area is therefore a lender condition, and the model's revenue per visit says plainly that it is an assumption.

Fort Knox and the veteran base

Fort Knox covers 109,000 acres, serves a daytime workweek population of about 26,000 and receives upwards of 15,000 more visitors a day, and the Army ranks it 20th in population among 174 installations [3]. A regional public radio report put its workforce at 22,000, the largest employer in the region, and its 2024 economic impact at $5.6 billion, including a payroll of $1.3 billion [4]. During the federal shutdown of October 2025 that workforce was in limbo [4], a reminder that a military payroll is steady but not immune to Washington.

The veteran count is the sharper demand signal. Elizabethtown has 3,152 veterans and Hardin County 12,344 [1]. The independent competitor in town says it accepts Medicare, in-state Medicaid, TRICARE and VA coverage [16], which tells a new entrant what the payer panel has to look like. A veteran with VA eligibility may use the new VA clinic for primary care once it opens, and the walk-in clinic then competes for the acute visits that fall between appointments.

A volatile industrial neighbor

The region's other large employer is less steady. The BlueOval SK battery plant at Glendale produced its first batteries in August 2025 and was expected to lay off roughly 1,600 workers in December 2025, with Ford planning to bring energy-storage capacity online within 18 months and expecting 2,100 jobs at the restart [5]. A layoff notice filed on February 12, 2026 deferred a further reduction of about 10% to March 31, 2026, and the $11.4 billion joint venture behind the plant was being dissolved [6]. For an urgent care clinic the effect runs two ways. Laid-off workers typically lose employer insurance and become self-pay or Medicaid patients, and the plant's occupational medicine business, if any of it was going to local providers, is on hold. The model therefore keeps its employer-contract line small.

Where the hospital fits

Baptist Health Hardin is the regional hospital in town and describes a catchment that reaches well beyond the county, across central Kentucky [19]. Its emergency department is the alternative for any patient with an after-hours need and the means to wait. The health system also runs urgent care sites in town [13][14], and that arrangement gives its clinics a referral path the subject lacks. It is the main competitive fact in the file.

What the field tells a newcomer

Three of the four in-city sites are health-system clinics, and one of the two systems that own them, Baptist Health, also owns the area's main hospital and its referral relationships [13][14][15][19]. A patient told by a primary care office to go to urgent care is plausibly pointed to one of those sites, where the record stays inside the same system. An independent clinic has to win the patient who finds it by search, by drive-by or by habit. That is a smaller pool, but it is not an empty one: CareFirst, the one independent chain in town, publishes a long review history [16], which points to steady patient traffic.

Every operator in the table is open on weekends, and the Ring Road and Radcliff clinics of Baptist Health and Norton's center offer online check-in or a queue, so a subject with ordinary hours and no queue tool would start behind [13][15][17]. The posted prices in the research are few. Norton states a self-pay range and says labs and X-ray cost extra [15]; Baptist Health, CareFirst and the Radcliff clinic post nothing [13][14][16][18]. A newcomer cannot undercut a price that is not posted, so it competes on wait time, hours and the insurance panels it holds.

Listings the study does not rely on

Three more names turned up only on aggregator pages and stay out of the table. An independent called Acutecare of Elizabethtown appears on a scheduling site with no confirmed operating status. A grocery chain's retail clinic is listed at more than one site, but retail clinics treat a narrower set of conditions than an urgent care center. And an aggregator record for a clinic on Ring Road is contradicted by Norton's own page, which places its center at the Towne Centre, so that record is treated as stale. The research also records an announcement by Baptist Health of an additional occupational medicine and urgent care clinic at the west end of Ring Road; the operator's own page could not be confirmed here, so it enters the lender conditions as a clinic to verify. If it is open, the in-city field holds one more health-system clinic.

New supply

No new urgent care permits or planning commission agenda items for 2025 or 2026 were identified. The one large pipeline item is the veterans outpatient clinic: 62,428 square feet, leased to the VA, with primary care, radiology, audiology, optometry, physical therapy, pharmacy, mental health and a lab, awarded in August 2025 [9]. It does not offer walk-in urgent care. A federal notice in January 2026 placed it on Ring Road [11], and a groundbreaking has been held, with opening planned for spring or summer 2027 [12]. A clinic opening the same year would face a veteran population with a new primary care option.

Site

A clinic is allowed by right in the commercial districts. The parcel itself is still unknown.

The subject is a hypothetical pad of about 1.25 acres on the Ring Road corridor, in a commercial district that permits a clinic. Which district the parcel lies in is a condition to confirm. The research could not retrieve a parcel identifier, flood zone, utility territory or zoning district from the county property valuation office, FEMA or the city's mapping tools, so the paragraphs below describe the rules that would apply and what each would cost.

Zoning and approvals

Under the city code, a clinic is a permitted use by right in both the C-2 Neighborhood Commercial and the C-3 Regional Commercial districts [20][21]. In C-2, a drive-through facility needs board approval, so a clinic with a pickup window would face a conditional use there [21]. A correctly zoned site needs development-plan review and a certificate of occupancy before use. The C-3 code sets a parking ratio for medical and dental clinics by floor area [20]; the site plan translates it into a stall count. A rezoning adds a planning commission hearing and two council readings. The nearby veterans clinic shows how that can go: the commission rejected the rezoning on a split vote in July 2025, the city council approved it in August, and the federal contract followed about a week later [9][10].

Frontage and traffic

Ring Road (KY 3005) is the city's northern bypass, meeting US 31W (N. Dixie Hwy) and KY 251, and the state has an extension to a new I-65 interchange in design [8]. The Kentucky Transportation Cabinet publishes a count map for Elizabethtown, but the station values did not extract, so no Ring Road count is in the file. As a substitute, I-65 carried 48,800 vehicles a day south of KY 222 and 53,700 north, approaching Elizabethtown, in 2022 [7]. That is highway traffic, not the corridor's, and it is a substitute only; a station count for Ring Road at the site is the first traffic condition.

Utilities

Hardin County Water District No. 2 publishes a tariff, issued March 25, 2025, that sets a tap fee of $1,600 for a standard 5/8 by 3/4 inch meter and charges larger meters at actual labor and material cost [22]. Whether the district serves the parcel is a condition to confirm. A clinic with a fire line or irrigation may need more than a standard meter, so $1,600 is a floor. The city's engineering page states a sewer connection fee per business tenant, without a date [23]. Natural gas comes from the city's own municipal utility [24]. Electric service could come from Kentucky Utilities or from Nolin RECC, whose territory is set by the state commission [25]; which serves the parcel is unknown. All of these fees are small beside the site work allowance in the model, and the real exposure is a line that does not reach the parcel.

Property tax, flood and environment

The city publishes a real property rate for tax year 2025, and a second source gives a slightly different figure [26]. The county and the school districts publish their own rates, but a combined rate for any parcel was not found as a single number, and neither was the assessment ratio. The model therefore uses a planning level for property tax and the actual levy is a lender condition. The flood zone for the subject was also not found; the city publishes a flood zone map application that should be read for the parcel. A clinic that handles medical waste and sits among other commercial uses should carry a Phase I environmental assessment regardless.

Revenue is net patient revenue, what the clinic collects, not what it bills. Two lines carry it: walk-in visits and occupational medicine for employers. The research holds no payer mix, no allowed amounts and no collection rate, so both lines are built from assumptions, each explained in the inputs table.

Walk-in volume

The model starts below its plateau, ramps through Year 2 and reaches 38 walk-in visits a day by Year 3, then holds it. The reasoning is share, not capacity. Hardin County has 113,482 residents and the city 35,448 [1], and a clinic that sees 38 patients a day needs only a small fraction of them in a year. But no competitor in the research publishes visits or exam rooms, so the share a newcomer can take is a guess, and the independent competitor's long review history [16] is the only evidence that a stand-alone clinic can build a durable flow here. The ramp assumes the subject opens with a credentialed provider panel and online check-in, because the systems around it offer both [13][15]. Payer credentialing runs ahead of revenue, which is why pre-opening and the working capital reserve are sized as they are.

Collected revenue per visit

The model carries $125 per walk-in visit, blended across payers and including X-ray and lab. That is a planning figure. The only price in the file is Norton's published self-pay range, which excludes labs and X-ray [15], and a self-pay patient is usually the least profitable payer. The figure is the most important assumption in the model after volume, and the sensitivity table moves it 15% either way.

Occupational medicine

Fort Knox and the region's plants employ the kind of workforce that needs pre-employment screening, injury care and return-to-work visits [3][4]. The model assumes 6 such visits a day by Year 3 at $95 each. It is a small line beside walk-in revenue, because the plant layoffs removed one buyer for now [5][6] and the model signs no contract it cannot show.

In Year 3 these lines produce $1,941,800 of revenue, rising to $2,067,725 by Year 5 as the occupational line and the ramp finish.

Labor is the largest cost and the one that does not flex with volume. The model keeps one advanced practice provider on duty for 12 hours a day, every day, at $65.00 an hour before payroll costs, plus a flat supervising physician fee. Behind the provider it staffs 3 support employees each hour at $24.00, because a clinic with an X-ray room needs a technician as well as a medical assistant and a front desk. A manager and 20.0% for payroll taxes and benefits complete the staff. No competitor publishes its staffing, and no Kentucky wage series is in the research, so all of these are planning figures.

The result is a cost base that barely moves between Year 1 and Year 3. Operating costs run $1,365,581 in Year 1 and $1,530,775 in Year 3, while revenue grows from $1,255,600 to $1,941,800. That arithmetic is why the ramp decides the loan: the clinic pays a full week of staff from the first day, and only the variable lines, supplies and billing, move with visits.

Property tax is a planning level of 1.10% of land, building and site cost; the actual levy is a lender condition [26]. Utilities, insurance, software, marketing and repairs are allowances. The model reserves 5.0% of equipment cost each year for replacement of the X-ray unit and analyzers. Net operating income is ($109,981) in Year 1, $229,508 in Year 2 and $411,025 in Year 3.

The loan is tested under SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, in force for applications received from October 1, 2026 [28]. A start-up puts in at least 10% of project cost, and its projected coverage must reach 1.15x within two years, which is why Year 2 is the test year here. A single 7(a) loan tops out at $5 million, and the sponsor's 10.0% equity proposal sits well under that line. Because land and building are more than half of the proceeds, the whole loan can run 25 years.

The rate is Prime of 7.00% on the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [30] plus the maximum variable-rate spread SBA allows at this loan size, 10.00% in all. A lender may price lower, and the final rate is set at pricing. SBA guarantees 75.0% of a loan this size [27], and the FY2027 upfront guaranty fee follows the schedule in the program's fee notice [29].

A clinic business is also a going concern, and its value rests on cash flow, not on the building alone. Our hub on urgent care feasibility explains why payer contracts and credentialing, not square footage, set that value. Whether a feasibility study is needed is decided case by case: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [31]. On a ground-up medical project with a start-up borrower, a lender may ask for one.

The proposal fails the test the program applies to a start-up, and the failure does not come from weak demand. It comes from asking a business with a long ramp to carry a mortgage-sized loan on day one.

The engine restructures by raising equity until Year 2 coverage meets the floor. At 54.4% equity, debt service falls to $199,214 and Year 2 coverage reaches 1.15x, rising to 2.06x in Year 3. That is a loan of $1,826,910, a little over half of what the sponsor asked for. It clears the floor by a hair in the test year, so a lender should treat it as the upper bound, not a target.

There are three ways to reach a structure like this, and the lender should ask which one the sponsor intends. The sponsor can bring the cash. The sponsor can lease a building instead of building one, which removes land and construction from the budget and turns them into rent; this case does not model a lease, and a clinic in an existing space would need its own study. Or the sponsor can find evidence that volume ramps faster than the model assumes, such as signed employer contracts or an acquired patient panel. What does not work is the original request.

Risks

What could break the Elizabethtown projection.

  1. The ramp is slower than modelled

    With three of four in-city sites owned by health systems [13][14][15], a newcomer may take longer than a year to build a steady flow. The break-even table shows how little room the stabilized year leaves, and Year 2 carries the test.

  2. The payer mix is unknown

    The research found no payer mix, no allowed amounts and no commercial credentialing timeline. If the trade area leans toward Medicaid and self-pay, collected revenue per visit falls below the model, and the sensitivity table shows what a one-step shortfall in collected revenue per visit does.

  3. The veterans clinic changes the mix

    The VA clinic, opening in 2027 [9][12], will give the county's 12,344 veterans [1] a primary care home. It may reduce the acute visits that a walk-in clinic otherwise captures, or it may refer overflow to nearby urgent care. The research cannot tell which.

  4. The industrial employer stays idle

    The battery plant's layoffs and the dissolution of its joint venture [5][6] cut local household income and insurance coverage. If the energy-storage restart slips past its planned 18 months [5], the self-pay and Medicaid share grows and the occupational line stays empty.

  5. Federal payroll is not immune

    The 22,000-person workforce at Fort Knox was in limbo during the October 2025 shutdown [4]. A repeat would hit the region's insured, steadily employed patients first.

  6. Building cost and land cost run high

    Both are assumptions. Every dollar of overrun at the restructured equity share adds debt the clinic can only just carry, so the contingency should not be spent on scope. An appraisal below the contract price also shrinks what a lender will advance.

  7. Provider supply and credentialing

    A clinic open seven days needs at least one provider on duty at all times. A vacancy closes the clinic or forces agency staff at higher cost, and a delay in payer credentialing postpones revenue while payroll runs.

Elizabethtown Questions

What lenders ask about the Elizabethtown urgent care case.

Is Elizabethtown too crowded for an independent urgent care clinic?

It is contested, not closed. Four urgent care sites in the city were confirmed on operators' own pages, and three belong to Baptist Health or Norton Healthcare [13][14][15]. The fourth, CareFirst, is an independent chain that accepts Medicare, in-state Medicaid, TRICARE and VA coverage [16], so an independent can hold a place. The city's 12.9% growth since 2020 [1] is the reason a newcomer can plan for volume, and the competitor census is the reason the model does not plan for much.

Does the new VA clinic help or hurt a walk-in clinic?

Both effects are possible, and the research cannot size either. The clinic offers primary care, radiology, pharmacy and mental health but no walk-in urgent care [9], and it is planned to open in spring or summer 2027 [12]. It may pull veterans away from after-hours visits to a clinic like the subject, or it may send overflow to nearby urgent care. The model keeps its volume assumption modest.

What happens to demand if the battery plant stays idle?

Household incomes and insurance coverage in the region suffer. The plant's layoffs of roughly 1,600 workers in December 2025 came with the dissolution of its $11.4 billion joint venture [5][6], and the Ford energy-storage restart is tied to a planned 18-month timeline [5]. An idle plant raises the share of self-pay and Medicaid patients and leaves the employer-contract line empty, which is why the model keeps that line small.

Why does the restructured loan need so much more equity than the program minimum?

Because the minimum is a floor, not a recommendation. A start-up puts in at least 10% of project cost [28], but it must also show projected coverage of 1.15x within two years [28]. A clinic with fixed seven-day staffing earns too little in Year 2 to carry a loan sized to the minimum, so the engine raises equity until coverage meets the floor. The coverage table on this page shows both structures.

Does SBA require a feasibility study for a clinic loan?

SBA does not require one in every case. The rule says that 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [31]. On a ground-up medical project with a start-up borrower, a lender may ask for one anyway, and a study that builds the competitor census and the coverage test answers the question before it is asked.

What could stop the project before any loan question arises?

Site facts the research could not find: the parcel's zoning district, its flood zone and the electric provider that serves it. A clinic is a permitted use by right in the city's C-2 and C-3 districts [20][21], but a different district or a drive-through feature changes the approval path, and the water district's tap fee of $1,600 for a standard meter [22] is a floor, not a quote.
