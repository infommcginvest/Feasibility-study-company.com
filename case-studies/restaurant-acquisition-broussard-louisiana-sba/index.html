Restaurant Feasibility Study: Buying a Full-Service Restaurant in Broussard, Louisiana, with an SBA 7(a) Loan

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published October 29, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

A buyer agrees to pay $1,000,000 for a hypothetical operating full-service restaurant and its building on the Broussard corridor and asks for a 7(a) loan with the minimum injection down. Broussard grew 17.5% between April 2020 and July 2025 while Louisiana lost 0.9% [1][2], yet tested on the seller's last fiscal year (illustrative here, because no statements are public) the request covers debt service 1.18x against a 1.25x floor and fails. At 14.9% equity the same purchase clears it, but the seller's history predates a new full-service competitor on the same parkway [12][13].

Market Conclusion

The dining room has a market. The price needs more than the minimum down.

Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility and market studies for owners, developers and their lenders, and this page shows the method on a restaurant purchase. Request a methodology briefing if you are weighing a similar deal. The case belongs to our restaurant and foodservice guide, sits in Louisiana and follows the lending rules in our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice area.

Broussard is the rare Louisiana town where the demand case is not the hard part. The city counted 13,447 residents at the April 2020 estimates base and 15,799 at July 1, 2025, a gain of 17.5% while the state shrank by 0.9% [1][2]. Its households earn a median $116,179, nearly twice the state's $60,756 [1][2]. Eighty-three percent of homes are owner-occupied, and the median home is valued at $310,200 [1]. Households like these are the core of a dining room's trade, and the 2022 Economic Census recorded $65,405,000 of accommodation and food services sales inside the city limits [1].

What the town does not offer is empty ground. Five full-service restaurants are confirmed on their own pages, from old-line Creole and steakhouse rooms to a neighborhood bar and kitchen, and two more names appear in a September 2026 Tripadvisor roundup but stay unconfirmed [6][8][9][10][11][27]. The pipeline holds a Cajun-Asian seafood house with a $1.45 million building permit on Ambassador Caffery Parkway [12][13]. A buyer of an existing dining room is therefore betting that a known address, a trained kitchen and a liquor license are worth more than the newer rooms' fresh paint.

Here is the credit question. The buyer pays $1,000,000 for the land, the building, the kitchen and the going business, a figure tied to the seller's illustrative earnings, and wants an SBA 7(a) loan with only 10% down. Because the deal is an initial acquisition, SOP 50 10 8.1 grades it on what the seller already earned, with a floor of 1.25x. Nobody has published statements for a Broussard restaurant, so the page builds a two-year seller history out of the model's own economics and marks it illustrative every time it appears. Against an illustrative FY2025 EBITDA of $160,000, the minimum-injection request is covered 1.18x, short of the floor.

Raising the buyer's cash is the cure the engine finds: 14.9% down leaves $974,898 of debt and brings the same year to 1.25x. A pass on that test is not the end of the analysis, since the test looks only at the past and the parkway is changing. King's Seafood broke ground in late March 2026 on the same road [12], and none of its trade is in the seller's FY2025. Our first-year projection absorbs it and covers the restructured loan 1.16x: clear of SBA's general 7(a) standard of 1.15x, short of the acquisition floor.

Our reading: Broussard can support an operating dining room, and it can support this purchase once the buyer puts in the restructured equity, but only if 2026 interim statements show the guest count held through the end of the interchange work and the new neighbor's arrival. A steeper slide than the one projected should lower the price before it raises the equity a second time. Readers comparing deal types can set this case beside the Opelika gas station purchase, which runs the same rule on a different business.

Three readings matter for a dining room. First, growth is recent and large, and it is a family kind of growth: 28.4% of residents are under 18 against 22.7% statewide, and households average 2.81 persons against 2.49 [1][2]. Family tables tend to mean larger parties and earlier dinners, which suits a Cajun menu better than a bar-led format. Second, incomes are high. A median household income of $116,179 and per capita income of $48,339 put the typical Broussard household well above Louisiana's [1][2], and the labor force participation rate of 77.1% against 58.8% points to earned income, which fits DOTD's 2010 note that residents commute to Lafayette [1][2][3]. Third, Broussard sells more at retail per resident than the state does, $27,416 against $19,351 in 2022 [1][2], which shows spending staying in town.

The same table carries the main caution. Broussard is not a retirement or tourist market: only 9.7% of residents are 65 or over [1], so the weekday senior lunch trade that carries many small-town dining rooms is thin. Sales depend on working households, which makes the weekday dinner and the weekend the restaurant's real business. The city's own economic development office publishes a higher population estimate than the Census does; the case uses the Census figure and treats the gap as a reason to verify local demand rather than assume it.

The register lines above place the city inside a parish that already runs thick with restaurants: the parish carries about a third more restaurants per resident than Louisiana as a whole. That density helps a buyer because the parish eats out, and it hurts because every new room is competing for the same table. Parish incomes sit well below Broussard's own, so the city is a high-income pocket inside a parish whose typical household earns much less.

The access evidence is mixed and dated. The last Louisiana DOTD counts the research found are from 2010: 40,247 vehicles a day on US 90 south of Broussard and 47,492 on Ambassador Caffery Parkway (LA 3073) [3]. DOTD described US 90 that year as the road residents use to commute from Broussard to Lafayette [3]. Current counts with station identifiers were not found and are a lender condition below. Since then DOTD has been rebuilding the US 90 and Ambassador Caffery interchange, a $136.52 million project whose February 21, 2024 release estimated completion by spring 2026 [4]. Access to a restaurant on the parkway was disrupted in the meantime, including an overnight closure of LA 3073 between the US 90 lanes in April 2025 [5]. Whether the finished interchange helps or hurts a given site depends on where its entrance sits relative to the new ramps, which the study treats as a site question and not a corridor-wide gain.

Two demand drivers the research could not size are crawfish season and festival weekends. Both are real in kind for Acadiana restaurants, but no source quantified them for Broussard, so the model carries no seasonal premium and a lender condition asks for monthly sales from the seller.

A buyer needs to know which of these positions the target occupies, because a mid-priced Cajun room competes with several of them at once.

The pipeline matters more than the census. King's Seafood, a Cajun-Asian seafood restaurant that is a third unit after New Iberia and Abbeville, began site work in late March 2026 at the corner of Ambassador Caffery Parkway and South Bernard Road, on a site once announced for a New Orleans concept that was never built [12]. The building permit is $1.45 million [13]. No opening date is confirmed. A limited-service wave follows: Fish Box Sushi signed a lease in a new 12,000 square foot center with seven restaurant, coffee and quick-service spaces [14], and a Tropical Smoothie Cafe is coming to Albertson Parkway in a former Taco Bell building [15]. Those are quick-service formats and the research names no operating quick-service restaurant inside the city, so they are cited here as pipeline and the case draws no quick-service conclusions from them.

Prejean's arrival shows both sides of the same fact. A new full-service name found room in Broussard, so entry is possible. The same arrival took tables from the rooms already there, which is exactly the kind of change a seller's two-year history may not show. A buyer should ask what happened to the target's sales in the twelve months after a new full-service competitor opened, and again in the year King's opens [6][12].

Site, Zoning and Utilities

An operating site whose files are incomplete.

Picture an independent full-service Cajun and seafood restaurant, owned with its building, somewhere along the Ambassador Caffery and US 90 corridor inside Broussard. The model assumes roughly four and a half thousand square feet, about one and a half acres and about 110 seats. Those round numbers are placeholders and describe no actual restaurant, and the address, parcel, parish and menu concept are left open on purpose. With an existing business the live question is not whether the use could be permitted but whether it keeps its permissions when the owner changes.

Zoning

The City of Broussard publishes its zoning ordinance through its permitting and licensing page [16]. No district was found for any restaurant parcel, and no by-right or conditional status. A zoning verification letter that names the district, confirms the restaurant and bar use, and states rebuild rights after a casualty is a lender condition. A restaurant that serves alcohol may also depend on separation rules from schools and churches that the research did not retrieve.

Utilities and taxes

  • Water, sewer, electric and gas: no provider was confirmed for any parcel on the corridor, and impact or connection fees were not found. An operating restaurant already holds its connections; the question matters if the buyer expands the kitchen.
  • Sales tax: the rate depends on the parcel. The table lists 9.50% inside Lafayette Parish, made of the 5.00% state rate, 2.00% for the parish school board and 2.50% for the city [17]. Parcels inside the Ambassador Caffery Extension economic development subdistrict pay 10.50%, because it adds 1.00%, and the part of Broussard in St. Martin Parish pays 11.00%, all effective October 1, 2025 [17]. The state rate rose from 4.45% to 5% on January 1, 2025 [18]. Sales tax is collected from guests and is not a restaurant expense, but it is part of the check a guest compares with other rooms.
  • Corporate income tax: Louisiana moved to a flat 5.5% rate for periods beginning on or after January 1, 2025 [19]. The model stops at EBITDA, so the rate does not enter the coverage test.
  • Property tax: the Lafayette Parish Assessor's 2025 report shows Broussard with no municipal general alimony millage, against 3.6 mills in Youngsville, 4.66 in Carencro and 4.32 in Scott [20]. The parish and school levies that apply to the parcel and the assessment ratio were not found. The model carries a placeholder of 1.00% of the real estate allocation, and the tax bill is a lender condition. For scale, the parish collected $288,314,896.36 in property tax for tax year 2025 [20].

Flood, wind and environment

The flood zone is undetermined and so are the site's wind and flood insurance costs. DOTD notes that the US 90 and Ambassador Caffery corridor carries hurricane evacuation traffic [3], which is a reminder that Acadiana restaurants are underwritten for storm closures. The Phase I environmental screening is smaller for a restaurant than for a fuel site but not optional: grease interceptors, any former on-site fuel tank and the age of the building's systems are all diligence items.

Guests. The model carries 55,000 guests in Year 3 for a room of about 110 seats; the inputs table shows the daily load behind that, under two turns of the dining room on an average day, which is a plausible load for a full-service Cajun restaurant that does lunch and dinner. Year 1 sits a little below the illustrative FY2025 volume because King's Seafood is expected to open during the year [12], and growth after that is slight. The city grew far faster than that [1], but a table-service restaurant cannot grow faster than its seats and its hours, so the model lets price do most of the work.

The check. Food sales are $19.50 per guest and beverage and bar sales $5.50 in Year 1, growing 2.5% a year. The food figure is a blend of lunch and dinner. The competitors' own menus show a lower lunch tier and a higher dinner tier [7][8], and the model places the check between them. The beverage figure averages every guest, including those who order water, and assumes the restaurant holds a standard liquor license that transfers with the business.

What is left out. Catering, takeout, a second dining room and delivery platforms are not modeled, though most Acadiana restaurants run at least one of them. The research did not evidence their volume for any Broussard restaurant, and a lender should not underwrite income it cannot trace. If the seller's records show catering or private events, they belong in the history, and the model's guests and check would need to be adjusted to match.

Cost of sales. The model charges 33.0% of food sales and 25.0% of beverage sales. A seafood-heavy menu carries a food cost that moves with the market for shrimp, crab and fish, and those markets can swing. The research held no Louisiana restaurant cost benchmark, so these are placeholders that the seller's purchase invoices replace.

Payroll. The restaurant buys 27,500 hourly hours a year at $14.00 an hour, plus 12.0% for payroll taxes and workers' compensation. Those hours cover cooks, dishwashers, servers, bussers and a bartender across lunch and dinner service. Two salaried managers cost $110,000, charged at a market rate even if the buyer runs the kitchen, so the test cannot lean on work the buyer does for free. Wages and other costs grow 3.0% a year, slightly faster than menu prices.

Card fees and property tax. Card processing is 2.7% of sales, since most dining checks are paid by card. The property tax placeholder of 1.00% applies to the real estate allocation only, because the parcel's actual levy was not found. A sale at the new price may also prompt a reassessment.

Other expenses. Utilities for a commercial kitchen, insurance with flood and wind cover, repairs, linen, smallwares, pest control, waste, licensing, marketing, point-of-sale software and accounting start at $195,000 and grow with wages. In a hurricane region the insurance part of that line is the one most likely to be understated.

Net operating income comes to 11.1% of Year 1 revenue and rises each year, because menu prices grow slightly faster than the fixed part of the cost base and guests grow with the neighborhood. The rising line is the buyer's case; the program reviews it without counting it toward the floor. Price, more than operations, settles this loan.

How the price is split decides three things at once. The model puts $620,000 of the $1,000,000 on land and building and the remainder on kitchen equipment, fixtures, licenses and goodwill. Nothing in the research prices land or construction in Broussard, so the split is a round placeholder for the appraiser to replace. Every dollar moved out of real estate and into goodwill amortizes over the shorter business-portion term and not the longer real estate term, which raises the payment.

  • Business Purchase Price. In SBA's words it is the purchase-agreement price less the appraised value of owner-occupied commercial real estate. On this allocation that figure exceeds $350,000, the ceiling for a lender valuing the business in-house, so an outside valuation is part of the file.
  • Quality of Earnings. The QoE report starts at a Business Purchase Price of $3 million, which this deal never approaches.
  • Valuation cost as equity. The out-of-pocket cost of the business valuation and QoE counts toward the injection, so a share of the closing line is equity already paid.

Smaller lines follow. Inventory of $25,000 is counted at closing, the working capital reserve is $55,000, and $40,000 covers the appraisal, valuation, screening and legal work plus the move of the liquor, health and occupational licenses to the buyer. The guaranty fee is rolled into project cost. SBA's example list of special purpose property leaves restaurants out [22], so a dining room gets neither the 25-year whole-loan term nor the projection exception that a gas station or hotel might claim. The building is worth most as a restaurant and less as anything else.

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, in force since October 1, 2026, the business share of a change-of-ownership loan runs at most 10 years, and the real estate share can stretch to 25. The table therefore shows two tranches whose payments per borrowed dollar differ sharply, with the equipment-and-goodwill piece costing most.

The model charges the highest rate the rules allow: Prime, 7.00% in the H.15 release, plus the largest variable spread SBA allows for a loan of this size, which gives 10.00% [24]. The model carries an SBA guaranty share of 75.0% [25], and the FY2027 upfront fee is financed with the project [23]. At the national level, a law firm relaying a lender trade group's quotation of Appendix 15 reports $8.29 billion of 7(a) acquisition lending in FY2025, with a default rate of 1.93% on acquisition loans against 2.71% on the rest [21]. The route is secondhand and should be checked against SBA's text.

A debt ceiling applies as well: total acquisition debt, including seller debt not on full standby, is capped at the business valuation; any excess must be equity. The restructured $974,898 funds part of the $1,000,000 price together with inventory, working capital, closing costs and the fee, and the buyer's equity covers the rest. The valuation and appraisal must together support at least $974,898 of debt, and any gap is added to the buyer's equity.

Several buyer-side rules apply too. The injection is 10% of project cost at minimum. A standby seller note, other standby debt and minority investors together may fund no more than half of it. As for price terms, the SOP says seller earnouts prohibited; buyer rebates must pay down the 7(a) loan; purchase-agreement working-capital true-ups are not rebates. A seller who stays on as a consultant is held to 24 months, which matters when the recipes and the regulars belong to the seller.

The 7(a) Small path, with its 1.10x test, is not open to this buyer; its basis is historical or projected, plus the two most recent months of business bank statements; projections must reach 1.10x within one year; does not apply to changes of ownership. The rule in Appendix 15 reads: the initial-acquisition floor is 1.25x on historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two. Forward-looking numbers get a narrow role: projections are reviewed but cannot meet the floor, except an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports, where the floor may be met on projections within two years.

As proposed. The made-up seller earned $172,000 in FY2024 and $160,000 in FY2025; the page flags both as invented. The case tests the later, lower year, the more cautious of the two bases the rule allows. The proposed $1,032,390 loan carries $135,407 of yearly debt service and is covered 1.18x. That is a fail, and the two-year average, though better, does not rescue it. In a market where a rival is about to open, the weaker recent year is also the fairer picture.

As restructured. With 14.9% equity ($170,693) the loan drops to $974,898, yearly debt service to $127,866, and FY2025 covers it 1.25x. Equity, loan size and coverage all clear their tests. What the buyer buys with the added cash is eligibility; the restaurant itself does not change.

What the projections add. Forward coverage on the restructured loan is 1.16x in Year 1 and 1.30x in Year 3, above the general standard and, in the first year, under the acquisition floor. Restaurants are outside SBA's special purpose examples [22], so the projection exception is unavailable and the history decides. When history and forecast diverge, neither should be trusted until 2026 interim statements arrive. If they track the forecast, the right answer is a lower price, not more equity.

The useful reading is the diagonal. A restaurant that loses a tenth of its guests while its food cost rises by one grid step at the same time, which is what a bad shrimp season and a new competitor look like together, falls well below the level where NOI covers debt service on the restructured loan. A restaurant that holds volume and cost clears the floor with room. The lender's real exposure is a combination, and the lender should test both at once.

Risk Factors

Risks particular to one dining room on this corridor.

  1. The history predates the newest competitor. King's Seafood began site work in March 2026 [12], so the seller's FY2024 and FY2025 cannot show its effect.
  2. Construction-era sales. The interchange work ran through the seller's recent years and included overnight closures [4][5]. A depressed history flatters the price paid today; a history that was helped by detour traffic would do the opposite.
  3. Key-person and recipe risk. A restaurant's value sits in a chef, a manager and a few recipes. The SOP limits a departing seller's consulting to 24 months, so the transfer plan has to be written before closing.
  4. Price allocation. If the appraisal puts more of the price into goodwill than the case does, the business portion grows, debt service rises and the required equity rises with it.
  5. Collateral. A restaurant building is worth most as a restaurant. If the business fails, recovery depends on another operator wanting the site; see our note on special purpose property risk for the general analysis, even though SBA's list does not name restaurants.
  6. Stale traffic evidence. The only counts found are from 2010 [3]. If traffic at the subject's entrance has changed with the new interchange, the guest base differs from the model.
  7. Sales tax jurisdiction. A parcel in the subdistrict or in St. Martin Parish charges guests more than one elsewhere in Lafayette Parish [17], which shapes how the menu compares with rivals' on the same road.
  8. Storm and flood exposure. Acadiana closures and insurance costs are real, and no insurance quote was available for any parcel.
  9. Food cost cycle. A seafood menu can lose margin before the printed menu catches up.
  10. Wage pressure. With 77.1% of residents already in the labor force [1], there may be little slack, and wages could rise faster than the 3.0% the model carries.

Broussard Questions

Questions about the Broussard restaurant acquisition case.

Why does this restaurant case test the seller's past instead of the buyer's plan?

Because buying an operating restaurant is an initial acquisition under Appendix 15 of SOP 50 10 8.1, where coverage is measured on the seller's last fiscal year or the average of the last two, and the buyer's projections are reviewed without being able to carry the loan over the floor [22]. The plan shapes the lender's judgment about risk; it does not change the arithmetic of the test.

Are the seller's FY2024 and FY2025 earnings on this page real?

No. The research found no statements for any restaurant in Broussard, so both years are illustrative model inputs built from the same economics as Year 1, and the page labels them wherever they appear. The determination is only as reliable as those two numbers, which is why the first lender condition replaces them with tax returns, monthly sales reports and 2026 interim statements.

Could a restaurant use the special purpose exception that lets projections carry a change of ownership?

Not on the evidence in hand. The exception is limited to an owner-occupied special purpose property whose price the appraised value fully supports, and SBA's example list of special purpose property does not include restaurants [22]. A dining room with a conventional kitchen can be converted to another food use, so a lender should expect the 1.25x history test to apply in full.

Which sales tax rate applies to a restaurant in Broussard?

It depends on the parcel. The Louisiana Association of Tax Administrators table shows 9.50% for Broussard inside Lafayette Parish, 10.50% inside the economic development subdistrict along Ambassador Caffery Parkway and 11.00% for the part of the city in St. Martin Parish, all effective October 1, 2025 [17]. Sales tax is collected from guests and is not an expense in the model, but the rate changes the price a guest sees, so the parcel's jurisdiction is a lender condition.

How crowded is Broussard with full-service restaurants?

Crowded and still growing. Five full-service restaurants inside the city are confirmed on their own pages, from a seafood house on Albertson Parkway to an Italian and Creole dining room on East 2nd Street, two more names appear in a September 2026 Tripadvisor roundup without an operator page we could confirm, and a Cajun-Asian seafood house is under construction on the Ambassador Caffery corridor with limited-service openings behind it [8][9][10][11][12][14][27]. Prejean's opened a Broussard unit, which shows the town can still absorb a new full-service name [6].

Is a feasibility study mandatory for this loan?

No. The regulation, 13 CFR 120.160(b), says only that SBA may ask for one [26]. For an acquisition the lender's core evidence is the seller's tax returns, the business valuation and the appraisal. A market study earns its place here because the trailing years predate a new competitor and an access change on the same corridor.
