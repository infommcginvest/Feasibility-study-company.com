Buying a Gas Station at I-85 Exit 64 in Opelika, Alabama: An SBA 7(a) Change of Ownership

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published July 13, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

A buyer agrees to pay $1,300,000 for a hypothetical older branded fuel and convenience store in the I-85 Exit 64 trade area and asks for a 7(a) loan with the minimum injection down. Tested the way Appendix 15 tests an initial acquisition, on the seller's last fiscal year (illustrative here, because no statements are public), that request covers debt service 1.20x against a 1.25x floor and fails. At 13.1% equity the same purchase clears the floor, but the seller's history predates a QuikTrip that opened one exit south in late 2025, so the loan should close only after 2026 results confirm the run rate [7][12].

Market Conclusion

The store has a market. The price needs more than the minimum down.

Exit 64 sits on the lighter-traffic stretch of I-85 through Opelika [3], and that is both its protection and its ceiling. A small branded store there sells to two streams of customers: drivers leaving the interstate on West Point Parkway (US-29) and Opelika residents commuting along it. The city those commuters live in grew 15.3% between April 2020 and July 2025, well ahead of Lee County's 8.9% [1][2]. That growth is the strongest demand fact in the file, and it explains why a store of this vintage still trades.

What the exit does not offer is room to take share. Two 24-hour branded stores already sit within 0.14 mile of the ramps [7][8]. At the next exit south, a RaceTrac on a 6.35-acre lot with 15 semi-truck spaces and a QuikTrip that opened in late 2025 now hold the large-format, food-and-fuel end of the market [9][11][12]. A QuikTrip travel center with six diesel bays, announced for a 2022 opening, now operates at Exit 58, and Buc-ee's Auburn, with 120 fueling positions, opened at Exit 50 in April 2023 [16][18]. An older store with a small forecourt does not win the price fight against that set. It keeps the customers for whom the nearest pump and a quick stop matter more than a few cents a gallon.

The loan question is therefore narrow. The buyer here pays $1,300,000 for land, building, fuel equipment and business, a price set as an assumption rather than taken from any real offering, and asks for an SBA 7(a) loan with the minimum injection of 10%. Because this is an initial acquisition, SOP 50 10 8.1 measures it on the seller's record, at 1.25x on historical EBITDA. No seller statements are public, so this page runs the test on an illustrative two-year history built from benchmark store economics, labelled as such everywhere it appears. On an illustrative FY2025 EBITDA of $210,000, the proposed structure covers its debt service 1.20x. It fails.

The engine restructures by adding equity until the seller's last year carries the loan at the floor: 13.1% down, $1,311,696 of debt and 1.25x coverage. That structure passes the rule. The study does not stop there, because the rule looks backward and the market moved. QuikTrip's opening announcement for its Exit 62 store came on November 21, 2025, so the seller's FY2025 contains at most a few weeks of that competitor [12]. Our projection of the buyer's first year carries a full year of it and covers the restructured loan 1.23x: above SBA's general 7(a) standard of 1.15x, below the acquisition floor.

The conclusion: the market supports an operating store at Exit 64, and it supports this purchase at the restructured equity, provided 2026 interim statements show the store held its volume after the QuikTrip opened. If they show a decline beyond the one projected here, the price should fall rather than the equity rise again. This is the acquisition counterpart to the new-build Cartersville case on I-75, where the test is a start-up projection instead of a seller's history; the two show how differently the program treats an existing store and a new one at an interstate exit. For the format itself, see our page on interstate and highway fuel sites and the broader gas station and convenience store underwriting guide.

The traffic evidence is the weakest part of the demand case and the most important. The only counts with segment detail date from 2010. They show the interstate carrying about 34,670 vehicles a day between Exits 64 and 62, against 47,570 between Exits 62 and 60 [3]. Exit 64 sits on the lighter stretch, which is consistent with the large-format chains choosing Exit 62 and Exit 58. A 2020 figure of 51,887 vehicles a day for I-85 at Opelika suggests strong growth since 2010, but its source does not say where it was counted [4]. A current ALDOT count with station identifiers for I-85 at Exit 64 and for West Point Parkway is a lender condition below, and so is any measure of truck volume, which the research did not find.

The employment base is anchored by a hospital, which is steady around the clock, and by manufacturers in the 200 to 999 employee range [5]. Shift workers are good convenience customers. The Baxter closure, 459 jobs in November 2023, is a reminder that a plant can leave quickly [6]. Seasonal demand from Auburn football weekends and Gulf Coast travel on I-85 is real in kind but was not quantified in any source, so the model carries no seasonal premium.

The closest competitor of all could not be confirmed and therefore sits outside the table. A Marathon-branded store at 2104 West Point Parkway, about 0.09 mile from the exit and listed in directories as Joe's Grocery, was open 24 hours and posting regular at $3.899 on October 15, 2026 [7]. Directories disagree on its truck parking and none links to an operator page, so its format and volume are unknown. JK Foodmart, an independent about 2.25 miles west of the exit, showed regular at $3.999 on the same check, flagged by iExit as more than a day old [7]. At Exit 62 and Exit 60, fuel-price directories also list stations at 1100 and 1001 Columbus Parkway (with conflicting 76 and Marathon branding), an Eagle 280 store and a Shopper Stop #37; we found no operator page or county record for any of them. Each of these is a lender condition.

The posted prices that are dated tell a simple story. At Exit 64 the Marathon priced regular below the Circle K on October 15, 2026 [7]. Any small store in the Exit 64 trade area has to price with the cheaper of the two or lose the price-led driver, which caps the margin it can take. The operators with the most pricing power, QuikTrip and RaceTrac, do not post prices on their store pages, so a 30-day price survey across both exits is a lender condition rather than a model input.

The pattern of entry matters more than any single store. Every confirmed opening since 2021 has been a large-format chain store at an exit other than 64: RaceTrac after a 2021 conditional use approval and rezoning, QuikTrip's travel center at Exit 58, announced for 2022, Buc-ee's at Exit 50 in 2023 and QuikTrip at Exit 62 in 2025 [10][16][18][12]. No planned or permitted station at Exit 64 was found. That protects the subject from direct new supply on its own corner and suggests the chains see Exit 64 as the weaker interchange. One city action could change that over time: Opelika awarded a $974,902.42 contract for I-85 sanitary sewer improvements on October 6, 2026, and sewer capacity is what highway commercial sites usually wait for [22].

Site, Zoning and Utilities

An operating site whose files are incomplete.

The subject is a hypothetical operating branded fuel and convenience store in the Exit 64 trade area on the West Point Parkway (US-29) corridor, in the city of Opelika. It is an older small-format store of a kind common on the corridor; it represents no real station, and no address, parcel, brand or seller is named. For an acquisition, the site questions are less about whether the use can be built and more about whether it can keep operating and transfer cleanly.

Zoning

The zoning district of a parcel at Exit 64 was not found. The relevant precedent is RaceTrac's Exit 62 store, which needed a Planning Commission conditional use approval on May 25, 2021 and the rezoning of a residential lot to C-3 before its plat was approved on October 26, 2021 [10]. If a fuel station is conditional in the district that covers the subject, the existing store may be operating under an older approval or as a legal nonconforming use, and either status limits what a buyer can rebuild after a casualty. A zoning verification letter from the City of Opelika is a lender condition. The Planning Commission meets on the fourth Tuesday of each month at 3 p.m. [25].

The city's package liquor rules adopted on October 6, 2026 set a 1,500-foot separation, a 2,000 square foot minimum and a requirement that package stores sit in centers of three or more tenants with at least 14,000 square feet; they do not apply to beer-and-wine-only sellers [22]. A single convenience store cannot add a liquor package department, so the beer and wine license is the alcohol revenue the store has.

Utilities and taxes

  • Water: the Water Works Board of the City of Opelika serves the city; service to the specific parcel was not confirmed.
  • Sewer: the City's Wastewater Division operates the system. The sewer access fee rose to $1,700 per equivalent average connection from $1,250 in May 2023 [24]. An operating store already holds its connection; the fee matters only if the buyer expands.
  • Electric: Opelika Power Services and Alabama Power both serve parts of the city, per the 2021 Planning Commission record [10]; the provider for the parcel was not confirmed. No natural gas provider was found.
  • Sales tax: 9% combined inside Opelika in 2026 [23], collected from customers and not a store expense.
  • Property tax: no dated millage table and no tax bill for a parcel at Exit 64 were found. The model carries a placeholder of 1.08% of the real estate allocation, and the tax bill is a lender condition.

Traffic, flood and environment

No current ALDOT count with a station identifier was retrievable for I-85 at Exit 64 or for West Point Parkway; the demand section uses 2010 city counts and an unlocated 2020 figure. The flood zone is undetermined. Environmental history is missing too, and for an older fuel site that is the gap that matters most: under SBA's policy for gas station loans, a Phase I report with tank and line testing records is due on every NAICS 457 property, large loan or small [38]. The number, age and condition of the fueling positions are a site-inspection question for any real purchase.

Gallons. The model carries 712,000 gallons in Year 3, below the 1.0 million gallons Yesway's older 3,400 square foot stores averaged in 2025 on 10 dispensers [28]. The subject has fewer fueling positions than that format, keeps 15-hour days while the 24-hour stores at the ramps stay open around the clock, and enters Year 1 with a full year of the new QuikTrip at Exit 62. Growth after Year 1 is slow, in step with national driving: vehicle-miles rose 0.9% in 2025 [35], and Opelika's population growth adds a little to that.

Fuel margin. The model uses 40.0 cents a gallon. NACS reports that retail fuel margins averaged over 40 cents in 2025 [30]. The listed chains earned between 35.1 and 52.6 cents in the second quarter of 2026 after a supply shock, and the five-year national averages to September 2026 were 39.3 cents for gasoline and 54.3 cents for diesel [32]. Forty cents is a mid-cycle number, not a 2026 peak. The store sells mostly gasoline, so the higher diesel average is not applied.

Inside sales and margin. Inside sales stabilize near $1,066,000 in Year 3, below Yesway's $1.7 million legacy-store average [28]. The store has no food program; across the industry, foodservice was 28.5% of in-store sales and 38.9% of in-store gross profit in 2025 [29], so a buyer who adds a food counter has real upside. None of it is in the model, because it would need capital and a permit the case does not budget. The inside margin of 35.2% is Yesway's fiscal 2025 merchandise margin [28].

Two things are left out on purpose. Lottery commissions and ATM fees are small and were not evidenced for any Exit 64 store. Leaving them out understates revenue slightly; the sensitivity table shows that the two drivers that move coverage are gallons and margin, not these.

Payroll. Two clerks cover each of the store's 15 daily hours, 10,950 paid hours a year, at $15.04 an hour, the industry's 2025 average [29], plus 12.0% for payroll taxes and workers' compensation. The average U.S. store carried 19.9 employees in 2025 [29]; this store is a fraction of that size. A salaried manager at $48,000 is charged even if the buyer runs the store, so the coverage test is not propped up by unpaid owner labor.

Card fees. NACS puts card fees at 8.4 cents a gallon on 2023 data [31], and industry card fees reached a record $21.3 billion in 2025 [29]. The model charges 8.4 cents on every gallon and folds card fees on inside sales into the other-expense line.

Property tax. The placeholder of 1.08% applies only to the real estate allocation, because Alabama taxes business property on assessed value and the parcel's actual bill was not found. Small as the line is, it is a condition: the purchase may trigger a reappraisal at the new price.

Other expenses. Utilities for coolers and canopy lighting, insurance, repairs, tank and line testing, supplies, systems and card fees on inside sales start at $128,000. Wages and other costs grow 3% a year; NACS measured total store expenses up 23.3% from 2021 to 2025 [30], so 3% is not generous to the borrower.

Net operating income comes to 16.0% of the revenue line in Year 1 and a little less each year after, because costs grow faster than a mature store's sales. That shape is typical of an older store in a market where the growth is going to newer formats, and it is the reason the purchase price, not the operating plan, decides this loan.

The allocation of the price drives three program rules at once. The model places $910,000 of the $1,300,000 price on real estate (land, building, canopy and tanks) and the rest on business assets, dispensers, fixtures and goodwill. No published norm for that split was found; for a special purpose property, the going-concern appraisal sets it.

  • Business Purchase Price. SBA defines it as the purchase-agreement price less the appraised value of owner-occupied commercial real estate. On the case allocation it is above $350,000, the level at or below which the lender may value the business itself, so an independent business valuation is needed.
  • Quality of Earnings. The QoE trigger is a Business Purchase Price of $3 million, far above this deal, and the SOP exempts Owner Buyout, ESOP & Cooperative, and any acquisition of an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property in any case. The diligence burden is the valuation, the appraisal and the seller's tax returns.
  • Buyer cash that counts. The SOP counts the out-of-pocket cost of the business valuation and QoE as equity, so part of the closing-cost line the buyer pays early is injection, not an extra cost.

The other uses are modest: $80,000 of fuel and merchandise inventory counted at closing, a $50,000 reserve and $45,000 of closing costs. The SBA upfront guaranty fee is financed. Casey's has said an acquired store costs it about $1 million less on average than a new build with the same return requirement [33]. For an independent buyer the same logic holds at smaller scale: buying an operating store avoids construction risk, and the price is what has to be underwritten instead. Sellers like this one are the norm, not the exception: at the end of 2025, 95,672 of the country's 151,975 convenience stores, 63%, belonged to companies with ten or fewer stores [34].

A change of ownership loan under SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, effective October 1, 2026, amortizes the business portion over no more than 10 years. The real estate portion may run to 25 years as a separate piece or a blended loan. That split is why the structure above has two tranches with very different debt service per dollar: the business portion costs far more each year than the real estate portion.

Special purpose status offers a way around the split that this case cannot use. When 85% or more of the use of total project cost is real estate and the owner-occupied special purpose property is integral to the business, the whole loan may amortize over 25 years. Gas stations are on SBA's special purpose list. Here real estate is well under that share of project cost, because the price carries goodwill and the project carries inventory and working capital. A buyer who negotiated a price closer to appraised real estate value would pay less and gain the longer term at the same time.

The case prices the loan at its ceiling, Prime of 7.00% from the H.15 release plus SBA's maximum spread, or 10.00% [40]. Alabama's 7(a) loans averaged 10.4% across 626 loans and $352.7 million in calendar 2025 [27], so the model is not pricing below the market. With a loan above $150,000, SBA guarantees 75% [37], and the bank rolls the FY2027 upfront guaranty fee into the project [39].

Three ownership and injection rules bind the buyer. The injection is 10% of total project cost and cannot be reduced or eliminated. A seller note on full standby, other full-standby debt and minority investors together may supply no more than half of the required injection. And the buyer's ownership must pass the March 1, 2026 rule: direct and indirect owners and guarantors who are U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals residing in the country, with no foreign share [36]. Fuel supply agreements also need attention: SOP 50 10 8.1 treats dealer, jobber and supply relationships covered by the Petroleum Marketing Practices Act as franchises [38], so the branded supply contract, its transfer terms and any image requirements are part of eligibility review, a point developed on our branded fuel section of our gas station guide.

Lee County has a deep bench of SBA lenders: 526 7(a) and 504 loans totalling $239,450,372 since FY1991, eight of them to gasoline stations with convenience stores, with PNC Bank (90 loans) and AuburnBank (17) among the most active [26]. The same data show a 23.6% charge-off rate on the county's FY1991 to FY2015 vintages, 55 of 233 loans [26]. Our 7(a) and 504 lending practice explains how lenders weigh county loss history against a single deal.

As proposed: the buyer proposes the 10% minimum injection for an initial acquisition, with 10.0% equity ($151,074) and $1,359,667 of debt. The structure does not qualify: coverage falls below the floor.

As restructured: 13.1% equity ($197,736) and $1,311,696 of debt with $167,922 of annual debt service. Every test is met.

Appendix 15 sets the rule plainly. For an initial acquisition the floor is 1.25x, measured as historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two. And projections are reviewed but cannot meet the floor, except an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports, where the floor may be met on projections within two years.

As proposed. The illustrative seller earned $222,000 in FY2024 and $210,000 in FY2025. Both years are invented for this example from benchmark economics, and the page says so wherever they appear. The model tests the last year, the lower of the two, because the store's market has been moving against it. On that basis the proposed $1,359,667 loan, with $174,063 of annual debt service, is covered 1.20x. The two-year average is higher than the last year but still short of the floor at this debt. The proposal fails.

As restructured. At 13.1% equity ($197,736), the loan falls to $1,311,696 and debt service to $167,922, which the FY2025 figure covers 1.25x. The structure meets the equity rule, the size limit and the coverage floor. The extra cash is real money for a buyer of a single store, and it buys nothing but compliance: the store is the same store.

What the projections add. The special purpose exception would let projections carry the loan if the appraised value fully supports the price. It does not help here for two reasons. The price includes goodwill that real estate value alone does not support unless the going-concern appraisal says otherwise, and the projections themselves sit below the floor: the restructured loan is covered 1.23x in Year 1 and 1.23x in Year 3. The projection is the study's measure of the store after the new QuikTrip; the history is the program's measure before it. When the two disagree, the lender should trust neither until 2026 interim statements arrive. If those statements run near the projection, the honest response is a lower price, not a third round of equity.

The research note on what SOP 50 10 8.1 changed for feasibility studies explains why the acquisition floor rose under the new SOP and why projections lost their standing in these deals.

Coverage in Year 3 ranges from 0.69x to 1.91x across the grid. The two drivers are chosen because they are where an Exit 64 store is exposed. Gallons carry the competitive risk: the large formats at Exits 62 and 58 compete on price and food, and every driver they win is a fuel sale lost. Margin carries the cycle risk: second-quarter 2026 margins of 35.1 to 52.6 cents at listed operators reflect a supply shock, not a steady state [32], and same-store gallons fell at three of five listed chains in the same quarter [32].

The useful reading is the diagonal. A store that gives up a tenth of its gallons and a tenth of its margin at the same time, which is what a price war at the next exit looks like, slips just below the point where NOI covers debt service on the restructured loan. A store that holds volume and earns a stronger margin than 40 cents clears the floor comfortably. The lender's real exposure is a combination of lost volume and a return to the low end of the margin range, and that is why the margin assumption is set at mid-cycle rather than at 2026's peak.

Risk Factors

Risks particular to this store at this exit.

  1. The history predates the newest competitor. QuikTrip #7183 opened at Exit 62 in late 2025 with 24-hour food and auto diesel [11][12]. A seller's FY2024 and FY2025 cannot show its effect.
  2. Two 24-hour stores at the ramps. Circle K #708 and the Marathon at 2104 West Point Parkway both stay open around the clock [7][8]. A store with shorter hours concedes the night trade.
  3. Environmental exposure of an older fuel site. Tanks, lines and dispensers of that age are the largest unknown liability, and SBA's gas station environmental requirements apply in full [38].
  4. Price allocation. If the appraisal puts more of the price into goodwill than the case does, the business portion grows, debt service rises and the required equity rises with it.
  5. Special purpose collateral. A station is worth most as a station. If the store fails, the lender's recovery depends on another operator wanting the site, which the analysis in our note on special purpose property risk treats in detail.
  6. Brand and supply terms. A fuel supply agreement may carry rebranding costs, volume minimums or image requirements on transfer, and it is treated as a franchise for eligibility [38].
  7. Stale traffic evidence. The only segment counts are from 2010 [3]. If Exit 64's traffic has grown less than the corridor's, the volume base is weaker than the model assumes.
  8. Manufacturing concentration. The Baxter closure of 2023 removed 459 jobs [6]; a similar loss among the 200 to 999 employee plants would cut the commuter base.
  9. Fuel margin cycle. 2026 margins were inflated by a supply shock [32]. The model's mid-cycle 40 cents could be too high in a soft year.

Opelika Questions

Questions about the Opelika acquisition case.

Why does this case test the seller's history instead of the buyer's projections?

Because the purchase is an initial acquisition under Appendix 15 of SOP 50 10 8.1, where coverage is measured on the seller's last fiscal year or the average of the last two, and post-closing projections are reviewed but cannot carry the loan over the floor [38]. The buyer's plan matters to the lender's judgment; it does not change the arithmetic of the test.

Are the seller's FY2024 and FY2025 figures real?

No. No seller statements for any Exit 64 station are public, so the case builds an illustrative two-year history from industry benchmarks for small older stores [28][30]. The determination on this page is only as good as those two numbers, which is why the first lender condition replaces them with tax returns and 2026 interim statements.

Does the gas station's special purpose status help the buyer here?

Only at the margin. SBA lists gas stations among its special purpose property examples, a status that exempts an owner-occupied special purpose acquisition from the Quality of Earnings report and can allow a 25-year term on the whole loan when real estate is at least 85% of project cost [38]. In this case the real estate allocation is well below that share, so the business portion still amortizes over 10 years.

How close is the nearest competition?

Very close. Circle K #708, an Exxon-fueled 24-hour store, sits about 0.14 mile from the exit, and a 24-hour Marathon about 0.09 mile away was posting regular at $3.899 on October 15, 2026 [7][8]. The next exit south now has a RaceTrac and a 2025 QuikTrip, both open around the clock [9][11].

What would make the minimum injection work at this price?

A seller history stronger than the illustrative figures, a lower price, or a smaller business portion. At the case price and the illustrative FY2025 EBITDA, the buyer has to bring noticeably more than the minimum cash before the seller's last year covers the loan at the floor; the table at the top of this page shows the exact figure.

Is a feasibility study mandatory for this loan?

No. The regulation, 13 CFR 120.160(b), says only that SBA may ask for one [41]. For an acquisition the lender's core evidence is the seller's tax returns, the business valuation and the going-concern appraisal. A market study earns its place here because the trailing years predate a new competitor at the next exit.

Underwriting a station purchase on an interstate exit? Start with the trailing twelve months.

We test the seller's history against Appendix 15, measure the competitors that opened after it, and size the loan to the earnings that will still be there.
