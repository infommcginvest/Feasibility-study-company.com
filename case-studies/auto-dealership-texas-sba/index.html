Auto Dealership Feasibility Study, Weatherford, Texas: A New Franchised Store on an SBA 504 Loan

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published July 13, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Weatherford is growing fast, but its I-20 auto row already carries a store for every mainstream line-make the research checked, so a new dealership here is a manufacturer's decision before it is a market one. On a hypothetical $17,568,513 ground-up store, the sponsor's opening request of 10.0% equity fails three tests: the equity tier, the debenture limit and Year 2 coverage. Restructured to 23.3% equity, which also brings the debenture back under the program limit, the loan covers 1.15x in Year 2, provided the line-make award, a Year 1 reserve and the floorplan line are in place.

Market Conclusion

A growing town on a crowded auto row: the store stands or falls on the franchise award.

Weatherford, the Parker County seat on I-20 west of Fort Worth, had 40,634 residents on July 1, 2025, up 31.6% from 30,882 in April 2020 [1]. The county reached 179,707 in 2024, a 21.2% gain in four years [2]. Households earn a median $81,516 against $78,476 statewide [3]. On population alone, Weatherford is the kind of fringe market where new rooftops follow new households, and Texas added a net eight franchised rooftops in 2025, second only to New Jersey's nine [43].

Population is not what sells a new-vehicle franchise, though. A franchised store sells the brands its manufacturer lets it sell, inside an area of responsibility the manufacturer assigns. On the Fort Worth Highway frontage between Weatherford and Hudson Oaks, Toyota, Ford, Chevrolet, Buick and GMC, Cadillac, Hyundai, Genesis, Honda, the Chrysler, Dodge, Jeep and Ram line, Nissan and Volkswagen all have stores already [14][15][16][17][19][21]. The three largest dealer taxpayers on the row carried a combined 2025 market value above $63.8 million on the Parker County rolls [22]. This is an established cluster, not an unserved town.

The market conclusion is therefore conditional. Weatherford's growth supports another franchised store only if a manufacturer without a rooftop on the corridor awards one. The research behind this case found no evidence of such an open point, so the case assumes a hypothetical award for one mainstream line-make that is not on the row, and the first lender condition is the manufacturer's letter of intent. Without it, no volume figure in this study means anything.

With that award assumed, the numbers tell a second story. The sponsor's opening structure puts 10.0% of a $17,580,220 project in equity and asks the CDC for a $7,032,088 debenture. That fails the new-business equity tier, breaks the $5 million debenture limit and covers Year 2 debt service only 0.98x. Restructured, the sponsor puts in $4,093,464 (23.3%), the debenture falls to $4,690,793, under the limit, and the bank first lien is $8,784,257, half of project cost. Year 2 coverage then reaches 1.15x, and it climbs to 1.64x in Year 3 as volume builds.

Two weak points remain after the restructure. Year 1 coverage is 0.39x, so the store needs a reserve sized to carry its first-year debt service. And coverage is very sensitive to the expense ratio: the sensitivity table shows Year 2 coverage swinging from 0.46x to 2.01x across plausible volume and cost outcomes. A lender that accepts this structure is underwriting the sponsor's operating discipline as much as the market.

Demand Evidence

Household growth, above-state incomes and a Texas market that sells more per store.

The trade area for this case is the City of Weatherford, with Parker County as the outer ring. Those are the geographies for which the Census Bureau publishes current estimates; the research found no primary radius or drive-time tabulation, and it found no daytime employment count. A dealership's customer base is wider than its town, because buyers cross-shop along I-20 toward Fort Worth, so the city figures understate reach and the county figures overstate loyalty.

The growth is real and recent. The city's 31.6% gain since 2020 is faster than the county's, which says new households are settling close to the I-20 frontage where the stores sit [1][2]. Mobility is high: 18.5% of Weatherford residents moved in the prior year, against 14% for Texas [3]. Newcomers are buyers without an established dealer relationship, which is the one demand feature a new store can actually capture. Weatherford College enrolled a record 7,242 students in fall 2025, up 9.7%, with 3,060 on the Weatherford campus [4]; students and their families add used-vehicle and service demand more than new-vehicle demand.

Income supports the price points a mainstream brand needs. Per capita income was $42,661 against $40,752 statewide, and the median age of 38 sits above the Texas median of 35.6 [3]. An older, higher-income household base buys more new vehicles and keeps them in a dealer's service lane longer. The margins of error on the city's ACS figures are wide (the income estimate carries plus or minus $9,025), so the lender should read them as direction, not precision.

The state backdrop favors volume. The average franchised store in Texas sold $108.401 million in 2025, against $76.603 million nationally [39]. Texas had 1,285 franchised dealerships at mid-2026 [40]. Nationally, light-duty new-vehicle sales reached 16,233,363 in 2025, with light trucks at 83.0% of the total [39], and the seasonally adjusted rate was 16.5 million in June 2026 [44]. Financing costs eased: the average new-vehicle loan rate was 6.35% in the second quarter of 2026, down from 6.79% a year earlier [40].

What the evidence cannot show is the number that a manufacturer and a lender would actually use: new-vehicle registrations in the area of responsibility and the brand's expected share of them. That is the standard way to size a franchised store, and neither the manufacturer's data nor any registration count was in the research. Vehicle-sales seasonality for this market was not found either. Both become lender conditions; until they are in the file, the unit volumes in the revenue build are assumptions anchored to the national average store, not to Weatherford.

Two more businesses appear in the research but stay out of the table. SouthWest Nissan, listed at 3050 Fort Worth Hwy in Hudson Oaks with a 4.6 CARFAX rating on 347 verified reviews in October 2026 [21], has no own website or registry record that could be confirmed: the obvious domain is parked. Pee Wee Cray, an independent used-vehicle dealer on Fort Worth Highway, appears only in an aggregator listing. Both are named here as unconfirmed, and confirming them is part of a lender condition. Neither changes the conclusion, but a Nissan store matters to any sponsor whose award is for an import line that cross-shops with it.

Three things stand out. First, the row is anchored by large, recent investment. Shottenkirk's Toyota building cost an estimated $12,060,930 in 2021 to 2022, and the three largest dealer owners together carry more than $63.8 million of 2025 market value [13][22]. A new store competes with buildings that already meet their manufacturers' current image programs. Second, the volume signals are lopsided. Shottenkirk posted 304 new and 740 pre-owned vehicles in stock [14] and carries 372 Yelp reviews [23], far more than any other store in the research's sample. Low star ratings on high review counts read as volume, not weakness; they also suggest that service experience is a real opening for a new operator that runs its service lane well.

Third, the market is consolidating rather than adding rooftops. No franchised dealership was found planned or under construction in the Weatherford and Hudson Oaks trade area in 2025 or 2026. In the next ring, Autobahn broke ground on October 29, 2025 on a campus of nearly 75 acres for six luxury dealerships in southwest Fort Worth, with opening expected in spring 2027 [28][29], and it closed its purchase of the Frank Kent Cadillac stores in Fort Worth and Arlington on October 6, 2025 [29]. Nationally, 458 buy-sell transactions moved 688 franchises in 2025 [51]. Frontage on this stretch of I-20 is still being absorbed, but by equipment dealers: Vermeer opened a 16,000 sq ft store with six service bays on 5 acres at exit 403 on April 15, 2026 [30]. For a sponsor, the lesson is that buying an existing point is the usual way into a mature row; a ground-up open point is the exception, and the lender should see why the manufacturer chose it.

The census also brackets the subject's scale. Local stores with known sizes run from 47,038 to 105,344 sq ft, and the two with known sites sit on 14.84 and 17.586 acres [20]. The hypothetical store in this case, at 40,000 sq ft on 5.0 acres, is smaller than all of them. That is deliberate for a single-line start-up, but the manufacturer's facility standard, not the sponsor's budget, will set the final size.

Site, Zoning and Utilities

An I-20 frontage tract where dealership use needs a conditional use permit.

The subject is a hypothetical hard-corner tract on the north I-20 frontage road in Weatherford, inside city limits, with interstate and city-street frontage. The land is priced at $20.00 per sq ft, an assumption set at the 2026 asking-price level for I-20 hard-corner frontage in Weatherford. The listing behind that level is not cited, so that no real parcel is identified, and an appraisal will set the value. Asking prices are not sales, and Weatherford's frontage is expensive for the region: a 4.58-acre I-10 frontage tract in Seguin was listed at $1,200,000 in October 2026 [5]. Land is one of the two largest lines in the budget, so an appraisal below the assumed level would cut the collateral cushion directly.

Zoning

The City of Weatherford's C2 Interstate Commercial district exists for commercial uses with a regionwide trade area along the I-20 corridor [6]. Vehicle sales in C2 have been approved by conditional use permit, not by right: two 2016 ordinances rezoned I-20 tracts to C2 with a conditional use permit for motor vehicle sales and rentals [7], and a Planning and Zoning Commission agenda carries the same combination for motor vehicle sales and storage [8]. A 2016 amendment, heard on December 7, 2016, set a separate parking standard for new and used auto dealers, though the ratio itself was not captured [9]. The state ties licensing to zoning: the dealer's premises checklist requires a zoning certificate that includes motor vehicle sales [11], and a new-vehicle store needs a general distinguishing number plus a franchised dealer license for each showroom location [10]. The district designation of any specific tract, the CUP timeline and its conditions were not found.

Utilities, access, flood and environment

The research found no named water, sewer, electric or gas provider for the tract, no FEMA flood zone or FIRM panel, no environmental report and no impact or connection fee schedule. It found no TxDOT count station at the site; TxDOT publishes its annual average daily traffic counts each July, with the current dataset published on July 22, 2024 [12], so the count is a pull, not a gap in the data. The exit number at the subject's interchange was not found. Each of these becomes a lender condition. For a dealership, the flood zone matters more than usual: a display lot holds millions of dollars of inventory at grade, and the floorplan lender will price that exposure.

Taxes

Inside city limits and Weatherford ISD, the 2025 rates are $0.392246 per $100 for the city, adopted September 9, 2025 [31], $1.0342 for the school district [32] and $0.285070 for Parker County [33]. Together that is $1.711516 per $100 before any emergency services district or junior college levy, neither of which was found. Parts and service carry the 8.25% local sales tax [34]; vehicles carry the 6.25% state motor vehicle sales tax, which the dealer collects and remits at titling [35]. Vehicle inventory is taxed through a unit property tax, and Parker County's 2025 unit factor for this city, school district and county combination is 0.001790 [50][36]. The model charges that factor against every vehicle sold, which treats the inventory tax as a cost to the dealer rather than an item passed to buyers.

A dealership earns money in four places: new vehicles, used vehicles, finance and insurance, and the service and parts department. The revenue build sizes each one against the average franchised store in NADA's 2025 profile, because the research has no Weatherford or brand-level data, and it states plainly where an assumption fills a gap.

New vehicles. The average store retailed 955 new vehicles in 2025 at an average price of $48,205 [39]. The model holds the price flat for five years; the average transaction price was already $50,562 in September 2026 [54], so the flat price is a cushion. Volume is the central assumption: the store retails 820 new vehicles in Year 3, rising toward the national average by Year 5. No ramp benchmark for an open point was found. The only first-month figure in the research, 400 vehicles at a new 83,000 sq ft Toyota store in Cape Coral, Florida, which opened on April 7, 2026 [52], comes from a far larger store in a different market and cannot stand in for a ramp here. Front-end gross is $2,764 per new vehicle, the second-quarter 2026 average in Haig Partners' sample, down from $3,284 a year earlier [44]. Haig notes its averages lean toward luxury and import stores, so a domestic or entry-level line could earn less.

Used vehicles. In 2025 new vehicles were 54.9% of the average store's sales dollars and used vehicles 31.8% [39]; the model keeps that ratio, so used sales follow new sales. Used gross is $1,634 per unit, Haig's second-quarter 2026 figure [44]. The research gives no used selling price, so the unit count rests on an assumed $30,000 average price, set above the $27,528 average amount financed on a used vehicle in late 2025 [39]. A lower price would mean more units and more gross, so the assumption errs low on gross.

Finance and insurance. F&I income is $2,703 per vehicle retailed, new and used, from AutoNation's first-quarter 2025 release [45]. That is a large public group's figure, and it is the only F&I figure in the research. A single-point start-up with a new F&I office may not reach it in Year 1, which is one reason the expense ratio starts high.

Service and parts. The average store sold $9,687,942 of service and parts in 2025 on 16,252 repair orders, with 16 technicians and a $186 hourly customer labor rate [39]. A new point starts with few of its brand's vehicles in operation locally, so the model starts the department at 72.0% of the average in Year 3 after a lower start, and holds a 37.6% gross margin. That margin is the 2025 figure for franchised truck dealers [42], the only service margin the research holds; it is used as a proxy and is likely conservative for a light-vehicle store.

On these drivers the store's revenue reaches $73,678,871 in Year 3 with $10,415,666 of gross profit. Revenue is still below the $108.401 million Texas average store in Year 5 [39], which is the right place for a single-line start-up to sit.

Dealership expenses are best benchmarked against gross profit, because gross, not sales, pays for people and advertising. AutoNation's selling, general and administrative expense ran at 67.9% of gross profit in fiscal 2025 and 68.2% in fiscal 2024 [46]. The model uses that ratio as the stabilized level and starts above it: a new store hires its sales, F&I and service staff before volume arrives and spends heavily on launch advertising. The ratio is 70.0% in Year 3 and reaches the benchmark in Year 5. For scale, the average store paid $5.61 million of payroll to 65 employees in 2025 and spent $586,246 on advertising [39]; both sit inside this line.

Floorplan interest is a dealer's largest financing cost after its real estate, and the 504 cannot carry it. The model holds 51 days of new and used inventory, the market-wide new-vehicle supply in June 2026, at a 7.50% rate. Days' supply varies widely by brand, from 25 for Toyota to 93 for the Chrysler, Dodge, Jeep and Ram line in the same report [44], so the sponsor's actual brand matters here. No manufacturer floorplan assistance is counted.

Property tax is charged on land, building, site work and equipment at cost and the combined 2025 rate, because the research found no assessment ratio. The vehicle inventory tax runs on sales. Insurance is a placeholder until a broker quotes garage liability, property and open-lot coverage; wind, hail, flood and wildfire conditions were not found.

Total operating expense is $8,513,170 in Year 3, leaving net operating income before real estate debt of $1,902,496, a 2.6% margin on revenue. NADA's guide for net profit on sales is 4.00% [41], measured after occupancy cost. The model's lower margin before any real estate cost is a conservative stance for a start-up, not a forecast that the store will underperform its peers.

Bank rate: Assumption: The research has no bank rate. Prime was 7.00% on October 9, 2026 [58]; the model uses 7.25% fixed. The rate is set at pricing. Debenture rate: Source [37].

An SBA 504 loan splits a project three ways: Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [56]. The split moves with the equity tier, and the tier depends on two facts about this borrower.

The first is age. The borrowing entity is a new business, two years old or less, so it needs 15% under SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026 [56]. The standard 10% is not available to it. The second is the property. SBA's special purpose list does not include dealerships, so a new dealership does not reach the 20% tier for a business that is both new and special purpose. The list does include service centers with pits and in-ground lifts [56]. This case specifies above-ground lifts for that reason, and the lender should get the CDC's reading of the actual service-department design in writing.

The debenture limit is $5 million. The higher $5.5 million limit is for small manufacturers, which a dealership is not. That limit is what breaks the sponsor's proposal: at the opening equity, the debenture would be $7,032,088. At the 15% tier alone the debenture would still sit above the limit, and the bank's first lien would have to carry the excess above its usual half of project cost. The restructure does not need that: the equity that coverage requires brings the debenture down to $4,690,793, under the limit, with the bank's first lien at $8,784,257, half of project cost.

The debenture carries a 25-year term, one of the 10, 20 or 25 years SBA allows, and the bank's term has a floor: 7 years for a 10-year debenture, 10 years for a 20- or 25-year debenture. The model amortizes the bank loan over 25 years. The debenture rate is 6.97%, the 25-year effective rate from the October 8, 2026 pricing, against a 5.85% debenture coupon [37]; the effective rate folds the ongoing program fees into the rate, so the model adds no separate annual fee. The rate is fixed only when the debenture is sold after construction, about a year from now, so it is a condition, not a fact. The upfront fee of 0.50% on the debenture is added to project cost, so the sponsor's equity share carries part of it [57]; the annual fee of 0.203% is among the ongoing fees inside the effective rate, and the lender should confirm the CDC's own pricing at closing. The bank rate is an assumption: Prime was 7.00% on the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [58], and the model uses 7.25% fixed.

Three eligibility points sit outside the numbers. Since March 1, 2026, every direct and indirect owner must be a U.S. citizen or national, under Policy Notice 5000-876441 issued February 2, 2026 [38]. SBA's 2025 SOP treated a new-vehicle dealer agreement as a franchise, so the agreement goes through franchise review and the brand must be on SBA's franchise directory [53]; the lender should confirm the same treatment under SOP 50 10 8.1. And the owner-occupied building must meet the 60% occupancy test for new construction [56]; a single-tenant dealership meets it easily. The lender does not need a feasibility study by rule: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [59]. On a start-up with no operating history, the lender and CDC will still want an independent read of what the store must sell. Our note on what SOP 50 10 8.1 changed for feasibility studies covers where projections still carry weight, and the SBA 7(a) and 504 practice explains how we build them for a CDC file.

As proposed: the sponsor proposes the standard 10% 504 injection, which puts the debenture at 40% of cost, with 10.0% equity ($1,758,022) and $15,822,198 of debt. The structure does not qualify: the equity injection is below the program minimum; the loan exceeds the program size limit; coverage falls below the floor.

As restructured: 23.3% equity ($4,093,464) and $13,475,050 of debt with $1,158,684 of annual debt service. Every test is met.

The 504 coverage floor is 1.15x [56]. SBA measures it on historical results where they exist; a new store has none, so the study tests the projection, and this case uses Year 2, the first full year after the launch.

As proposed. The sponsor's 10.0% injection fails the equity tier. It also pushes the debenture to $7,032,088, over the limit, and the resulting debt service of $1,357,228 is covered only 0.98x in Year 2. Any one of those failures stops the loan.

As restructured. The engine first lifts equity to the 15% tier and caps the debenture, which moves the excess onto the bank's first lien. That alone is not enough for Year 2 coverage, so equity rises again, to 23.3%, until coverage meets the floor at 1.15x. At that equity the debenture is $4,690,793, under the limit, and the bank's first lien returns to half of project cost. Total debt falls to $13,475,050 with $1,158,684 of annual debt service. Coverage is 1.64x in Year 3 and 2.14x by Year 5.

Year 1 is the problem the ratio hides. Coverage of 0.39x means the store needs a reserve, funded at closing, sized to carry the gap between Year 1 income and debt service. That reserve is additional sponsor cash, and a lender could reasonably ask for it as a condition rather than lean on the restructured equity alone. The alternative is a sponsor guaranty backed by the cash flow of other stores, which turns the analysis into a global cash flow test outside this study.

Risk Factors

What could break this dealership's coverage.

  • No award, no store. The research found no open line-make. If the sponsor's brand cannot be confirmed, the case does not exist; if the award comes with a smaller area of responsibility than the volume ramp implies, the Year 2 test fails.
  • A mature row with deep incumbents. The three largest dealer owners carry more than $63.8 million of 2025 market value between them [22], and their buildings already meet current manufacturer standards. A new import store competes for the same cross-shoppers on Fort Worth Highway.
  • Front-end gross is falling. New gross per unit fell 15.8% in a year in Haig's sample [44]. The model holds the lower figure flat; another year of compression would cut Year 2 gross before volume has built.
  • Import costs. A 25% tariff on imported vehicles applied from April 3, 2025, and on listed parts from May 3, 2025 [55]. Depending on the brand, that can raise prices and shrink allocations, which matters more to a new store than to an incumbent with an established allocation history.
  • Land priced at an asking level. The land price is an assumption at the 2026 asking level for I-20 frontage, not a sale. If the appraisal comes in lower, the first lien's loan-to-value rises, and the bank may ask for more equity at closing.
  • Construction cost. The building price is a 2021 registry estimate plus an allowance. Input costs rose 8.9% in a single recent year [48]; a contractor's bid above the allowance comes straight out of the sponsor's equity.
  • Special purpose reading. If the service department is built with in-ground lifts and the CDC reads it as a service center, the equity tier rises and the structure must be resized.
  • Thin first year. Year 1 coverage of 0.39x needs a reserve. Without one, a single slow quarter could put the loan behind before the store reaches volume.
  • Floorplan dependence. The store cannot operate without a floorplan line, which the 504 cannot finance. A floorplan lender that tightens advance rates or curtails the line in a downturn can starve the store of inventory regardless of its real estate coverage.

Other 504 cases on this site face different versions of the same structural questions. The Texas truck stop case shows a special purpose property where the higher equity tier applies, the Texas car wash case shows how a single-purpose building changes collateral, and the Texas RV and boat storage case shows a 504 project whose operating costs are far lower than a dealership's. Outside the 504 program, the Boerne assisted living and memory care case shows the same sizing logic under HUD Section 232, where coverage, not cost, sets the loan. Our special purpose property note sets out why that classification matters to a lender's recovery. The auto dealership hub covers subtypes beyond the franchised new-vehicle store, and the Texas market page and Dallas-Fort Worth page place Weatherford in its region.

Weatherford Dealership Questions

Questions lenders ask about this dealership case.

Why does the sponsor's standard injection fail for this Weatherford store?

The borrower is a new business, two years old or less, so the 504 tier is 15% under SOP 50 10 8.1 [56]. The standard 10% applies only to an established business buying a property that is not special purpose. A sponsor forming a new entity to hold a new franchise does not qualify for it, whatever its principals' record at other stores.

Why is the debenture capped at $5 million when SBA also cites $5.5 million?

The higher limit, $5.5 million, is reserved for small manufacturers. A franchised dealership retails vehicles; it does not make them, so its debenture stops at $5 million [56]. Everything above that has to come from the bank's first lien or from more equity. In this case the equity that Year 2 coverage demands is large enough to bring the debenture back under the limit on its own.

Is a car dealership a special purpose property for SBA 504?

Dealerships are not on SBA's special purpose list, but service centers with pits and in-ground lifts are [56]. A store designed with in-ground lifts could be read as falling into that category, which would lift the equity tier to 20% for a new business. This case specifies above-ground lifts and makes the CDC's written reading of the design a lender condition.

Does the 504 loan pay for the cars on the lot?

No. The 504 finances land, the building, site work and long-life equipment. New and used inventory is financed on a separate floorplan line, and SBA's 2025 SOP listed floor plan financing among the uses 7(a) and 504 proceeds cannot fund [53]. The floorplan lender, the manufacturer's working-capital requirement and the parts inventory sit outside this project budget and need their own commitments.

What does the lender need from the manufacturer before relying on the study?

A signed letter of intent or dealer agreement for a line-make with no store on the Weatherford corridor, the area of responsibility it covers, the manufacturer's planning volume for the point and its facility requirements. The research behind this case did not evidence an open point: every brand it checked already has a rooftop on the corridor.

Does SBA require a feasibility study for a dealership 504 loan?

No rule makes one mandatory. Under 13 CFR 120.160(b), SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey or a feasibility study [59]; a lender or CDC may ask for one at its discretion. On a ground-up start-up with no operating history, the study is the main evidence of repayment, and here its job is to show what the store must sell in Year 2 to carry its real estate debt.
