Study at a Glance

A new owner-occupied distribution building in Chesapeake, tested against the SBA 504 rules.

Feasibility Study Company is an industrial warehouse feasibility study company: we prepare these studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A warehouse study answers four questions in order: what the market shows, who else supplies it, what the building costs, and how much debt the operation can carry. This page puts those questions to one hypothetical project, an owner-occupied distribution warehouse financed with an SBA 504 loan in Chesapeake, Virginia. If you are looking for an industrial warehouse feasibility study consultant for a site of your own, ask us to run the same work.

The subject is generic: a single-story, unrefrigerated building of about 60,000 square feet on about 4.5 acres in southern Chesapeake, built for an established regional wholesale distributor that is leaving leased space. Each market figure is a dated public record. Each model figure is engine output on the input table near the foot of the page, where every line is tagged as sourced or assumed. This work sits in our industrial and warehouse practice, the city is part of the Hampton Roads market of Virginia, and the 504 rules the page applies are summarized on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

Market Conclusion

Hampton Roads can feed a distribution building. The research cannot price one, so the loan rests on the operator.

The demand answer is yes, with a limit on what the public record can say. In September 2026 the Virginia Beach-Chesapeake-Norfolk metro counted 832.9 thousand nonfarm jobs, drawing on a civilian labor force of 864,565 [1]. In March 2026 Chesapeake had 105,954 jobs in covered establishments [3]. A broker's Hampton Roads industrial report says leasing for the first three quarters of 2026 topped 2.3 million square feet and sales volume was above $600 million [11]. A market that trades and leases at that pace is not a market without industrial users.

The limit is price. The same broker page prints no vacancy rate, no asking rent and no absorption for Hampton Roads industrial space [11], and no other dated industrial rent for the metro is in the research. A study that sizes a loan on a rent the record does not contain would be guessing. This case therefore does not underwrite the building as a rental property. It underwrites the operating company that will sit in it, and it lists the missing rent, vacancy and land evidence as lender conditions so that a lender, a broker and an appraiser can fill them with signed leases and sale records.

The competitive answer is also thin. The research confirms one operator with its own website that shapes where cargo and warehousing demand goes: the state's port authority, which finished a harbor deepening in February 2026 and describes terminal capacity still to come [15]. It names no private warehouse owner, third-party logistics provider or developer with a page the study could verify. Those names are a lender condition, and the competitor census below says so.

The financing answer follows from the operator's numbers. Under the sponsor's 10.0% equity proposal the project costs $11,178,557, the bank lends $5,589,279 and the debenture is $4,471,423. The equity tier and the debenture limit both pass. Coverage does not: Year 2 debt service is covered 0.75x against a floor of 1.15x. Restructured, the sponsor brings $4,709,753, 42.2% of project cost, the debenture falls to $870,523, and Year 2 coverage reaches 1.15x. Stabilized coverage is 1.57x in Year 3.

Demand

A port-anchored labor market, a lean wage base and housing that workers can still find.

Employers and labor

Hampton Roads has the labor force a distribution operator hires from. The metro's nonfarm payroll of 832.9 thousand includes 53.5 thousand manufacturing jobs and 164.5 thousand government jobs, a composition that mixes defense and civil service with industry [1]. The area BLS publishes includes counties across the North Carolina line, so every metro-wide number in this case includes them, while the city figures do not [1]. A site is underwritten on the city it sits in, and this case reads Chesapeake first.

Chesapeake's covered employment was 105,954 in March 2026, Virginia Beach's 180,830 and Norfolk's 142,220 [3]. Average weekly wages in the first quarter of 2026 were $1,201 in Chesapeake, $1,215 in Virginia Beach and $1,531 in Norfolk [3]. Chesapeake's wage sits below Norfolk's by a wide margin, which matters to a labor-intensive business: a warehouse operator competes for dock and driver labor against every employer in the metro, not against Chesapeake's average. Across all occupations the metro's mean hourly wage was $30.83 in May 2025, and construction and extraction trades alone employed 41,190 people [2]. The second figure matters because a new building competes for the same trades as every other project in the metro while it is being built.

The state context is large. Virginia had an estimated 8.88 million residents on July 1, 2025 [4], an average weekly wage of $1,665 across 324,758 establishments in the first quarter of 2026 [3], and a 2025 gross domestic product of 807,925.0 million dollars [5]. The metro's own output was 127,459.149 million dollars for 2023, the last year of a series BEA has since discontinued [6]. The state numbers frame the customer base a regional distributor sells to; they do not size one operator's sales.

Workforce housing

Workers need somewhere to live within reach of the building. Median listing prices in September 2026 were $487,250 in Chesapeake, $487,000 in Virginia Beach and $349,900 in Norfolk, relayed from Realtor.com by FRED [8][7][9]. By the second quarter of 2026 the federal house price index for the metro stood at 388.95 [10]. The broker's apartment report counts vacancy of 4.4% and annual rent growth of 6.4% for the third quarter, while 2,156 units sit in its pipeline and more than 6,680 have been absorbed since 2024 began [12]. Tight apartments and high listing prices argue that a distributor must pay enough to draw labor from beyond the immediate neighborhood, which is why the model carries a payroll burden and a wage above the entry level.

The broker's retail report shows the same tightness from another side: vacancy of 4.1% and net absorption just under 310,000 square feet for the first three quarters of 2026 [13]. A distributor that sells to retailers and contractors depends on those customers staying busy.

Cargo, air traffic and power

The harbor is the reason a distribution building in Chesapeake is more than a local convenience. The state port authority reports a harbor depth of 55 feet after the deepening completed in February 2026, and a throughput capacity of 5.8 million TEUs once its North Berth work is complete [15]. The same release puts the deepening at $450 million and a rail capacity expansion at 2 million TEUs a year for $83 million [15]. Those are single projects and capacity statements, not volumes handled. The research found no 2025 or fiscal 2026 TEU total from the port, so this case does not claim that cargo volume is rising. It says that the infrastructure exists and is being extended.

Norfolk International Airport carried 4,892,594 passengers in 2025, up 0.6%, and 396,470 in December 2025 [14]. That is a measure of regional activity and not of air cargo, which the research does not report for Norfolk. Power is a cost line: Virginia's average retail electricity price was 10.62 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2024 on retail sales of 138,044,772 megawatt-hours [16]. That is an all-sector average, not an industrial tariff, and the model uses it as a placeholder for the utility's rate schedule.

Competitor Census

No research document names a competitor, so the census is a lender condition and the supply table stands in.

A census of competitors lists each operator that would bid for the same customers, with a page the operator controls. The research for this case contains no news item, broker report or agency list naming a Chesapeake distribution warehouse, third-party logistics provider or competing wholesaler. The only operator the research documents with its own release is the state port authority, which moves cargo through marine terminals and is the reason cargo reaches the region at all; it is not a competitor to a wholesale distributor's warehouse, and this study does not list it as one. Naming a business the record does not name would be invention. The census therefore becomes a lender condition, a roster of competing operators, each linked to its own website or to the county assessor's parcel record.

What the supply table tells a newcomer

The class is small in Chesapeake's own count and larger across the metro. A distributor that builds a new building adds to the stock of a market with a modest number of establishments in the proxy class and a much larger number of other industrial users. That says nothing about vacancy. The broker's industrial page for the metro reports leasing and sales totals but no vacancy or rent [11], so the competitive field is known only as a count of businesses and a pace of deals. The lender conditions ask for the missing field: the names, buildings, vacancy and asking rents of competing space within a short drive of the site, and the third-party logistics providers that would bid for the subject's customers.

One useful reading does come from the record. When a broker reports leasing above two million square feet and sales above six hundred million dollars in three quarters, owners and users are both active [11]. An owner-occupant that builds takes one user out of the leasing pool and puts a building into the sales pool when it leaves. That is a point for the lender's appraiser, who will ask what the building is worth to a user other than this operator.

Site

A generic southern Chesapeake industrial site: use, flood zone and utilities are still unknown.

The subject site is hypothetical. It is assumed to be an industrial parcel in southern Chesapeake with road access to an interstate or primary highway, large enough for the building, a truck court and trailer parking. The study names no parcel, street, owner or listing, and the research holds no Chesapeake site record. The land is a round assumption of about 4.5 acres at $250,000 an acre, set out in the inputs, and a purchase contract and appraisal replace it.

Several site facts decide whether the project can be built and none is in the research. Zoning must permit warehousing and distribution with outdoor truck movements, and the city's determination for the parcel is a lender condition. The flood zone matters more here than in many markets, because low ground is common near the water, and a building in a special flood hazard area carries insurance cost and elevation cost the model does not price beyond its allowances. The utility providers, the water and sewer capacity, the cost of taps and the stormwater rules are not in the research either. The only utility price the record gives is the state's average retail electricity price of 10.62 cents per kilowatt-hour [16], which the model uses for the power line.

Traffic is the third unknown. A distribution site lives on truck access: the distance to the nearest interchange, the weight limits and bridge clearances on the routes to the port and the customers, and the turning movements at the site entrance. The research holds no count station or route data for Chesapeake. The harbor depth of 55 feet and the planned terminal capacity of 5.8 million TEUs describe the port [15], not the roads between the port and the site. The lender conditions ask for a traffic count, a truck route plan and the interchange distance.

Labor is the last site factor. The workforce lives across the metro, and Chesapeake's covered employment of 105,954 in March 2026 [3] is smaller than Virginia Beach's 180,830 and Norfolk's 142,220 [3]. A site that can be reached from all three cities draws on the whole metro's labor force of 864,565 [1]. A site with poor commuting access would not.

Revenue Build

Customer accounts, average sales per account and the gross margin that pays for the building.

The operator is a regional wholesale distributor, an existing business that sells from inventory to contractors, retailers and institutions and delivers by its own trucks. It is not a start-up, and it is not a rental owner. Its revenue is the sales it makes, less the cost of the goods. That is why the case does not need a Hampton Roads industrial rent: the building is a cost of doing business for the operator, not an income property, and the rent the operator pays today in leased space disappears when it moves in.

Sales are the number of active accounts times the average sales per account. The model carries 470 accounts in the stabilized year, rising from a smaller base in Year 1, with $45,000 of sales per account and a gross margin of 23.0%. All three are assumptions. The research holds no wholesale sales, margin or account data for the market, and the study uses no figure from any operator's books. What the research does show is that the region supports activity: payroll in the metro stands at 832.9 thousand jobs [1], and the broker reports active leasing and sales of industrial property [11]. That is a reason to believe a distributor can grow accounts in the larger building. It is not a measure of how fast.

Operating Expenses

Payroll and trucks are the costs that follow sales; the building adds power, tax and upkeep.

A distributor's costs are mostly people and vehicles. The model carries warehouse payroll, driver payroll, a management and sales allowance, and a cost per delivery truck that covers its lease, fuel and repairs, so no truck is in the project budget. Payroll takes a burden for taxes, workers' compensation and benefits, and every cost except the building's own grows at the assumed inflation rate. The wage assumptions are deliberately lower than the area's all-occupation averages, which are $30.83 an hour for the metro [2] and $1,201 a week for Chesapeake [3], because those averages include every profession. The lender conditions ask for wage quotes from the trade area for dock and driver work.

The building's own costs are smaller but real. Electricity is the building area times a planning load times the state average price of 10.62 cents a kilowatt-hour [16]. Insurance, repairs, the replacement reserve and other utilities are allowances. Property tax is a planning share of cost because the research holds no Chesapeake rate or assessment ratio, so the real levy is a lender condition. Bad debt is a small share of sales, because a distributor extends trade credit.

Bank rate: Assumption: Prime of 7.00% on the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [19] plus a half point. No published 2026 bank terms for industrial real estate in Chesapeake were found; the bank sets the rate at pricing, and SBA caps the third-party spread. Debenture rate: Assumption: The research holds no debenture pricing. The study carries a round 6.5% for a 25-year debenture; the actual rate is set when the debenture is sold, and a CDC quote replaces it.

Three layers fund a 504 project. Under the rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [17]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the project is both [17].

The subject takes the standard tier on two readings the lender must confirm. The operator is an established company, not a business of two years or less. And a general-purpose dry distribution building is not the kind of property SBA describes as special purpose: a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [17]. A building with ordinary docks, racking and an office can be used by many distributors. A plan that added refrigeration over most of the floor would change that reading, and so would a newly formed operating company, so both are lender conditions.

The equity tier and the size limit pass at the sponsor's 10.0%. The debenture of $4,471,423 is under the $5 million limit for a project that is not a small manufacturer [17]. Coverage is what fails. As restructured, the debenture is $870,523, under the program limit, and the bank lends $5,580,276, 50.0% of cost. Maturities of 10, 20 or 25 years are offered, with the choice tied to whichever asset category holds the larger share of proceeds. Real estate dominates this budget, so the model picks the longest term and gives the bank loan an equal amortization.

Owner occupancy also shapes the design: the operating borrower has to fill 60% of a new building and 51% of an existing one [17]. The model has the operator use the whole building, so the test is met on its face, and a lender will check the plan for any space that might be sublet.

The debenture rate of 6.50% is an assumption, since the research holds no debenture pricing and the CDC fixes the real rate at sale. The bank rate of 7.50% is also an assumption: Prime was 7.00% in the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [19] plus a half point, and SBA limits the third-party spread to 6% over New York Prime [17]. The FY2027 upfront fee, 0.50%, is charged on the debenture and rolled into project cost [18]. SBA's jobs standard is one job per $95,000 of debenture for loans approved on or after October 1, 2025; at the restructured debenture the operator's payroll is far above what that standard asks, and a CDC confirms the count. A CDC serving Hampton Roads is a lender condition, since the research names none.

The 504 coverage standard is 1.15x. As proposed, at 10.0% equity, the equity tier and the size limit pass, and Year 2 coverage is 0.75x. Debt service of $857,948 a year is too heavy for the second-year earnings of a distributor whose sales are still growing into a larger building. Demand is not the problem here. Operating income of this size cannot carry a building of this cost.

As restructured, the engine searches for the contribution at which Year 2 coverage meets the floor. At 42.2% equity, debt service falls to $565,387, Year 2 coverage reaches 1.15x and Year 3 reaches 1.57x. The bank's share stays at half of cost, so additional equity is taken from the debenture, the longest and cheapest layer. The margin over the floor in the test year is narrow, which makes this the least equity that works and a poor target for underwriting.

A sponsor has three routes to a structure like this. One is cash, perhaps from selling the operator's current property. Another is a smaller building, or a lease, either of which shrinks the budget; the case does not model a lease. The third is evidence, in signed customer commitments and trailing sales, that accounts and margin run above the model. The original request is not one of the routes. Risks

What could break the Chesapeake projection.

  1. No industrial rent or vacancy in the record

    The broker's metro industrial page prints leasing and sales totals but no rent, vacancy or absorption [11]. If the market is soft, the building's resale value to another user is lower than cost, and the lender's collateral is weaker than this page can show.

  2. Gross margin

    The model rests on an assumed gross margin. Supplier price increases that cannot be passed to customers reduce coverage as surely as a loss of accounts does.

  3. Customer concentration and the account ramp

    A distributor that loses a large customer loses sales at once. The ramp in accounts is an assumption, not a market measure.

  4. Labor cost and availability

    The metro's mean wage is $30.83 an hour across occupations [2], and warehouse and driver labor competes with every employer in the metro. A wage higher than the model's raises payroll, the largest cost.

  5. Flood, wind and insurance

    Low ground and coastal exposure may add elevation, insurance and construction cost that the allowances do not capture. The research holds no premium or flood record for the site.

  6. Construction cost and timing

    The building cost is an assumption, and the metro competes for trades: construction and extraction occupations employed 41,190 people in the metro in May 2025 [2]. A bid above the model raises the project cost and the debt.

  7. Rates

    The bank rate is set at pricing and the debenture rate at sale. A higher rate raises debt service and lowers coverage in the same proportion.

  8. Port and trade exposure

    The port is an engine of regional logistics, but the research found no cargo volume [15]. A distributor that sells domestically leans on local construction, retail and institutions more than on the harbor.

Lender Conditions

Conditions before commitment.

These conditions name what the public record did not supply.

  1. Named competitor census

    A census naming each competing operator, linked to its own website or county assessor record: competing distributors, third-party logistics providers and the warehouse owners within a short drive of the site.

  2. Industrial rent, vacancy and absorption

    A broker opinion with Hampton Roads or Chesapeake industrial vacancy, asking rent and absorption for the building size. The broker page used here prints none [11].

  3. Site, title and zoning

    The assessor's parcel number, a written zoning letter, the land's appraisal, survey and sales record, the FEMA flood map panel and a Phase I environmental report.

  4. Traffic and access

    A traffic count for the access road, the nearest interchange distance, truck route and weight limits to the port and the main customers.

  5. Utilities, fees and taxes

    The utilities that serve the parcel, the industrial rate schedule, capacity fees, the adopted real estate tax rate and the assessment method.

  6. Operator records

    Trailing sales by account and customer, gross margin by product line, customer concentration, inventory turns, receivables aging and current lease terms, to replace the account, margin and cost assumptions.

  7. Costs and equipment

    Contractor bids, a construction cost comparison, racking and equipment quotes, and insurance quotes covering property, liability and flood.

  8. Program eligibility

    Confirmation that the operator is not a business of two years or less, that the building is not special purpose, that no refrigerated area is planned, and the operator's use of the whole building. The CDC and bank terms are set at pricing, along with the jobs standard for the debenture.

  9. Opening reserve

    Cash held back at closing for Year 1, when operating income will not yet reach debt service.

  10. Port cargo volume

    The port's cargo volumes for the most recent year, which the research did not find, to test whether regional logistics demand is growing [15].

Chesapeake Questions

What lenders ask about the Chesapeake warehouse case.

Why does a warehouse case have no market rent?

The research holds none. The broker's Hampton Roads industrial page prints leasing of more than 2.3 million square feet and sales above $600 million for the first three quarters of 2026 but no rent or vacancy [11]. The case underwrites the operator's sales and margin instead, and makes a broker's rent and vacancy opinion a lender condition.

Is Chesapeake the right place for a distribution building?

The record supports the region more than the city. Chesapeake had 105,954 covered jobs in March 2026 [3] inside a metro of 832.9 thousand jobs [1], and the port reports a harbor depth of 55 feet [15]. Site access, flood exposure and zoning decide the specific parcel, and none of them is in the research.

What makes the equity this warehouse needs so much higher than the minimum?

Two tests apply, and the second binds. An established operator contributes at least 10% of project cost [17], and projected coverage must also reach 1.15x [17]. A distributor still filling a larger building cannot service a loan sized at the minimum out of second-year earnings, so the engine adds equity until coverage reaches the floor.

Is a distribution warehouse special purpose property?

Not on its face. SBA defines the term as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [17]. A dry building with ordinary docks and racking suits many users, but a lender and the CDC decide, and a plan with refrigeration over most of the floor would change the answer.

Is a feasibility study mandatory for a warehouse loan?

Not as a blanket rule: the regulation reads 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [20], and the research note on that wording explains why "may" is not "must". Where a ground-up building has no market rent in the record, a lender often asks anyway, and an industrial warehouse feasibility study consultant can hand over the market read and the coverage test at the start.

Which site unknowns could end the project early?

The research holds no zoning, flood zone, utility or truck-route record for a Chesapeake parcel, and any one of them can end a site. Cost and rate assumptions then decide how much of the project the operator must fund.
