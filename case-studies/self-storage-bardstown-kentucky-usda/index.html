Self-Storage Feasibility Study Case: A Climate-Controlled Facility in Bardstown, Kentucky, with a USDA B&I Loan

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published October 29, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Bardstown's storage market is fragmented and mostly drive-up: of the operators confirmed on their own websites, only Heartland and Bluegrass advertise climate control [19][20], in a county that grew from 46,732 to 49,036 residents between April 2020 and July 2025 [1]. Zoning permits the use by right [8], but no operator posts a rent. At the sponsor's 20.0% equity the project covers debt 0.69x in Year 3; at 54.8% equity and a $1,922,626 guaranteed loan it reaches 1.25x.

Study at a Glance

A climate-controlled storage facility in Bardstown, tested against a USDA loan.

Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders, and this page shows what one looks like for a self-storage project. The subject is a hypothetical new facility in Bardstown, the Nelson County seat, built in two parts: a climate-controlled building of 30,000 net rentable square feet and a drive-up wing of 24,000, on about 3 acres along the US 150 corridor. It is financed with a USDA Business and Industry guaranteed loan. If you are weighing a project like this one, you can request a methodology briefing before you commit to land.

The market evidence below is real and dated, drawn from public sources. The site, the sponsor and the numbers in the model are hypothetical, and every model input that no source supports is labelled an assumption. For the wider picture, see our pages on self-storage feasibility, Kentucky markets and the USDA Rural Development programs.

Market Conclusion

Permitted by right, thinly supplied with climate control, and unproven on price.

Bardstown is a better storage market than its size suggests. Nelson County counted 49,036 residents on July 1, 2025, up from an April 2020 base of 46,732 [1]. Its households own their homes and stay in them: 9.7% of residents had moved in the past year, against 12.5% across Kentucky [2]. Tourism built around the bourbon trade produced an estimated $90.9 million of economic impact in 2024 [4], and distillers have kept committing capital to the town [5][6]. All of that creates storage demand of the ordinary kind (households with a full garage, retirees downsizing, small businesses with stock) and a seasonal commercial layer.

The supply side is where a climate-controlled project would find its opening. The census behind this page confirmed operators on their own websites. Only Heartland Self Storage and Bluegrass Storage advertise climate control [19][20]. The largest local multi-site operator describes itself as an outdoor operation [21]. The research found many more operators with Nelson County addresses in directories, almost all of them selling drive-up space. A climate-controlled building would not be the first in town, but it would join a very short list.

What the research could not find matters as much. No operator in the census posts a verified rate. No competitor's occupancy, unit count or assessed tax bill was found. No planned or permitted storage project turned up for 2025 or 2026, and absence in a search is not absence in the field. The zoning that makes the site easy to entitle also lets a competitor arrive without a public hearing, as the site section explains [8].

Those gaps shape the loan. With rents set as labelled placeholders, the model produces net operating income of $218,129 in Year 3 on a project that costs $4,253,598. The sponsor proposed 20.0% equity and a $3,438,967 guaranteed loan; that structure covers Year 3 debt service only 0.69x, against a 1.25x floor, and it falls short of the equity test as well. The engine restructures it to 54.8% equity and a $1,922,626 loan, which reaches 1.25x in Year 3. The finding is not that Bardstown cannot hold a new storage facility. It is that, at these costs and these placeholder rents, the market carries a modest loan, and a rent survey that comes back higher is the fastest way to a larger one.

Demand

Stable households, a bourbon economy and a metro commute.

Residents and households. Nelson County is growing faster than Kentucky as a whole: its population is up from 46,732 in April 2020 to 49,036 in July 2025, while the state moved from 4,506,287 to 4,606,864 [1]. The five-year survey for 2020 to 2024 counts 19,150 households in 20,330 housing units, and a median age of 39.5 years against 39.1 for the state [2]. Storage demand follows households, and a county of this size with this many homes has room for the garage-overflow customer who rents the first unit. The register's tenure and age counts, set out in the second table below, show how many of those homes are owner-occupied and how many residents are 65 or older.

A settled population. The survey puts the share of residents who moved in the past year at 9.7% in Nelson County and 12.5% statewide [2]. Low mobility cuts both ways for a storage owner. It means fewer move-in rentals of the kind that fill a facility in a college town or a military market. It also means the tenants who do rent may stay long, because their belongings come from an estate, a downsizing or a project that will take a year, and not from a short assignment. The research found no tenure data for any Bardstown facility, so the model does not claim a stay length; it assumes only a normal ramp to the Year 3 occupancy shown in the inputs.

Income and housing cost. Median household income in the county is $69,562, against $63,726 for Kentucky, and the median owner-occupied home is valued at $238,000, against $205,600 [2]. Residents who own a house worth that much have things to store and the means to rent space for them. The same survey puts the mean commute at 26.6 minutes, against 24.0 for the state [2]. A longer commute is a small sign that many residents work outside the county, and it raises the value of a facility on the way home.

Employers and the bourbon economy. A 2022 profile counted 2,612 manufacturing jobs at 59 establishments and 2,610 retail jobs in the county [3]. It named American Fuji Seal, Tower International, Heaven Hill, Sazerac and Toyota Boshoku Kentucky as the major employers [3]. Heaven Hill's new distillery, announced at a groundbreaking on June 6, 2022, carried a $135 million investment, 38 full-time jobs and an average wage of $33.62 an hour including benefits [5]. Bardstown Bourbon Company announced a $28.7 million expansion that spring on the same footing, adding 29 jobs and 55,000 barrels of annual capacity [6]. These are industrial wages, and employees on them rent storage for boats, seasonal gear and second vehicles.

Visitors. Visit Bardstown, citing the Kentucky Department of Tourism, put the county's 2024 tourism impact at $90.9 million, of which $62.6 million was direct visitor spending, supporting 450 tourism jobs directly and 623 in all [4]. A trade publication counted 11 distilleries within 16 miles of the court square in April 2025, when the town was preparing for its second National Bourbon Week [7]. Visitors do not rent storage. The businesses that serve them do: event suppliers, bourbon retailers, restaurateurs and the vendors who work the festival weeks. The model gives that commercial layer no revenue of its own and leaves it as upside.

What the research did not tie to Bardstown. Elizabethtown and Fort Knox lie in the adjacent region, and the research recorded one local operator naming Fort Knox among the areas it serves. The research found no figure that links the installation to Bardstown storage demand, so the model gives it no weight. It found no radius or drive-time demographics either, which is why the tables use the county as the reporting geography. Defining a trade area by drive time from the actual site is the first task of a full study.

Competitor Census

Five operators confirmed on their own sites, and one product gap.

The census started from a directory-based list of many storage operators with Bardstown or Coxs Creek addresses, assembled in October 2026. Each operator in the table below has its own website, which was opened on October 29, 2026 to confirm what it says about the business. Operators found only in directories, chamber listings or review sites are not in the table, because a directory entry can be out of date and a review site is not an operator's own statement. Their existence is a lender condition to confirm, not a fact this page relies on.

The rate column is empty on purpose. None of these sites posts a price: some tell visitors to call, some route them to an online rental page, and one refers to affordable rates without naming any. An earlier search snippet had shown drive-up rates for one operator, but the page behind it did not reproduce them when the research rechecked it, and its home page shows none today [23]. The research also found aggregator rates for facilities in Mount Washington and Shepherdsville, the next ring toward Louisville. They carry no publication date and the research itself called one of the aggregators unreliable, so this page prints none of them.

What the table shows. Most of the confirmed operators sell only drive-up space, and Heartland and Bluegrass are the only ones that advertise climate control. Heartland describes its Bardstown facility as one of the town's largest for climate-controlled space [19]. Bluegrass shows climate control on its own page but gives no sizes [20]. For a lender, that is a thin but real competitive set: a short list of climate-controlled alternatives for a county of this size [1].

What the table cannot show. The research found no unit counts, rentable square footage, land areas, assessed taxes or occupancy for any competitor. The competitor size that would matter most to the model, the climate-controlled square footage already in the county, is unknown. If Heartland and Bluegrass hold between them many times the subject's 30,000 square feet, the Year 3 occupancy ramp is optimistic, and if they hold far less, it is cautious. The county assessor's records and a drive-by survey settle the question, which is why they head the lender conditions.

The next ring. Mount Washington and Shepherdsville, the first towns toward Louisville on the KY 245 and Bluegrass Parkway route, hold national-brand and climate-controlled facilities according to the research. Their operators are outside the primary trade area and none is confirmed here, so they are context and not competitors in the table. The county's mean commute of 26.6 minutes [2] suggests that some Bardstown residents already travel toward that metro area for work.

Pipeline. No planned or under-construction storage project in Bardstown or Nelson County was found for 2025 or 2026. The research did not search planning agendas or newspaper archives in full, so this is a gap in the search and not a finding that nothing is coming. The Development Review Board checks commercial plans for compatibility with their surroundings [12], and because storage is permitted by right in the B-3 district, a new competitor may need only site plan review and no public hearing [8]. Pipeline visibility is therefore structurally low, and the lender should read the planning commission's agendas and the permit log before closing.

Site

A generic commercial tract where storage is a principal use.

The hypothetical site is about 3 acres of commercial land on the US 150 corridor in Bardstown. US 150, locally Springfield Road, connects the town to KY 245, which runs on to the Bluegrass Parkway and, beyond it, to I-65 near Shepherdsville. The Kentucky Transportation Cabinet's District 4 project list shows corridor work on US 150 in Bardstown, and the construction schedule near the site belongs in the lender's file [13]. No particular tract is assumed, and the study uses no listing, parcel or owner.

Zoning. The model assumes a tract in the B-3 Regional Retail Business district. The Joint City-County Planning Commission's Section 6.13 lists mini-warehouses among the district's principal permitted uses, so a storage facility needs no conditional use permit there [8]. The same section limits buildings to 50 feet in height, requires a 50-foot front setback, side yards of 15 feet (25 feet beside residential land), a rear yard of 25 feet and 20% open space [8]. A site of about 3 acres with two single-story buildings clears those limits with room for drive aisles, but the civil engineer has to prove it on a real parcel. Revised joint zoning and subdivision regulations took effect on April 8, 2025, and the Fiscal Court passed revised county regulations that define the mini-warehouse on June 3, 2025 [9][10]. A lender should confirm that the district text above survived those revisions for the parcel in question.

Approvals. By-right use shortens the entitlement path to a land disturbance permit, a building and zoning compliance permit through county code enforcement and the planning commission, and review by the city's Development Review Board [11][12]. The research found no published approval timeline, no impact or connection fees and no local construction cost index, so those items are conditions and not numbers.

Utilities, flood and traffic. Water, sewer, electric and gas service to the hypothetical tract are unknown and become lender conditions: will-serve letters, tap and impact fees, and the electric load for heating, cooling and dehumidifying a climate-controlled building. A FEMA flood zone determination for the parcel is a condition too, as is a count of average daily traffic on US 150 at the site from the state's count stations. None was retrieved. Storage tenants drive past a facility more than they search for one, so a traffic count is more than a formality for a drive-up wing.

Property tax. The tax bill depends on whether the parcel lies inside the city and in the Bardstown City Schools district or in the county school district. The model takes the more expensive case. Bardstown City Schools set a real property rate of 79.10 cents per $100 and a personal property rate of 81.30 cents, approved on September 29, 2026 [14]. The city held its own rate at 16.10 cents for fiscal 2025-26 [15]. Nelson County Schools cut their real rate to 67.0 cents from 67.3 in August 2025 [16], and the county Fiscal Court set 12.0 cents in 2024, down from 12.7 [17]. The 2025 county rate was not found. The county's 2025 tax roll was certified on July 22, 2025 [18]. State and library rates were not found as dated figures, so the model carries a placeholder of 0.16% for both. The sum of the dated components is applied to cost, not to an assessed value, which the property valuation administrator will set.

Revenue is $180,274 in Year 1, $354,307 in Year 2 and $480,206 in Year 3, the first stabilized year. The build is simple: net rentable area, times occupancy, times twelve months, times a rent per square foot, for each product, plus a share for fees and retail.

Climate-controlled rent. The model charges $1.06 per square foot per month in Year 3, after modest annual increases from a round opening rate. It is the least-supported number in the model. The operators confirmed in the census post no climate-controlled rate on their own sites [19][20][22][23], and the aggregator listings the research found for the next ring could not be dated. A secret-shopper survey of the two local climate-controlled operators, by phone and by web quote, is the first lender condition, and a rent above or below the placeholder moves the whole determination.

Drive-up rent. The drive-up wing earns $0.64 per square foot per month in Year 3, below the climate rate by a margin that is itself an assumption. Most of the confirmed operators sell only this product [21][22][23], and it is the cheap end of the market. The wing exists to give the facility an entry price and a place to fill quickly, not to earn the project's return.

Occupancy. Climate-controlled space reaches 78.0% in Year 3 and drive-up space 82.0%. The ramp is a convention, not a finding: no competitor occupancy exists in the research. The ramp also assumes that the facility opens in one phase. A sponsor who built the drive-up wing first and added climate control after the first lease-up would have a different Year 1 and Year 2, and would carry less construction debt at the start.

Other income. Administrative fees, tenant protection plans and lock and box sales are carried at 7.0% of rent. The research held no local benchmark, so the share is an allowance and a lender will ask for the operator's actual attach rate at comparable facilities.

Payroll starts at $85,000 and grows 3.0% a year. It pays a manager and part-time cover for a site that is open to tenants around the clock through its gate and staffed only by day. The confirmed climate-controlled competitors advertise on-site or remote management [19][20], and the sponsor's plan decides which model applies. Utilities run at 5.5% of revenue because the climate-controlled building must be heated, cooled and dehumidified all year. Repairs, marketing, administration and a management fee run as shares of revenue.

Property tax applies the four rates in the inputs table (city, city schools, county, and a placeholder for state and library) to land plus improvements at cost, including the contingency, and grows 2.0% a year. The assessment will not equal cost, and the PVA's value is a lender condition. Insurance is a placeholder of $19,000 in Year 1, because the research found no insurance terms; a bound quote that includes wind cover must replace it. The USDA annual retention fee of 0.55% is shown, on a base the model sets at the outstanding guaranteed balance (the terms sheet does not print it), as a cost on the assumption that the lender passes it to the borrower [24]. The replacement reserve is a modest allowance per square foot for roofs, doors, paving and mechanical equipment.

Total project cost is $4,253,598 as restructured, or $79 per net rentable square foot. The hard-cost inputs, $62 per square foot for the climate-controlled building and $29 for the drive-up wing, are placeholders: the research found no Bardstown construction cost index and no bid, and the contractor's bid is a lender condition. Site work of $380,000 is an allowance for grading, stormwater, paving, fencing and a keypad gate.

Land is carried at $160,000 an acre for about 3 acres. The research held asking prices for listed commercial tracts in and near Bardstown, but an asking price is a seller's opening position and the study does not use any of them. The land figure is a round placeholder for improved commercial land, and an appraisal with comparable closed sales replaces it. Because land is a minor part of the budget, a miss on land moves the answer less than a miss on rent or construction cost.

The USDA guarantee fee of 3.5% is added to project cost and shown as a use. It is the 3.0% initial fee plus the 0.50% charged when the guarantee issues before construction is complete, as it would for a ground-up building [24]. The terms sheet does not print the base the fee applies to, so the model applies it to the guaranteed amount and the lender must confirm the base. The model also funds a lease-up reserve of $200,000 inside the loan budget because the Year 1 shortfall in the pro forma shows the need; whether working capital may sit inside a guaranteed B&I loan is a lender condition.

The guarantee. For fiscal 2026 the Business and Industry program guarantees 85% of a loan under $5,000,000 and 80% of a loan from $5,000,000 to $25,000,000, with an initial fee of 3.0%, an annual retention fee of 0.55% and a 0.50% fee for a guarantee issued before construction is complete [24]. A reduced-fee category charges 1.0% and 0.50% but guarantees only 80% [24]. The 85% tier replaced 80% in a notice published on March 9, 2026, effective October 1, 2025, and USDA announced it on March 27 [24][28]. A loan of $1,922,626 is well inside the 85% tier.

Eligibility. USDA's December 2021 rule removed the old ineligibility of self-storage facilities, so a storage project is no longer excluded by type [33]. Kentucky's program page lists the purchase and development of land, buildings and infrastructure for commercial properties among eligible uses and requires hazard insurance on collateral [34]. The project site must be in a rural area, which the program defines as outside a city or town of more than 50,000 people, with the project site governing [29][28]. The research did not retrieve the eligibility map result for any Bardstown parcel, so a determination for the actual site is a lender condition.

Rate and term. The lender and the borrower negotiate the rate, fixed or variable, and the term runs to the useful life of the collateral, up to 40 years or the state-statute limit, whichever is less [26]. The model carries 7.75%, which is Prime of 7.00% on October 9, 2026 [35] plus 0.75 points, and amortizes over 25 years because roofs, paving and mechanical systems wear out well before 40 years.

What the terms sheet leaves open. The USDA terms in the research print no equity minimum and no coverage ratio for B&I, and no separate term for real estate. The study therefore tests two lender-style parameters and labels both as assumptions: 25.0% equity and a 1.25x coverage floor in Year 3. The regulation asks for projections through at least two years at stable operations [25], which is why Year 3, the first stabilized year, is the test year. The lender's credit evaluation must include a written evaluation of the feasibility study [27].

The feasibility study. A guaranteed loan of more than $1,000,000 to a new business requires a study prepared by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to USDA [25]. The restructured loan of $1,922,626 is over that line, and for a loan of $1,000,000 or less USDA may still require one if the analysis is insufficient [25]. This page shows the market and financial sections that such a study contains; USDA sets the scope case by case.

The program's own risks. The fiscal 2027 budget request proposes no Business and Industry program level [29]. In May 2026 USDA revoked the approved-lender status of ten lenders whose portfolios held about $620 million in delinquent loans, roughly 47% of Rural Development's delinquent loans, and said more than 750 lenders remained [30]. A trade-association study reported that Kentucky received $470 million in guaranteed volume across 74 loans from 2012 to 2022 [31], and a $9.95 million guaranteed loan for an 81-room hotel in Campbellsville was announced in October 2026 [32]. The research found no Nelson County closing from 2024 to 2026, and the city's industrial development board lists local banks without any recorded Business and Industry activity [12]. Finding a lender that is both approved and active is a task of the engagement, not a given.

Risks

What could break the case.

  1. Unverified rents

    No Bardstown operator posts a rate on its own site [19][20][21][22][23]. The model's climate-controlled rent is a placeholder, and the determination swings with it. The survey is not optional.

  2. Easy entry for competitors

    Mini-warehouses are a principal permitted use in the B-3 district [8], so a rival can file a site plan and build without a hearing. The research found no storage project in the pipeline, but it did not search the planning agendas in full.

  3. A short list of climate-controlled rivals

    Only Heartland and Bluegrass advertise climate control [19][20]. If either expands, or a national operator from the next ring enters Bardstown, the scarcity that supports the premium rent disappears.

  4. Demand tied to one industry

    Tourism produced $90.9 million of impact in 2024 [4], and the major employers named in 2022 center on distilling and its suppliers [3]. A downturn in bourbon would reach commercial storage demand and household income at once.

  5. Construction cost

    The research found no local cost index, and a metal building with heating, cooling and dehumidification is a mechanical project as well as a shell. A cost overrun comes straight out of the loan capacity shown here.

  6. Wind and insurance

    Insurance terms were not found. A light metal building in central Kentucky needs wind cover, and a premium far above the placeholder would cut net income directly.

  7. Program funding and lender base

    The fiscal 2027 request proposes no Business and Industry program level [29], and ten lenders lost approved status in May 2026 [30]. A delayed appropriation or a lender exit can stall the guarantee after the land is bought.

  8. Tax district

    The city schools' real property rate is 79.10 cents per $100 and the county schools' is 67.0 cents [14][16]. The district for the actual parcel changes the tax line.

Lender Conditions

Open items to close before underwriting.

Every item the research could not find becomes a condition. They are listed in the order a lender would want them closed.

  1. Rents. A secret-shopper survey of posted and quoted rates for climate-controlled and drive-up units at every operator in the census, by size and with any concession, on the same date.
  2. Competitor size and occupancy. Unit counts, rentable square feet, land area, assessed value and tax for each operator, from the Nelson County property valuation administrator's records and a site visit, and an occupancy reading from the operators who will share one.
  3. Pipeline. A review of the planning commission and Development Review Board agendas, the permit log for new storage buildings, and local news for 2025 and 2026, in Bardstown, Coxs Creek and the next ring.
  4. Rural eligibility. A USDA eligibility determination for the exact parcel, printed and placed in the file.
  5. Site and title. An appraisal with closed sales of commercial land in town, a survey, a title commitment and confirmation that the parcel is in the B-3 district and that Section 6.13 still reads as described after the 2025 revisions [8][9][10].
  6. Utilities. Will-serve letters for water, sewer and electric, the electrical load for climate control, gas service where needed, and tap and impact fees.
  7. Flood, traffic and access. A FEMA flood zone determination for the parcel, a state traffic count for US 150 at the site, and the driveway permit.
  8. Construction cost. Contractor bids for the climate-controlled building, the drive-up wing and the site work, and an engineer's estimate of heating, cooling and dehumidification load.
  9. Taxes. The parcel's school district, the 2025 Fiscal Court rate, the state and library rates from the Department of Revenue's rate book, and the assessment ratio.
  10. Insurance. A bound quote for property and liability that includes wind and tornado cover.
  11. Lender and program. A USDA-approved lender with Business and Industry experience, its credit memo on equity and coverage, and the independent feasibility study that USDA requires above $1,000,000 for a new business [25].
  12. Permits and licenses. The land disturbance permit and the city's business license and occupational tax requirements, none of which the research retrieved [11].
  13. Phasing. A decision on whether the climate wing opens with the drive-up wing or follows it.
Bardstown Self-Storage Questions

Questions lenders ask about this Bardstown self-storage case.

Is self-storage eligible for a USDA Business and Industry loan?

Yes. USDA's December 2021 final rule removed the paragraph that had made self-storage facilities ineligible, so the project is judged on the program's ordinary tests, not excluded by type [33]. The site still has to lie in a rural area, meaning outside a city or town of more than 50,000 people, and the research could not retrieve the map result for any Bardstown parcel [29].

Does USDA require a feasibility study for this project?

The restructured loan is well over $1,000,000 and the borrower is a new business, which is the combination for which 7 CFR 5001.306 calls for a study by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to the Agency [25]. For a loan of $1,000,000 or less, USDA may still ask for one if the lender's analysis is not enough. The lender then writes its own evaluation of the study into the credit file [27].

Why does the model restructure the sponsor's loan?

The proposed equity share leaves too little net income to pay a larger loan: Year 3 coverage falls below the floor the study applies, and the structure also misses the equity test. The engine therefore raises equity and cuts the loan until Year 3 coverage reaches the floor; the page's determination table gives both structures. The rents behind that income are placeholders, so a rent survey could move the answer in either direction.

Can a new competitor open next to this site without a public hearing?

Possibly. Mini-warehouses are a principal permitted use in the B-3 Regional Retail Business district, so a conditional use permit is not needed there [8]. A project may still need site plan review by the city's Development Review Board [12]. That is why the research could not rule out a pipeline it did not find, and why the lender should read the agendas before closing.

Why does this page print no competitor rents?

None of the operators confirmed in the census posts a price on its own site [19][20][21][22][23]. The research found aggregator rates for facilities in the next ring toward Louisville, but they carry no publication date, so this page uses none. The model's rents are labelled assumptions, and a secret-shopper survey is the first lender condition.

What would a full study add to this worked case?

It would put the model on a real parcel: drive-time demographics from the site, a count of the climate-controlled square feet within the trade area, bids for the buildings, a bound insurance quote and a rent survey. Those replace the placeholders in the inputs table, and the loan the market supports could be higher or lower than the figure shown here.
