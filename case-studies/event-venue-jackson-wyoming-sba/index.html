Event and Wedding Venue Feasibility Study Case: Jackson, Wyoming, SBA 504

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published November 4, 2026 · Data as of November 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Grand Teton drew 3,800,648 recreation visits in 2025 [6] and Jackson Hole Airport boarded 598,923 passengers in the year to July 2026 [5], so a Jackson wedding venue can find guests. No competing venue or booking calendar is public, and a start-up venue carries a year of fixed cost against a short season. An event venue feasibility study consultant tests the rest: at the minimum contribution Year 2 coverage is 0.74x, and only 42.2% equity reaches the floor.

Study at a Glance

A new wedding and event venue in Jackson, tested against the SBA 504 rules.

Feasibility Study Company prepares feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders. If you are looking for an event venue feasibility study consultant, or an event venue feasibility study company that shows its arithmetic, this page is a worked example. It follows one hypothetical wedding and event venue in Jackson, Wyoming, financed with an SBA 504 loan, from the public market record to a debt service coverage test. The subject is generic: a single-story hall of about 4,500 square feet that seats about 150 guests, with a terrace, a ceremony lawn and 75 parking spaces, on about 2.0 acres along the US-26/89/191 corridor in Teton County. No parcel, owner, operator or listing is behind it. Every market figure below is a dated public record, while the model figures are engine results that rest on the input list at the foot of the page, where each input says whether a source or an assumption sets it. The venue is part of our event and wedding venue practice, the town is in Wyoming, and the 504 rules in play are explained on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

Market Conclusion

Jackson has the guests. The bookings, the rates and the land price are what the public record leaves out.

The demand answer is yes, with a caution about timing. Teton County had 23.333 thousand residents on July 1, 2025 [1], and it sits between two national parks. Grand Teton recorded 3,800,648 recreation visits in 2025 [6], and Jackson Hole Airport boarded 598,923 passengers in the fiscal year that ended in July 2026 [5]. A wedding venue sells a place that guests are willing to travel to, and the county already moves guests at that scale. The county also tilts toward the visitor trade: one report put its share of the state's visitor spending on goods and services at 35% in 2024 [7].

The caution is the calendar. Unemployment in the county was 1.7% in June 2026 and 2.8% in February 2026 [11][12], a swing that shows how much of the work in the county follows the seasons. A venue lives on the same curve. Its wedding dates sell from late spring through early autumn, and its payroll, insurance and heat run all year.

The competitive answer is that the public record does not name a single competing venue. The research found no venue, caterer, planner or booking calendar in Teton County. The only count of the field is a proxy from the Census Bureau's County Business Patterns: 16 establishments in the two industry codes that hold event venues in 2023. The competitors section sets it out and turns the census into a lender condition.

The land answer is that the research holds no Teton County land sale, and the county's median home value is far above any figure in this study's state [2]. The model carries land at a round planning price, and the appraisal will move it.

The financing answer follows from those. Under the sponsor's 10.0% equity proposal the project costs $4,702,605, and Year 2 coverage is 0.74x against a floor of 1.15x. The equity tier fails as well, because a new business owes more than the standard contribution. Restructured, the sponsor brings $1,981,303, 42.2% of project cost, and the model reaches 1.15x in Year 2 and 1.73x at stabilization. The sponsor would be buying a different deal from the one proposed, and the SBA debenture shrinks to $366,212, so most of the 504 program's leverage is gone.

Millions of park visits, a record airport year and a county that stays near full employment.

The guests: parks and the airport

Two measures of arrival carry most of the weight. Grand Teton counted 3,800,648 recreation visits in 2025, according to the National Park Service [6]. The Jackson Hole News&Guide's Market Watch column, reporting the airport's and the park service's figures, put the airport at 598,923 enplanements and the park at 3,806,677 recreational visits for the fiscal year that ended in July 2026 [5]. The same column counted 83,388 enplanements in July 2026 and 773,094 park visits that month, and its headline called the airport's July a record for monthly traffic [5]. Enplanements are boardings, so each is a person leaving. A venue's weekend guests mostly arrive on the same flights and leave on them.

The neighbouring national park to the north adds to the pool of travelers who might extend a trip. Yellowstone recorded 4,762,988 recreation visits and 1,238,983 overnight stays in 2025 [17]. In 2026 its May count was 570,272 [18], June was 903,025 and the first half of the year was 1,676,678 [19]. The first seven months reached 2,635,384 [20], August was 856,467 [21], September was 868,450 and the first nine months totaled 4,360,300 [22]. The News&Guide's column put July at 958,705 [5]. Yellowstone spans three states, so these are whole-park counts and not a measure of Teton County, but they show how long the travel season runs: more than 850,000 visits in each of August and September 2026 [21][22].

The visitor economy

The Wyoming Office of Tourism's 2024 study, as Wyoming Public Media reported it, had Teton County at 35% of the $4.9 billion that visitors spent on goods and services in the state [7]. Lodging tax follows the same pattern. Teton County's collections rose 6.4% in fiscal 2025, according to a radio station's account of the state Economic Analysis Division's report [8], and its sales and use tax collections rose 4.2% in the same year, while the statewide total fell [9]. The most recent annual report of the county's travel and tourism board that the research reached covered July 2022 to June 2023, and a local outlet reported $10,095,202.90 of lodging tax revenue and 7,890 tourism-related jobs for that year [10]. That is older data, and the 2025 annual report was not found. It still shows what the visitor trade means to the county's payroll.

Statewide, the Office of Tourism's figures, relayed by a Newcastle newspaper, put travel spending at $5 billion in 2025, visitor-generated state and local taxes at $291.8 million and travel industry earnings at $1.47 billion [14]. Overnight visitors numbered 8.8 million in 2025 [14]; a separate report counted 8.7 million visitors in 2024 [15]. A venue needs to know whether that trade is growing and not just large. Statewide lodging tax collections reached $67.4 million in fiscal 2025 [8], but the 4% lodging sales and use tax fell 4.2% in March 2026 against March 2025 [16]. The signal is mixed: spending is at a record, and one early-2026 month of lodging tax was soft.

The labor market a venue hires from

County unemployment is the first number a venue owner should check, because staffing a seasonal business at a low rate of joblessness is hard. Teton County had the lowest rate in the state in every month the research reached: 1.6% in September 2025 [13], 2.8% in February 2026 [12] and 1.7% in June 2026 [11]. The February figure is the seasonal high and the June figure is the summer low, a spread of more than a point that gives the season's shape in one comparison. The statewide labor market is steadier. Wyoming had 302,100 nonfarm jobs in August 2025 [25] and 304,100 in June 2026 [11]. Private-sector openings created 4,143 jobs in the fourth quarter of 2025 while closings removed 2,668 [23]; the private-sector net job change for the quarter, which also counts expanding and contracting establishments, was +2,174 [23]. In the year to the fourth quarter of 2024, covered employment rose by 1,805 jobs, or 0.6%, and payroll by $194.8 million, or 4.5% [24].

The register describes a wealthy county. Personal income per resident is very high because the measure includes investment income and rent, which accrue to many second-home owners, and the median home value is the clearest sign of the cost of land and housing [2][4]. Households of that kind host destination weddings and family gatherings. They are also the reason a venue's staff cannot easily live nearby, a cost the model carries in its payroll. Median household income is the figure the lender will see first [2], and it is not a measure of what a wedding customer spends: most customers are not residents.

Competitor Census

No competing venue is named in the public record. The supply count is a proxy, and the census is a lender condition.

A competitor census would name each rival venue with its capacity, rates, booking calendar and website. This study could not build one. None of the research documents for Jackson or for Wyoming names a wedding or event venue, and a name found only in a marketplace listing or a search result would be a claim that no owner confirmed. The study does not invent a competitor, and it names none that it cannot link to the operator's own website or the county assessor record. A census that names each rival and links its own website or assessor record is therefore the first lender condition for this project.

What the public record can count is the supply of establishments in the nearest industry codes. What the proxy count says

Teton County has 6.89 establishments per 10,000 residents in the proxy class, against 2.40 for Wyoming as a whole [3]. The county's density is nearly three times the state's, which is what a visitor economy would produce, and it is the first sign that a newcomer would enter a contested market and not an empty one. The count overstates the number of true venues, because it includes every lessor of nonresidential buildings, such as the owners of offices and shops. It also leaves out an event hall that a hotel, a ranch or a restaurant operates as part of its main business. The lender should read the figure as a ceiling on dedicated venues in one respect and a floor on event capacity in another, and neither is the number of rooms that can host a wedding on a Saturday in July.

The class holds 81 paid employees across those 16 establishments [3]. Five employees an establishment is the arithmetic, and it describes small businesses and sole lessors better than staffed venues. A fully staffed venue would be at the top of that range. The metro area's count is two establishments higher than the county's, which means that nearly all of the supply sits in the Wyoming county [3]. The register does not publish employment for the metro area in this class on the page, and the table says so.

Lodging beside the venue

Guests also need rooms. The register counts 41 hotel and motel establishments in the county in 2023 [3]. The class is not mutually exclusive with the venue class and the two counts are never added. The point is that a wedding of this size needs rooms that the venue does not own, and the lodging stock in the county is a dependency the sponsor cannot control. Teton County's lodging tax revenue of $10,095,202.90 in the year to June 2023 [10] gives the scale of the trade that fills those rooms.

What the census must still show

A named census would add, for each rival, the seated capacity, whether the property permits outside caterers and alcohol, the weekend dates open in the next season, the published or quoted rental fee, the lodging on site and the year opened. The model needs two figures from it above all, the fee a couple pays for a Saturday and the share of summer dates already booked, and neither is in the public record this study could read. The model carries the fee as an assumption and the number of weddings as a ramp, and both are replaced when the census arrives.

Site

The corridor is named. The parcel, the zoning, the utilities and the traffic count are not.

The assumed site covers about 2.0 acres on the US-26/89/191 corridor in Teton County. The parcel, the zoning district, the access permit and the utility territory all remain to be confirmed. The research did not retrieve a traffic count for the corridor, a zoning district for the site, or any building permit or short-term rental rule with a date, and it listed all three as not found. The paragraphs below describe what each gap means for the loan.

Zoning, access and traffic

A wedding venue is a place of public assembly that serves alcohol, hosts amplified music at night and draws hundreds of car trips on a Saturday. Whether the county or the town allows it on the site, under what conditions, with what hours and noise limits, and with what road access, decides whether the building can be used at all. The research could not establish any of these. The lender needs a written zoning determination for the actual parcel and the permit history for the use. A venue needs visibility less than a retail store does, because couples book it in advance, but it needs a safe turning lane and a clear route for shuttles, and a state traffic count for the corridor would show the roadway's capacity. No count was found.

Utilities, wastewater and water

A hall that seats 150 guests discharges a great deal of wastewater on a single day, and a site outside the town's sewer needs an engineered septic system or a package plant. The model carries a site work allowance of $420,000 that covers a line extension or an on-site system, and no provider was confirmed. The same site also needs fire flow. If the water comes from a well, the supply for a large event and for fire protection must be proved. These items can cost more than the allowance, and the utility answer is a lender condition.

Land

The research holds no Teton County land sale, asking price or assessed value, and the county's assessed valuation and mill levy came back from the research as an unreadable file. A listing's price would describe one property and not the market, so the study cites none. The model carries land at a round $650,000 an acre, a planning figure chosen with the county's median home value of $1,633,900 in mind [2]. It is not a sale price. The appraisal and an executed purchase contract take its place, and a lender should expect the appraisal to move the project.

Property tax

The research does not hold Teton County's assessed valuation or mill levy. The model carries property tax at a planning share of 0.65% of land, building and site cost, and the actual levy on a commercial parcel is a lender condition.

Bank rate: Assumption: Prime of 7.00% on the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [28] plus a half point for a start-up venue. No published 2026 bank terms for event-venue real estate in Teton County were found; the bank sets the rate at pricing, and SBA caps the third-party spread. Debenture rate: Assumption: A round planning rate. No debenture pricing notice is in this case's research, and the rate is set when the debenture is sold. A CDC's rate sheet on the day of application replaces it.

A 504 project stacks three funding layers. Under the rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [26]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or a limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the business is both [26]. The subject's borrower is a new business, so the model applies the middle tier. The sponsor in this case proposes the standard contribution of 10.0%, which fails the tier before any coverage test is run.

Whether the building is a limited or special purpose property is a question for the CDC. SBA describes such a property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [26], and gives an example list that is not all-inclusive. A single-story hall with a kitchen shell and a dance floor is a reasonable candidate for that treatment. The model does not assume it, and a CDC that did would move the contribution to the top tier. That is a lender condition and a risk.

At the proposed contribution the debenture would be $1,881,042, under the $5 million limit [26], so the size test passes. At 42.2% the debenture falls to $366,212, under the program limit, and the bank lends $2,347,516, 50.0% of cost. The debenture can run 10, 20 or 25 years, and the larger asset category sets the maturity; land, building and site work dominate the proceeds, so the model uses the longest term and matches the bank amortization to it [26]. Because the bank's share is fixed at half of cost, extra equity can only come out of the debenture, the cheaper and longer money, so the restructured project keeps little of what the program offers.

The owner-occupancy test shapes who holds the building. For a new building the test is 60% [26]. The operating company occupies the whole hall, so the test is met if the company owns the property or leases it from an affiliate under the program's rules, which a lender or CDC will want documented. The debenture rate of 7.00% is an assumption that the sale of the debenture will settle, and the bank rate of 7.50% is another: Prime was 7.00% in the October 9, 2026 H.15 release [28], plus half a point for a start-up venue, while the third-party spread is capped at 6% over New York Prime [26]. The FY2027 upfront fee is 0.50% of the debenture and joins project cost [27].

The text of the rule is 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [29], so the lender decides file by file. A ground-up venue run by a start-up borrower is a likely candidate for the request. Our event and wedding venue hub explains how a venue is valued on its cash flow and its building separately.

Risks

What could break the Jackson projection.

  1. The ramp is slower than modelled

    The Year 2 test leaves no room. A venue that books fewer dates in its second season fails the floor, and the research holds no booking or occupancy series to show how fast a new venue fills in this market.

  2. The fee is lower than assumed

    No Teton County venue price is in the research. A fee a step below the planning figure takes Year 2 under the floor, and a competitor that cuts prices to fill midweek dates sets the market's ceiling.

  3. Land costs more than the model

    The county's median home value is $1,633,900 [2], and the model's land figure is a round planning price. Land priced above it adds debt on top of a contribution that is already large.

  4. The use is not allowed, or is allowed with limits

    Hours, noise, alcohol service, traffic and wastewater limits can cut the number of dates a venue can sell. The research found none of the local rules, so a lender must see the permit.

  5. The season is short

    County unemployment ran from 1.7% in June 2026 to 2.8% in February 2026 [11][12], which shows how seasonal the local economy is. A venue carries a year of fixed cost against a few months of its best revenue.

  6. The visitor trade softens

    Yellowstone's first nine months of 2026 were 4,360,300 visits, down 1% from the same months of 2025 according to the park service [22], and Grand Teton's July count was below the year before [5]. A softer visitor year would reach wedding guests' choice of Jackson.

  7. The CDC treats the building as special purpose

    That would raise the borrower's contribution to the top tier and push the project further from the program's leverage.

  8. Staff are hard to find

    The county's unemployment rate was the lowest in the state in every month the research reached [11][12][13], and the lack of housing for staff is a risk to the plan for event-day labor.

Lender Conditions

Conditions before commitment.

What follows is the list of open questions the public record left, with several items the research reported as not found grouped together.

  1. A named competitor census

    A census of rival venues in Teton County and the nearby trade area, with every rival linked to its own website or county assessor record, and with seated capacity, rental rates, open dates, caterer and alcohol rules, lodging on site and year opened. The register's County Business Patterns count is a proxy and is not a census [3].

  2. Occupancy, rates and booking evidence

    Booked and open dates by month for the next two seasons, rental fees actually quoted, the share of weddings from out of the area, and the average daily rate and occupancy of the lodging that houses the guests. The research found no hotel occupancy or average daily rate for Jackson.

  3. Site, title and zoning

    The assessor's parcel number, a written zoning and use letter for a public-assembly venue serving alcohol, the permit history, the hours and noise limits, and the land's appraisal, survey and sales record.

  4. Traffic, access and environmental

    Daily traffic from a state count station on the US-26/89/191 corridor at the site, the access permit and turning-lane requirements, the FEMA flood panel, a Phase I environmental report and, for a mountain site, wildlife and wildfire review. The research found no traffic count for the corridor.

  5. Utilities and wastewater

    The names of the electric, gas, water and sewer suppliers to the parcel, the septic or sewer capacity for an event of the hall's size, fire flow, and the connection fees. No provider was confirmed.

  6. Taxes and local rules

    Teton County's assessed valuation, mill levy and commercial assessment ratio, which came back from the research as an unreadable file, the lodging and sales tax treatment of venue rentals and add-ons, and current building permit and short-term rental rules, which the research listed as not found.

  7. Costs and equipment

    Contractor bids with a mountain-county labor and materials premium, a quote for tables, chairs, lighting and sound, and quotes for property, liability, liquor liability and flood insurance.

  8. Liquor licensing and operating model

    The license class and the county or town process for serving alcohol, the rules for outside caterers, the bar commission structure the model assumes, and a written operating plan for event-day staff and housing.

  9. Special purpose treatment

    The CDC's written view on whether the building is a limited or special purpose property, and the equity tier that follows [26].

  10. Program items

    Terms from the CDC and the bank at pricing, owner eligibility papers under the SOP, the jobs test for a debenture of $95,000 per job [26], and the sponsor's plan to document owner occupancy of a new building. The research did not establish rural, HUBZone or Opportunity Zone status for the tract.

  11. Opening reserve

    Cash reserved at closing to bridge Year 1, when operating income falls short of debt service, until the stabilized year.

Jackson Questions

What lenders ask about the Jackson event venue case.

Is there enough demand in Jackson for a wedding venue?

There are enough arrivals: Grand Teton recorded 3,800,648 recreation visits in 2025 and the airport boarded 598,923 passengers in the year to July 2026 [6][5]. What the public record lacks is wedding demand itself, meaning bookings, rates and open dates, so the model treats the wedding count as an assumption and the lender should ask for booking evidence.

Who are the competitors?

The research names none. The Census Bureau's County Business Patterns counts a proxy class of establishments in Teton County, and the metro area count is close to the county's [3]. A named census, with each rival's own website or assessor record, is the first lender condition.

Why does this venue need so much equity beyond the program minimum?

Land is costly in a county with a very high median home value [2], and second-season income is thin against the debt. Because the bank keeps half of cost whatever the sponsor adds, each extra dollar of equity replaces debenture money and leaves the bank loan where it was.

Is a feasibility study mandatory for a venue like this?

Under the regulation SBA may call for professional appraisals, a survey or a feasibility study [29], and the research note on that wording explains why that is not a mandate. A lender decides case by case, and a ground-up venue with a start-up borrower is a project where one may be asked for.

Is the land price in the model a real price?

No. It is a round planning figure labelled as an assumption, because the research holds no Teton County land sale. An appraisal replaces it.
