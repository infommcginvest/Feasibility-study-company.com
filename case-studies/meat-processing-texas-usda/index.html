Meat Processing Plant Feasibility Study, Elgin, Texas: A New Inspected Plant on a USDA B&I Guaranteed Loan

Market analysis by Mark Otava · Published July 13, 2026 · Data as of October 2026

Representative worked example. The site, sponsor and project are hypothetical. The market evidence comes from the public sources listed below, with their dates. This page shows how the market analysis is built and how it sets the loan. It is not a client engagement.

Elgin has the sausage trade, the industrial park and the cattle country around it, but none of the 21 registered processors in its six-county trade area posts a price [16], so a new plant's revenue rests on throughput commitments, not on market data. On a hypothetical $14,376,787 beef and sausage plant, the sponsor's 20.0% equity covers Year 3 debt service only 1.11x. Restructured to 28.4% equity, the guaranteed loan of $10,293,780 meets the lender's floor at 1.25x, with a funded reserve for the ramp-up and every fee and volume input still to be proven.

Market Conclusion

The location and the supply chain are sound; the revenue has to be contracted.

Elgin, on US 290 at the eastern edge of the Austin metro, is the strongest place in the six-county area for a new USDA-inspected plant. The Texas Legislature named it the Sausage Capital of Texas, and its smoked-meat trade goes back to 1882 [34]. Three federally inspected plants and one state-inspected plant already operate in town [16][17][19][22]. The city markets an industrial park zoned for food processing, with utilities listed and a Phase I environmental audit complete [7]. And the population is growing: Elgin had 13,327 residents in July 2025, up 37.4% from 9,700 in April 2020 [1].

The supply side supports a beef plant. Texas held 12.1 million head of cattle on January 1, 2026, the same as a year earlier, while the national herd of 86.2 million was at a 75-year low [32]. Capacity at the big packers is shrinking: Tyson closed its Lexington, Nebraska beef plant on January 20, 2026 and laid off 3,200 workers [64]. Small producers who sell beef direct need inspected processing, and Texas cannot ship state-inspected product across state lines [33].

What the market cannot show is price. The six-county trade area holds 21 registered processors, from four federally inspected plants to small custom-exempt shops [16], and none of them posts a processing price sheet. The research found no kill fee, no cut-and-wrap rate, no yield and no capacity figure for a small Texas plant. So the revenue build in this case is an assumption from top to bottom, labelled as such, and the first lender conditions are a fee survey and signed throughput commitments.

On those assumptions the case is close. A hypothetical 20,000 sq ft plant harvesting up to 30 head of beef a day, with a sausage and smoked-meat room, costs $14,376,787. At the sponsor's 20.0% equity, Year 3 income covers debt service 1.11x, short of the 1.25x floor this case's lender uses. Restructured to 28.4% equity, the $10,293,780 guaranteed loan covers 1.25x in Year 3. The plant loses money in Year 1 and does not cover debt service in Year 2, so a funded reserve is part of the structure, and the break-even sits only 5.7% below the underwritten utilization. That last number is the real finding: a small meat plant's lender is underwriting throughput, and throughput must be contracted before closing.

Demand Evidence

A sausage town in a fast-growing county, with cattle on every side.

For a processing plant, the trade area is a supply and customer catchment, not a retail radius. This case uses Bastrop County and the five counties around it: Travis, Williamson, Lee, Fayette and Caldwell. Live animals come from the ranches in those counties; customers include the Elgin sausage makers, direct-market producers, restaurants and the Austin metro's retail buyers. No drive-time demographic run was available, so the figures below are for Elgin and the counties.

Three kinds of demand matter. The first is the local processing trade. Meyer's says wholesale "remains the largest part of the operation" and that it has shipped nationwide since it gained federal inspection in 2002 [18]. Southside Market makes fresh sausage in Elgin daily for its restaurants and wholesale customers [20] and sells wholesale in 50 lb and 200 lb increments [21]. A federally inspected co-packer can sell capacity to that trade when the established plants are full or when smaller brands need an inspected kitchen. The 39th annual Hogeye Festival on the fourth Saturday of October [35] is a reminder that the town's identity is built on the product.

The second is direct-market beef. Small ranchers who sell by the quarter or the box need a slaughter and fabrication slot, and when capacity is short they wait. In North Carolina, the typical wait for a processing appointment went from about 30 days before COVID to 210 days in 2021 [62]; no Texas wait-time figure was found, and a local slot survey is a lender condition. Federally inspected cattle slaughter in January to May 2026 ran at 91% of the same months in 2025 [59], which says the big plants are processing fewer cattle, not that local demand has fallen.

The third is population. Bastrop County had about 118,900 residents in 2025 [2] and added about 2,970 in a year, a 2.6% gain [3]. Neighboring Caldwell County grew 4.18% and Hays and Williamson more than 3% each, while Texas added 391,243 people between July 2024 and July 2025 [4]. The city's economic development office claims 15,000 new homes under way [7], a marketing figure the research did not verify. Per-capita availability of red meat and poultry was 226 pounds in 2025, including 58.5 pounds of beef [63].

The register reads very differently once activity is separated from count. Most of the 21 only process meat; they buy boxed product or carcasses and cut, grind, cure or cook it. Beef harvest under inspection is rare. Only Weimar Meat Company holds a federal grant that includes meat slaughter, and only Smithville Food Lockers, H. W. Doyle and Willie Joe's harvest livestock under state inspection; the rest of the slaughter capacity is custom-exempt, which can serve an animal's owner but cannot produce meat for sale. Pasture Raised Foods slaughters only its own poultry. For a rancher who wants to sell federally inspected beef in Austin or beyond, the choice in six counties is narrow.

That narrowness is the opportunity, and it cuts both ways. A plant that offers inspected beef harvest and fabrication fills a gap the register shows. But the established Elgin plants are processors with their own supply chains, not obvious harvest customers, and the small state-inspected harvest plants compete on price for local ranchers. The research could not establish any competitor's fees, capacity or waiting list. Three state plants and one federal plant that already harvest beef will respond to a newcomer, and the case cannot say how.

The wider Texas market is adding capacity at both ends. Producer Owned Beef received a $253 million building permit in Amarillo on August 8, 2025 for a plant planned at 3,000 head a day and 1,600 employees at peak [26], part of a $670 million project on 1,108 acres [27]. Standard Meat's fifth plant in Fort Worth received its federal grant on February 12, 2026 as a large establishment [28], reported at 182,000 sq ft [29]. At the small end, East Texas Slaughter and Packing in Laneville received a very small FSIS grant on July 18, 2025 [30]. Texas gained nine federally inspected slaughter plants in 2025, more than any other state [60]. None of these is in the trade area, and no new plant was found in the six counties in 2025 or 2026.

Site, Zoning and Utilities

An industrial park lot zoned for food processing, with slaughter still to be cleared.

The subject is a hypothetical 6.0-acre lot in Elgin Business Park III, a city-owned industrial park just off US 290 [12]. The Elgin EDC lists the park as shovel-ready, inside city limits, divisible and available for build-to-suit, with a completed Phase I environmental audit [7]. LoopNet showed six lots of 2.02 to 17.95 acres priced from $615,938 to $1,896,602 in one 2026 snapshot [5], and four lots of 3.66 to 17.95 acres at $1,280,664 in another [6]; the two do not reconcile. The best price evidence is a sale: on April 21, 2026 the City Council approved the sale of 12 acres for about $1.5 million to a developer of a speculative industrial building [8], about $125,000 an acre, the figure this case uses.

That same sale is a supply risk for the site. The buyer must invest at least $10 million and finish within 18 months of closing, on what will be "the largest industrial building in Elgin," divisible into up to four spaces, with the EDC agreeing to master-lease vacant space for up to a year [8]. The EDC lists the project at 30,000 to 120,000 sq ft on 11 acres [9]. Industrial users are now competing for the remaining lots, and lot availability after the April sale must be confirmed.

Zoning

The park is zoned I General Industrial, one of three industrial-zoned areas in the city, and a 66.22-acre lot in the park was rezoned to I in 2020 [10]. The district text in that 2020 packet permits "food products processing and packing" and the processing or packaging of food products in an enclosed building, provided no noxious odor, fumes or dust leave the property [10]. It does not name livestock slaughter, and the research found only the proposed text, not the final adopted version. The city has been moving parcels in and out of industrial zoning: a February 4, 2025 hearing rezoned two parcels from C-3 to I and a 0.996-acre park lot from I to C-2 [11]. A written use determination or special permit for slaughter is the site's most important lender condition. Chapter 48 of the city code adds site development rules for industrial sites [15], and the EDC advertises a 90-day fast-track permit, a marketing claim [7].

Utilities, flood and environment

The EDC lists water, sewer, electric, natural gas and high-speed telecom as available but names no providers [7]; CenterPoint Energy's gas transportation rate schedule appeared on a City Manager report in February 2025 [11]. A meat plant's wastewater is the critical utility: slaughter and sausage production send strong, fat-laden effluent to the sewer, and the city's capacity and pretreatment requirements were not found. EPA withdrew proposed tighter effluent guidelines for meat and poultry plants in September 2025 [65], which lowers the near-term federal burden but leaves the city's industrial pretreatment rules as the test. The park listing carries a "100-Year Flood Plain" attribute without saying which lots it affects [7]; the FEMA zone for any lot was not found. The Phase I audit is reported complete, but its findings are not in the research.

Incentives, licensing and taxes

The park sits in a Triple Freeport Zone and an Opportunity Zone [7], designated census tract 48021950200 [14]. Neither benefit is quantified in the research. A plant selling across state lines needs an FSIS grant of inspection [40]; the state's Meat Safety Assurance program offers grants of inspection for intrastate sale and asks applicants for the nature and volume of their operations [41]. The 2025 property tax rates are $0.42870 per $100 for Bastrop County [36], $0.619300 for the city, $1.223400 for Elgin ISD and $0.103400 for Austin Community College [37]. The model adds the four; any emergency services district levy was not found. Bastrop County approved a rise from 42 to 45 cents for tax year 2026 [38]. Sales tax is 8.25% [39].

The plant earns fees, not meat margins. It harvests, fabricates and packages beef for ranchers and direct-market producers, and it co-packs sausage and smoked meats for brands that need federally inspected capacity. That model keeps the plant out of the cattle market: it never owns the animal, so it does not carry the price risk that produced large beef losses at the national packers. The trade-off is that revenue depends entirely on how many customers book slots and what they will pay.

Harvest volume. Capacity is 30 head a day over 245 days. Utilization reaches 70.0% in Year 3. The only ramp figure in the research is from a large Missouri plant that started at 800 head a day toward a 2,400 head-a-day capacity [55], which shows ramps are slow even with a committed supply base. Seasonality was not found for Texas; deer season and holiday orders typically load a small plant's calendar unevenly, so the model stays below full capacity.

Harvest fee. The all-in fee for harvest, fabrication and packaging is $1,000 a head in Year 1, rising 2.5% a year. No competitor posts a price, so this is the most important unsourced number in the case; the lender should replace it with a phone survey of the four plants that harvest beef and with signed customer commitments.

Further processing. The sausage and smoked-meat room runs 550,000 pounds in Year 3 at $1.40 a pound. Elgin's established makers sell wholesale at scale [18][21], which shows the demand for the product, not for a co-packer; contracts are what prove it.

On these drivers, revenue is $6,214,447 in Year 3. Packaging, casings, spices and offal disposal are charged as cost of sales, leaving $5,465,955 of gross profit. FSIS's size rules place a plant like this among small establishments, with 10 to 499 employees [61]; a plant with fewer than ten employees or less than $2.5 million of sales would count as very small [61], and this plant exceeds both thresholds from its first year.

A meat plant is labor-intensive, and labor is set by the shift, not by the head. The model staffs harvest, fabrication, processing, sanitation and the office with 38 people in Year 3. Pay is the U.S. average for animal slaughtering and processing: an industry payroll of $28.4 billion across 556,205 employees in the Census Bureau's 2023 County Business Patterns [57], grown 3.0% a year to the operating years, plus a 25.0% load for payroll taxes, benefits and workers' compensation. No Texas or Austin wage series for meat workers was found, and Austin's labor market may be tighter than the national average. Labor is by far the largest line in the table.

Utilities and sewer surcharges run at 7.0% of revenue, because refrigeration, scalding and cleaning water and the surcharge on strong wastewater all scale with output. Repairs are a share of building and equipment cost; insurance, including product recall cover, is a placeholder. Property tax uses the 2025 combined rate on land, building, equipment and site work at cost. The last line is USDA's annual retention fee of 0.55% of the guaranteed balance [44], which the model charges on a loan at the minimum equity before amortization, a conservative simplification.

Net operating income is $1,166,436 in Year 3, a 18.8% margin. That is thin in absolute terms and heavily geared: because most costs are fixed, a small fall in volume takes a large share of the income with it. The large packers show how hard the business can be: Tyson's beef segment lost $1,135 million on a GAAP basis in fiscal 2025, an operating margin of minus 5.2% [70]. A fee-for-service plant avoids that cattle-price exposure but not the operating gearing.

How the B&I program shapes this loan, and what is not yet published.

A B&I loan is made by a commercial lender and guaranteed by USDA Rural Development. Under the FY2026 notice, published March 9, 2026 and effective for the fiscal year that began October 1, 2025, the guarantee is 85% for loans under $5 million and 80% for loans of $5 million to $25 million, with a 3.0% initial fee and a 0.55% annual retention fee [44]. USDA announced the 85% tier on March 27, 2026 for loans obligated in FY2026 [43]. This loan is above $5 million, so the case uses the 80% guarantee.

Those are FY2026 terms, and FY2026 is over. The research found no FY2027 fee notice, no FY2027 guarantee percentage and no enacted FY2027 program level; the FY2027 budget request proposed a B&I program level of zero [53]. A lender working this deal now must confirm what USDA will charge and guarantee when it obligates the loan. That is the first program condition, and it could change the fee, the guarantee share and whether funding is available at all.

The rate is negotiated between lender and borrower, fixed, variable or a mix [49]; the research has no rate, so the model uses 7.75%, an assumption. The term may run to the useful life of the collateral, up to 40 years [49]; the model amortizes over 25 years because process equipment wears out long before the building. The research has no B&I equity or coverage rule, so the 20.0% equity minimum and 1.25x coverage floor are the lender's own assumptions in this case. The program caps a loan at $25 million [44].

The feasibility study is not optional here. A B&I guaranteed loan of more than $1,000,000 to a new business needs a study by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to the Agency [50], covering economic, market, technical, financial and management feasibility [51]. The lender must evaluate that study in writing [52], and the projections must cover at least two years at full capacity or stable operations [50]. A plant with more than 50 new direct jobs on a loan above $1,000,000 would also go to the Department of Labor [50]; this plant stays below that count. Our note on the five USDA feasibility components sets out what each component must prove, and the USDA Rural Development practice explains how we prepare them.

Eligibility starts with the site: the project must be in an area outside any city or town of more than 50,000 people [43]. Elgin's population is far below that, but the USDA eligibility map was not checked for this park and is a condition. A city of under 25,000 earns five priority points [50]. The program is busy in Texas, which took $289.2 million of B&I obligations in FY2025, 13.7% of the national total [53], and USDA removed ten lenders in May 2026 whose portfolios held about $620 million of delinquent loans, about 47% of Rural Development's delinquencies [54]. A lender with a clean B&I record matters. Banks may also receive CRA consideration for B&I loans [45]. The nearest Texas precedent in the research is a $4,950,000 Food Supply Chain guarantee in February 2023 to refinance the purchase of a custom meat plant in Amarillo [46].

SBA is the obvious alternative and works differently. A meat plant is a manufacturer, so its 504 debenture could reach $5.5 million rather than $5 million, and FY2027 fees are waived for manufacturers [71]. SBA does not mandate a study: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [73]. SBA's 90% grocery guarantee, announced in June 2026, does not list meat processing among its eligible industry codes [67], although a trade report described it as open to food processing [48].

Coverage in Years 1 and 2 is -0.65x and 0.37x; the reserve, not income, carries those years.

The test year matters. USDA asks for projections that cover at least two years at full capacity or stable operations [50], and this case tests coverage in Year 3, the first year near stable utilization, with Years 4 and 5 as the stable years. A lender that tested Year 2 would need far more equity or a larger reserve. A lender should also look past the ratio to the reserve: if the ramp runs six months longer than modeled, the reserve runs out before the income arrives.

On the restructured loan, the plant covers its debt service at 66.0% harvest utilization in Year 3, a cushion of only 5.7% below the underwritten level, and meets the floor at 70.0%. That is what operating gearing looks like: most of the cost base is people and buildings, so a few points of lost volume take a large share of income.

The lesson for the loan is that contracted volume is worth more than equity. A signed commitment from a group of ranchers or a co-packing customer for a fixed number of head or pounds a week moves the break-even question from forecast to contract. Our note on the break-even trap shows why a thin cushion should weigh more in a credit decision than a coverage ratio that clears the floor.

Risk Factors

What could break this plant's coverage.

  • Unproven fees and volume. Every revenue input is an assumption. No competitor price or capacity figure exists in the research.
  • Zoning for slaughter. The industrial district lists food processing, not livestock slaughter, and the research has only the 2020 proposed text.
  • Wastewater. City capacity and pretreatment requirements are unknown, and a plant that cannot discharge cannot harvest.
  • Program terms. FY2027 B&I fees, guarantee percentage and funding are not published, and the FY2027 budget request proposed no B&I lending [53].
  • Labor. The plant needs dozens of trained workers in a fast-growing metro with a 73.1% labor force participation rate in Elgin [1]; the research has no local wage, staffing or turnover data.
  • Cattle supply and the big packers. The national herd is at a 75-year low [32], and new large plants in Amarillo and Fort Worth will compete for Texas cattle and for buyers of processed product [26][28].
  • Ramp-up. Small plants fail in their first years. USDA's Inspector General found $208,285,667 of questioned costs across 35 Phase I MPPEP grants and recorded one grantee that closed its business [66].
  • Flood and lot availability. The park listing carries a flood-plain attribute, and the April 2026 land sale took 12 acres out of the inventory [7][8].

Two other USDA cases on this site test the same program from different angles. The Texas RV park case shows a B&I loan whose revenue rests on published nightly rates and occupancy data, the evidence this plant lacks, and the Hankamer travel center case shows a B&I project on an interstate with measured traffic. Our USDA Rural Development deadline calendar covers the fiscal-year timing that matters here. The Texas market page and Austin metro page place Elgin in its region.

Elgin Meat Plant Questions

Questions lenders ask about this B&I meat plant case.

Is a feasibility study required for this B&I loan?

Yes. Under 7 CFR 5001.306(a)(3)(i), a B&I guaranteed loan of more than $1,000,000 to a new business needs a feasibility study by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to USDA [50]. This plant is a new business borrowing well over that amount. The rule is USDA's; SBA programs work differently, and the lender must also evaluate the study in writing [52].

Which B&I guarantee and fees apply to a loan closed now?

The last published terms are FY2026's: an 80% guarantee for loans of $5 million or more, a 3.0% initial fee and a 0.55% annual retention fee [44]. FY2026 ended on September 30, 2026. The FY2027 guarantee percentage, fees and program level were not found, and the FY2027 budget request proposed no new B&I lending [53]. The lender must confirm the terms in force when USDA obligates the guarantee.

Why does the plant need federal inspection rather than state inspection?

Texas is not a Cooperative Interstate Shipment state, so state-inspected product can be sold only within Texas [33]. A plant that wants to sell sausage across state lines, as the Elgin makers do, needs an FSIS grant of inspection [40]. Federal inspection also widens the customer base for co-packing work.

Can the plant slaughter cattle at an Elgin Business Park lot?

Not confirmed. The park's I General Industrial district lists food products processing and packing as a permitted use in the 2020 text the research found, with a rule against noxious odor beyond the property line, but it does not name livestock slaughter [10]. A written use determination or special permit from the City of Elgin is a lender condition before the loan is approved.

Could the sponsor use an MPPEP grant to reduce the loan?

Not on current terms. Phase 4 of USDA's Meat and Poultry Processing Expansion Program closed to applications on August 7, 2026 [47] and required at least one year in cattle processing [69], which a new plant does not have. The case relies on no grant.

What would most quickly break this loan?

Throughput. The plant covers debt service in Year 3 only if harvest utilization stays within a few points of the underwritten level, because labor and fixed costs do not fall when volume does. Signed processing commitments from ranchers and co-packing customers are the evidence that closes that gap.
