Why we treat feasibility as a market question

Federal rules define a feasibility study by its parts. Under 7 CFR 5001.3, a feasibility study is a report by an independent qualified consultant "evaluating the economic, market, technical, financial, and management feasibility of the proposed project or operation" [1]. In practice the market component decides the other four. Revenue, ramp-up, staffing and debt coverage all inherit the demand, rate and capture assumptions. If those assumptions are asserted rather than measured, the financial section is only arithmetic on a guess.

So we start where weaker studies end: we delineate the trade area, count the households, visitors and businesses in it, inventory every competitor and every project in the pipeline, survey what the market actually charges, and derive capture and absorption from that evidence. Then we test how much debt the realistic case can carry.

How we measure a market

  1. Trade area delineation. Drive-time and gravity models, checked against commuting flows, visitor origin data and state traffic counts where the use depends on them (fuel, car wash, quick-service restaurants, travel centers).
  2. Demand quantification. Population, households, income, age and employment from Census and BLS series; visitor and room-night demand for hospitality; boat, RV and vehicle registrations for storage and marine uses. Margins of error are shown, not hidden.
  3. Competitive supply inventory. Every operating competitor, located and described: size, product, season, amenities and published rates.
  4. Pipeline tracking. Building permits, planning board and zoning agendas, and announced projects. Supply is reported as existing plus pipeline, never existing alone. In our Townsend, Tennessee case, a 75-cabin resort that broke ground this autumn is the single largest variable in the loan.
  5. Rate and rent surveys. Published rates and documented quotes, each dated. Where data vendors disagree, we show both and underwrite between them.
  6. Gap, penetration and capture. Net demand against competitive supply, following the Appraisal Institute's sequence of market delineation, demand, supply, equilibrium and subject capture, and tested against published thresholds where they exist.
  7. Absorption and sensitivity. Ramp-up from comparable openings, seasonality by month, and downside cases a credit committee can test.

What the market-first approach changes

The worked cases show the method reaching different answers in different markets:

  • In Ossipee, New Hampshire, seasonal-site demand supports a 32-pad expansion at mid-market rates, but not an acquisition priced at listing-level expectations.
  • In Oak Hill, West Virginia, a record national park sits beside a rental market whose supply grew 47.8% in a year. Local rates, not national glamping averages, set the loan.
  • In Cape Coral, Florida, the boats are there, but published rack rates would need to roughly double to pay for a new building. The case says so.

A study that can say no is the only kind whose yes is worth anything to a lender.

The standards we work inside

  • USDA Rural Development. 7 CFR Part 5001 and the feasibility study outline in Appendix A to Subpart D, with its economic, market, technical, financial and management components. An independent study is required for B&I guaranteed loans above $1,000,000 to a new business [1].
  • SBA. 13 CFR 120.160(b) allows SBA to require a feasibility study, and lenders request one for specialized property and start-ups. SOP 50 10 8.1 took effect on October 1, 2026 [2].
  • HUD. The MAP Guide (4430.G) sets market study timing: an effective date within 120 days before pre-application and within 180 days before firm commitment [3].
  • Rental housing. The National Council of Housing Market Analysts' Model Content Standards, updated September 2025, adopted in whole or part by most state housing credit agencies [4].
  • Market analysis practice. The Appraisal Institute's six-step market analysis, and James Graaskamp's test of feasibility: a reasonable likelihood of satisfying explicit objectives when a course of action is tested against specific constraints and limited resources [5].

Questions about how we work

Is a market study the same as a feasibility study?

No. A market study measures demand, supply, rate and capture. A feasibility study takes those results and tests whether the specific project, at its specific cost and financing, can meet its objectives and service its debt. Ours begin with the market study and carry it through the financials.

What capture rate is too high?

It depends on the asset and the program. Some state housing agencies publish limits, such as an overall capture rate of 10% or less for tax-credit housing. Where no published threshold exists, we compare the subject's required capture with what comparable openings achieved.

How current is your data?

Each figure shows its date. Federal series refresh on their own schedules, from quarterly (SBA loan files, QCEW) to annual (ACS). For HUD market studies, the effective date must fall within 120 days of pre-application.

How do you handle rural markets with thin data?

We say where the data are thin, use the nearest reliable geography, and widen the downside case. Rural markets are where USDA programs concentrate, so thin data is a normal condition, not a reason to guess.

Do you provide appraisals?

An opinion of value is a separate assignment, prepared by a state-certified appraiser under USPAP. Our studies identify what the appraisal must address, such as going-concern value and equipment discounts.

Can I cite your published figures?

Yes, with attribution and a link. The underlying federal data are public domain; the analysis is ours.
