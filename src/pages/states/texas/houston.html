No U.S. metro gained more residents than Houston in the year to July 2025: 126,720, by the Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 count [^1]. The research did not retrieve the 2025 MSA total, so the base on this page is the prior vintage: 7,796,182 people on July 1, 2024, which is 9.0% above the 2020 base of 7,150,233 [^2]. A secondary report puts the 2025 total slightly above 7.9 million [^4]; a study should swap that for the Census table before it sizes a trade area. Inside the city limits the year's gain was 11,515, for 2,397,315 residents [^3]. Most of the metro's growth therefore landed in unincorporated county land and suburban cities, where a different government approves the site.

Payroll data are firmer ground. BLS counts 44,700 more nonfarm jobs in August 2026 than in August 2025, 3,495.3 thousand against 3,450.6 thousand, a rise of 1.3% [^5]. The reading is preliminary and unadjusted for season, and it is the one 12-month change among the large Texas metros that the agency flags as statistically significant. That makes it better evidence than a headline population count. It still describes the whole metro, so the study has to connect the subject to the job centers its customers or tenants can actually reach.

Permits point north. HBWeekly's tracking universe counted 35,520 residential permits for the Houston area in 2025, down 10%, with Harris County at about 16,000 and Montgomery County at about 10,720 [^9]. Those are not Census Building Permits Survey counts, and the Census total for 2025 is not in the research, so the two should not be combined into a growth rate. What the split does show is that Montgomery County, home to The Woodlands, Conroe and Magnolia, takes a large share of new rooftops. Retail, childcare and storage projects that live on new households need permit and plat counts drawn from their own trade area, as our note on trade areas explains.

For a lender the working question is share. A convenience store, a childcare center or a storage facility in a fast-growing Montgomery County subdivision competes for the same new households as every operator that read the same permit counts. The study should count rooftops and approved plats inside the subject's drive time, list the competitors already open or permitted there, and state what share of the new households the subject needs to cover its debt. When that share runs above the subject's share of existing supply, the study has to say why the subject wins it.

Houston's image is oil and gas, but its largest payrolls belong to hospitals, a retailer's logistics network and local government. On Harris County's 2025 list, Memorial Hermann ranks first with 31,425 employees, Amazon third with 24,000, the City of Houston fourth with 22,170 and Houston Methodist fifth with 22,015 [^6]. UT Health adds 16,150, putting three Texas Medical Center systems among the county's ten largest employers [^6]. The second name on that list is illegible in the copy the research extracted, so we leave it out rather than guess. The University of Houston reported 6,986 employees on the 2024 list [^7].

For a lender, the hospital cluster is the steadiest demand source in the metro. It fills medical office suites, sends patients' families to hotels near the Medical Center and the suburban campuses, and supports workforce rentals within commuting range. The research records Memorial Hermann expansion in Cypress, Pearland and Lake Houston, but without budgets or opening dates [^8]. A study that leans on one of those campuses must document the project itself: permit, budget, opening date, beds and staff.

Energy and the port are the evidence gap. The research holds no current headcount for Port Houston, the Ship Channel industries or the energy sector, and no Houston project over $50 million with a confirmed 2025 or 2026 budget. Where a subject's demand depends on that economy (an east-side hotel that books plant crews, a truck service near the Ship Channel, a warehouse leased to an oilfield supplier), the study must source the segment directly, from BLS industry employment for the metro and Port Houston's own statistics, and then run the pro forma with that segment cut back. We do not estimate a number we cannot cite.

Apartments: fewer cranes, rents still soft

The Greater Houston Partnership reports 88.1% occupancy in Q2 2026, which is 11.9% vacancy, with average asking rent of $1,368 against $1,380 a year earlier, 13,066 units under construction and 4,719 delivered in the quarter [^10]. Cushman & Wakefield's stabilized series reads 11.1% vacancy, $1,312 effective rent and 11,756 units under construction, 36.4% fewer than a year earlier [^11]. Northmarq publishes 7.1% for a different property universe [^12]. Houston delivered 25,000 apartments in 2024 [^13], and RealPage forecasts slight rent declines over the four quarters from Q2 2026 [^14]. Build-to-rent adds about 3,000 units under construction metro-wide, 1,200 of them in Spring and Tomball [^15].

The read for a lender: a project that delivers after today's pipeline clears should face fewer competing lease-ups than 2025 projects did, but there is no pricing power yet. That is a different problem from Dallas-Fort Worth, where the open question is how much is still being built rather than where rents go. In Houston the apartment work begins with effective rent in the subject's own submarket (our note on effective rent versus asking rent explains the gap) and with a plain statement of which of the three vacancy universes the study relies on. Our multifamily feasibility page sets out the absorption test.

Hotels: 2025 was the trough

Full-year 2025 occupancy fell 8.6% to 58.9%, the steepest decline among the top 25 U.S. markets [^16]. December 2025 ran at 50% occupancy, a $108 average daily rate and $54 RevPAR [^17]. By March 2026 RevPAR had climbed to $98.93, up 11.9%, at 69.9% occupancy [^18]. One strong month after a weak year is not a stabilized year. A hotel study should build from trailing twelve-month data for the subject's competitive set (see reading the STR report) and test break-even occupancy against the 2025 numbers (see the break-even occupancy trap). Hotel pipeline and opening data were not found. Our hotel feasibility page covers the method.

Industrial, retail, medical, senior housing and storage

CBRE names Houston as one of three U.S. leaders for industrial absorption over January to June 2026 [^20], and Cushman & Wakefield puts the industrial pipeline at 23.0 million square feet [^19]; industrial vacancy itself was not found. Retail is tighter but softening: Cushman & Wakefield reports 5.8% vacancy with 161 projects and 3.8 million square feet under construction [^21], while Matthews reports 5.5% vacancy and net absorption of minus 478,000 square feet in the same quarter [^22]. Medical office vacancy was 11.7% in H1 2026 [^23]. The metro had 275 urgent care centers [^24], and HCA's CareNow took over 8 more in August 2026 [^25], which matters to any independent clinic study. Senior housing sold for $157,518 per unit over 1Q to 3Q 2025 [^26], and occupancy of 84.7% in 1Q 2025 was among the lowest of the major markets [^27], so an assisted living study must show demand the existing stock is not already absorbing. Data center vacancy was 19.7% in H1 2025, with 15 MW of new capacity scheduled [^28].

RV and boat storage is the one storage class with metro data: 178 properties on 1,252.52 acres in Yardi Matrix's count, 13 of them new in three years, advertising $5.60 per square foot [^29]. A Katy and Richmond portfolio of 705 units was 94% occupied when it sold in early 2026 [^30], and a single Conroe facility registered 319,181 square feet of storage buildings with an estimated cost of $1,470,000 [^31]. A new RV and boat storage project near Lake Conroe or the west side must count those competitors by name. Self-storage per capita, office and convenience-store counts were not found; a study needs its own competitor census, as on our self-storage page.

The city and its ETJ

The City of Houston has no zoning. Its Planning Commission must approve plats that meet Chapter 42 and cannot disapprove one because of objections to the use [^32]. In the extraterritorial jurisdiction the city reviews plats only; development there is outside the city's permitting and building inspection [^33], and Harris County issues site, building, driveway and fire-code permits. Use is therefore rarely the approval risk. Private deed restrictions are, which is why a feasibility study consultant in Houston starts with the title commitment and confirms that its restrictions allow the use.

What remains is engineering that decides how much of a parcel can be built. Chapter 42 sets a 25-foot building line along major thoroughfares [^34]. A preliminary plat can take up to 30 business days per submittal [^35]. Harris County takes its detention rate from a design manual the research could not date, so the study should get the current rate from the county engineer. The county fire code, effective January 1, 2025, was amended again from October 1, 2025 [^37], and Montgomery County moved to the 2024 International Fire Code on May 14, 2025 [^36]. On a small frontage pad, those rules set the footprint and the slab elevation, so the site budget should come from a civil engineer's layout, not a per-acre allowance.

The suburban counties

Waller County on the I-10 corridor has no zoning and controls projects through its development regulations: a policy of zero net increase in stormwater runoff with a 0.55 acre-feet per acre detention minimum, a design rainfall of 16.00 inches for the Katy area, and buildings in the 100-year floodplain raised at least 2 feet above the 500-year elevation [^38]. Montgomery County's drainage manual, adopted August 26, 2025, also requires zero increase in peak flow [^39], and the county asks for buildings within 100 feet of a flood zone to sit at least 2 feet above base flood elevation [^40]. Conroe has no zoning ordinance [^41]. In Magnolia, the city paused building permits in December 2022 over water-well capacity [^47]; no 2025 or 2026 moratorium was found, but the episode is why a study needs a written capacity letter from the city or municipal utility district that serves the site.

Parking, fuel stations, hotels and short-term rentals

Unlike Austin, Houston still sets parking minimums. Sec. 26-492, in its 2014 printing of the 2013 ordinance, requires 4.0 spaces per 1,000 square feet of general retail, 10.0 per 1,000 for a sit-down restaurant, 1.0 per room for a hotel up to 250 rooms and 1.333 per one-bedroom apartment [^42]. Later amendments were not verified, so the table must be confirmed with the city before a site plan is fixed. A 2026 council request sought to stop new gas stations near homes for 90 days [^43]; a fuel study should check whether anything was adopted. The Texas Comptroller uses Houston as its example of a 17% combined hotel tax [^45], a rate the Texas Municipal League's 2018 guide places among the highest of any major U.S. metro [^44]. Houston's short-term rental ordinance, No. 2025-322, took effect January 1, 2026 [^46]; its terms were not captured, so an STR-dependent project must read it directly.

Taxes, fees and capacity

The only Houston property tax figure in the research is a 2.337% effective rate on a $1 million commercial property, from 2010-era data [^48], which is too old to underwrite. The study must pull current rates for every taxing unit on the parcel, including any municipal utility district, because MUD taxes can add a line the city rate does not show. No Houston-area city's impact fees or utility capacity turned up in the research; the fee estimate and will-serve letter that replace them are listed with the other written conditions on our Texas page.

Houston's suburban growth runs out along its radial freeways and the outer ring that joins them: I-10 west through Katy toward Brookshire, US 290 northwest through Cypress, I-45 north to The Woodlands and Conroe, and SH 99, the Grand Parkway, linking those exurbs on a wide loop. These are the corridors where travel centers, fuel and convenience stores, express car washes, storage and limited-service hotels compete for the same frontage, and where most Houston site work happens.

The research ties no traffic count to any Houston corridor, so the count method on our Texas page applies in full. Two local features change how a count should be read. Much of the Grand Parkway is tolled, so a mainlane station there includes drivers who cannot reach a frontage corner without leaving the toll road. And because most of the metro's growth sits in unincorporated county land, the nearest station may be miles from the subject on a different road; the study should say how far, and why that count still describes the site.

Access approval comes before traffic. On a state frontage road, TxDOT's Houston District driveway application asks the applicant to show every driveway within 501 feet on either side and every signal within 1,000 feet [^52]. That spacing review can cut a frontage parcel to one driveway, so a study should model the site plan the district is likely to approve, not the one in the broker's flyer. See our truck stop and travel center and gas station and c-store pages for how access drives volume.

GoSBA Loans, a ranking site that works from SBA data and is not an SBA publication, counts $388.1 million of 7(a) lending to 613 businesses in the city of Houston in calendar 2025 [^49]. By dollars its leaders are Texas Capital Bank, with $20.0 million on 12 loans, then Frost Bank and Comerica at $16.0 million each, on 13 and 21 loans; Zions made more loans than anyone, 59. That split is worth a sponsor's attention: the dollar leaders write fewer, larger loans and the count leader many small ones, so the right bank depends on the size of the request and the bank's appetite for the asset class.

The SBA Houston District lent $983 million in fiscal 2025, a total that probably covers only three quarters, and ranked second among the six Texas districts [^50]. Its 32 counties stretch from Harris, Fort Bend and Montgomery to Galveston, Brazoria, Jefferson and Brazos [^51], so it reaches Beaumont and Bryan-College Station. No official fiscal 2025 lender ranking for the district turned up, and no certified development company is named as active in Houston; a 504 sponsor should ask the CDC for its own production record. The SBA 7(a) and 504 practice page covers what an SBA lender asks a study to prove. Apartment and hotel loans above SBA size go to banks, debt funds and agencies (see conventional and institutional lending), whose own vacancy and occupancy assumptions will be tested against the series on this page.

USDA's Business and Industry guarantee is out of reach in Houston, Pasadena, Sugar Land and the other large suburbs. It comes into play at the metro's rural edge, at sites like the Chambers County exit in the case below, and the parcel test that decides it is set out on our Texas page.

Houston's worked case sits east of the metro, at an I-10 exit in Hankamer, Chambers County, where the market is the highway rather than the town. The Hankamer travel center case sizes a fuel, food and truck-parking program from interstate through-traffic with no published station count at the exit, shows an unzoned county approving the use by permit rather than at a hearing, and carries a USDA Business and Industry guarantee through equity and study scope.
