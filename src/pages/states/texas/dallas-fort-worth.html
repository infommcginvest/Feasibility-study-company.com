Apartments: strong absorption, and still more deliveries

Dallas-Fort Worth absorbs apartments at a pace few metros match, and it still has the most under construction. Cushman & Wakefield counted about 10,800 units absorbed in Q2 2026 alone, with stabilized vacancy down 30 basis points in the quarter to 9.6% and about 30,200 units under construction, down 21.3% from a year earlier [^15]. Grapevine was the tightest submarket at 5.5% [^15]. Apartments.com's all-property projection for the quarter was 12.6% [^16], because it counts buildings still leasing up. Dallas delivered 35,000 apartments in 2024 [^17], more than 4,000 market-rate units in Q2 2026 [^19], and RealPage expects nearly 19,000 more over the next four quarters, with Fort Worth rent growth forecast below 1% [^18]. Build-to-rent adds about 3,700 units under construction in Dallas [^21].

What a lender should take from this: absorption is real, but a new building's lease-up competes with every other building opening in its submarket over the same months. The study for a Dallas-Fort Worth apartment loan has to name those buildings, price the concessions they are offering, and set the subject's pace from comparable recent lease-ups (our note on absorption forensics shows how to read them). Building into that pipeline is not cheap: a Dallas apartment project carried $190 to $280 a square foot in hard cost in Q2 2026 [^22], and Dallas construction prices rose 3.52% in the year to Q1 2026 [^23], so a late start pays more to compete for the same renters. Our multifamily feasibility page covers the method.

Industrial: balanced, with a large pipeline

Industrial vacancy sat between 8.1% and 9.3% in Q2 2026 depending on the broker: 8.1% at Cushman & Wakefield [^9], 8.8% at Kidder Mathews [^11] and 9.3% at JLL, which also counted 31.2 million square feet under development [^10]. That range is balanced by Texas standards, and it contrasts sharply with Austin, where industrial vacancy runs at roughly twice its long-term norm. The risk here is not vacancy today but the speculative space still to deliver, so a study for an owner-user or small-bay project should identify the competing buildings within its submarket.

Hotels: the biggest pipeline in the country

Dallas carried the largest hotel construction pipeline of any U.S. market in Q2 2026, at 183 projects in Lodging Econometrics data [^24]. RevPAR slipped 1.5% in Dallas and 0.8% in Fort Worth and Arlington over January to September 2025 [^25]. Short-term rentals add a second layer of supply: Dallas listings grew 34.4% in the year to March 2025 [^26]. A limited-service hotel study here has to list every pipeline project within the competitive set, test break-even occupancy against the new rooms (see the break-even occupancy trap), and treat 2026 World Cup demand as a one-time event, not a stabilized year. See our hotel feasibility page.

Medical, senior housing, storage and retail

Medical office vacancy was 11.4% in Q1 2026, after a peak near 12.0% in late 2025 [^28], and Dallas had 429,000 square feet of medical outpatient space under construction [^29]. The metro had 316 urgent care centers, third among U.S. metros [^30]. Senior housing penetration in Dallas was 13.3% in 2023 [^31], and transactions averaged $187,375 per unit in the first three quarters of 2025 [^32]; a new assisted living study must show unmet demand after that existing penetration (see assisted living and memory care). RV and boat storage is crowded: Yardi Matrix counts 130 properties on 1,488.91 acres, the largest inventory by acreage in its national sample, with supply up 28.3% over 36 months, 20 new properties in three years, rent growth of 1.9% and advertised rent of $5.69 per square foot [^33]. Retail recorded one of the largest quarterly vacancy increases in the country in Q2 2026 [^34]; Class A neighborhood centers traded at cap rates of 5.5% to 6.25% in H2 2025 [^35], and shopping center hard costs ran $210 to $345 per square foot [^22]. Self-storage supply per capita was not found and must come from a site-level census (see self-storage).

With 8,477,157 residents on July 1, 2025, the Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington MSA is the largest in Texas; against its 2020 base of 7,638,294, that is 11.0% growth in five years [^1]. Its gain of 123,557 in the latest year was the second-largest numeric increase of any U.S. metro, just behind Houston [^1]. Dallas reached 1,329,491 residents and Fort Worth 1,028,117, after a gain of 19,512 that made it the tenth-largest city in the country [^2]. Unlike Houston, where the central city took only a small share of the metro's gain, Dallas-Fort Worth's growth is spread across dozens of incorporated suburbs, each with its own zoning code, fee schedule and council.

Payrolls lag the headcount. The August 2026 preliminary count of 4,357.0 thousand nonfarm jobs sits 43,100 above the 4,313.9 thousand of August 2025, growth of 1.0% before seasonal adjustment [^3]. That is the ceiling a lease-up schedule should respect: a study that models demand growing faster than the job base should say what fills the gap.

Housing permits show where the rooftops are going. The Census series for the MSA ran at a seasonally adjusted rate of 5,104 units in December 2025 [^4]; the full-year 2025 Census total is not in the research. HBWeekly's narrower tracking universe counted about 25,260 permits for 2025, down 5%, led by Collin County at 8,634, Tarrant County at 6,579 (up 65%) and Ellis County at 2,330 [^5]. For calendar 2024, CultureMap, citing Construction Coverage and Census data, ranked the metro first in the nation with 71,788 units [^6]. These are three different measures and should not be chained into a growth rate. Read together, they say that Collin County's northern suburbs still lead on volume, that Tarrant County's count jumped, and that Ellis County to the south has become a real growth market, each with its own council, code and approval calendar.

Dallas-Fort Worth has no single industry that can sink it, and that is the first thing a lender should credit. The Dallas Regional Chamber places American Airlines Group, Texas Health Resources and Walmart in its band of employers with 10,000 or more workers, but it publishes bands rather than ranked headcounts [^7]. Tarrant County's top-ten list, built from 2023 data of the North Central Texas Council of Governments, names DFW Airport at 14,000 employees, GM's Arlington Assembly plant at 10,512, Naval Air Station Joint Reserve Base Fort Worth at 10,500, BNSF at 4,900 and UT Arlington at 4,383 [^8]. Those counts are three years old, so a study that relies on one of them should ask the employer for a current figure.

The metro is often sold to lenders as a corporate relocation story, and the population and payroll counts above are consistent with that. The research, however, holds no count of relocations, no list of new headquarters and no office absorption figure for 2025 or 2026. A study that leans on a specific move, such as a campus expected to fill a hotel's weekday rooms or to anchor a retail pad, has to document it: the company's announcement, the building permit, any incentive agreement with its job targets and clawbacks, and the move-in date. The office side of the story is also mixed. Dallas had 3,966 apartment units in its office-to-apartment conversion pipeline at the start of 2026 [^20], which tells a lender that some older office stock is being retired rather than leased (see what office repurposing means for a study).

Freight is the second pillar, and it is better documented. CBRE reports that Dallas-Fort Worth led U.S. industrial absorption in the first half of 2026 together with Houston and Phoenix [^12]. Cushman & Wakefield puts the Alliance submarket in north Fort Worth first, with 3.9 million square feet absorbed year to date in Q2 2026, ahead of the East Dallas suburbs at 2.4 million, South Dallas at 2.0 million and South Fort Worth at 1.8 million [^9]. On October 14, 2026 the U.S. Department of Transportation awarded the City of Fort Worth a $5 million INFRA grant for about 100 new truck parking spaces at a Pilot travel center on I-35 [^13]. For a lender, logistics growth pays twice: warehouse jobs, and the truck traffic that travel centers, fuel, truck parking and quick-service restaurants live on.

Health care rounds out the base. UT Southwestern's Texas Behavioral Health Center in Dallas adds 292 beds in 505,000 square feet, with phased opening from summer 2026 [^14]. Near the Medical District, a study can treat that campus as a documented anchor; rumored projects stay unconfirmed until they carry a permit.

Dallas-Fort Worth is a patchwork of incorporated suburbs, and the rule that decides a project is usually one suburb's zoning code, fee schedule and council vote rather than anything set by the state.

Parking reform in the two central cities

On May 14, 2025, Dallas removed parking minimums downtown, within half a mile of light rail and streetcar stations, on historically designated property, for offices and most retail, and for industrial uses unless they sit next to single-family zoning [^36]. Apartment parking moved from per-bedroom ratios to tiers: none under 20 units, 0.5 spaces per unit for mid-size projects and 1 per unit above 200 units [^36]. Fort Worth now applies minimums only to uses on residential property or within 250 feet of one- or two-family zoning, and caps parking at 125% of the minimum [^37]. San Antonio has gone the other way and still writes a maximum beside the minimum for most uses. A lower legal minimum does not lower the parking a tenant, franchisor or lender expects, so the site plan should still show enough spaces for the business plan.

Discretionary approvals in the suburbs

Car washes show how much a suburban council can decide. In Mansfield a car wash needs a Specific Use Permit; the City Council denied one for a 2.6-acre tunnel site on April 13, 2026 [^38], and the Planning and Zoning Commission had recommended denial 5-0 on another site in November 2024 [^39]. Of the four Mansfield car wash cases the research reviewed from 2024 to 2026, none reached adoption [^38,39]. The application fee is $2,500 plus $100 per acre, capped at $7,500 [^40]. Fort Worth requires drying and vacuum equipment to sit at least 25 feet from residential districts and a conditional use permit within 200 feet [^41]. A study for an express car wash, fuel station or auto use in these suburbs should report the approval path and the hearing record, and the lender should not fund on a site that has not cleared it.

County rules at the edge

Outside city limits, counties regulate by subdivision and infrastructure standards. Parker County requires RV parks to sit on at least 5.0 acres, caps density at 60 spaces per 5 acres and requires at least 12 feet between units [^42], which sets the site count an RV park study can model. In Hood County, eight data-center proposals covering more than 7,600 acres had been filed by June 2026, raising local fights over water and power [^43]. A data-center project there also faces the statewide pause on new large data-center interconnections that began in August 2026, covered on our Texas page.

Hotel tax and fees

The Texas Comptroller lists Dallas County's venue tax at 3%, above the usual 2% venue limit [^44]. The research found no current, primary-source combined hotel tax rate for Dallas or Fort Worth; the only figure it located came from a 2012 council exhibit prepared by another city, which we do not use. A hotel study must confirm the state, city, county and venue rates that apply to the parcel. Growth in Dallas-Fort Worth follows its radial highways and tollways: US 75 and the Dallas North Tollway north into Collin County, I-35W and SH 114 toward Alliance, I-20 east into Kaufman County and west into Parker and Hood counties, and I-45 and US 287 south into Ellis County. The industrial absorption leaders named above are the best available guide to where freight concentrates.

The research ties no traffic count to any of these corridors. What it does turn up is the kind of evidence a lender should discount: broker flyers for I-20 sites near Terrell and Hudson Oaks that quote counts with no station attached, or that cite TxDOT without naming one. The method on our Texas page replaces them. On I-20, I-35W and I-45 the truck share decides a travel center's diesel and parking revenue, so the classification count matters as much as the total.

A travel center or truck parking project on those corridors should also count competing spaces within a defined drive time, including the new grant-funded spaces in Fort Worth, before it assumes overnight demand. Our truck stop and travel center page sets out that census.

Fiscal 2025 lending through the SBA's Dallas/Fort Worth District came to $1.5 billion, likely through the third quarter, the most of any of the six Texas districts [^46]. Run from an office in Euless, it spans 72 counties, among them Dallas, Tarrant, Collin and Denton but also Bell, McLennan, Smith and Wichita [^47], so its totals reach well past the metro to Waco, Tyler and Wichita Falls. The research did not find the district's official fiscal 2025 ranking of lenders. The latest district release it found is from fiscal 2017, when Wells Fargo led 7(a) lending with $102 million and Greater Texas Capital led certified development company lending with $55 million [^45]. That is nine years old; it shows who has historically been active, not who is lending today. Among CDCs, the SBA's current list includes Alliance Lending Corp, based in Fort Worth [^48].

For a 504 project the CDC's appetite for the asset class matters as much as its size, and on a larger owner-user or industrial building the program's size limits can bind: the debenture tops out at $5 million, or $5.5 million for a small manufacturer, under SOP 50 10 8.1. What an SBA lender looks for in the study is set out on our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice page. The governing rule is 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study, so whether a study is needed is a credit decision (see the SBA feasibility myth). Larger apartment, hotel and industrial projects go to banks, debt funds and agencies on conventional terms, where cap rates such as the 5.5% to 6.25% range for Class A neighborhood centers [^35] set the exit test (see conventional and institutional lending).

A USDA Business and Industry guarantee needs a rural site, and nothing inside Dallas, Fort Worth or the large suburbs qualifies. The county seats and unincorporated land of Kaufman, Parker, Hood, Ellis and Johnson counties are where a site can pass the rural test, which, with the Opportunity Zone and HUBZone lookups, is run parcel by parcel as our Texas page describes.

Four of our worked cases sit in the Dallas-Fort Worth region, three of them on the I-20 corridor where the metro meets its exurbs. In Terrell, Kaufman County, the truck stop case sizes a diesel and truck-parking program at an I-20 exit east of Dallas under SBA 504, with only broker claims for traffic near the site. In Granbury, Hood County, the RV and boat storage case sets lake-driven storage demand against the crowded regional supply described above. In Weatherford, Parker County, the franchised dealership case underwrites a dealership's real estate and franchise relationship on the western growth edge. The multifamily case on the outer ring of a major Texas metro carries absorption, effective rent and the agency takeout coverage test, the three numbers that decide an apartment loan in a pipeline this deep.
