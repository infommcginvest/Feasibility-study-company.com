Kansas markets itself on commitments. The Department of Commerce claims more than $30 billion of committed investment and almost 78,000 jobs attached to state-attracted projects since 2019 [^4]. A feasibility study has to turn announcements of that kind into people on a payroll, and the De Soto battery plant in the Kansas City section shows how long the conversion can take: the credit officer's first question about any Kansas demand case is how much of it is still a promise.

Under the announcements, the household base barely moves. From an estimates base of 2,937,986 residents in April 2020 [^1], the state added 11,968 people, or 0.4%, between July 2024 and July 2025 [^2].

That leaves the demand to be found at a named source: a plant still hiring toward its target, a regional hospital, or a packing town short of housing for the workers it already employs. The eight market sections, from Wichita to Garden City, each name that source and what the record holds about it.

Weather is the cost no announcement offsets. Severe storms dominate the state's federal disaster declarations [^24], which puts hail and wind in the operating budget and the roof reserve rather than in a paragraph on remote risks. A site in a county on the Missouri, Nebraska or Oklahoma line adds one more adjustment: its customers and competitors cross the border, so its trade area should too.

Seasonally adjusted nonfarm employment in Kansas was 1,464.9k in July 2025 [^3], and the gain to the preliminary July 2026 count was 5.6k jobs [^3]. On the unadjusted series the state counted 1,455.3k jobs in July 2026, up 8.9k or 0.6% on the year [^3]. A study should name its series and flag that the latest month is preliminary: a gain this small can vanish in the annual benchmark revision, and a demand case built on it should survive that.

The direction of each sector matters more than the total. Construction employment rose by 3.9k, or 5.1%, to 80.1k [^3], the signature of a cycle driven by a few large projects. It supports demand for workforce lodging and contractor flex space, and it tightens the labor pool a new project's own contractor hires from, so a budget prepared before 2026 should be re-bid, not escalated. Leisure and hospitality added 4.5k jobs, or 3.3%, reaching 141.7k [^3]: good for a hotel or restaurant demand case, harder on its payroll line. Government lost 3.6k jobs, or 1.5%, to 232.3k [^3]. In Topeka, Manhattan and Lawrence, where a state agency, a university or an Army post is the anchor, that decline is a local question a study must answer.

Households and income set the ceiling on consumer demand. The 2020 to 2024 ACS five-year estimates count 1,174,060 Kansas households and put their median income at $74,275 [^69]. Per capita personal income for 2024 comes to $65,856 in the federal income accounts [^70]. The BEA series counts farm proprietors' income, transfers and investment income that never appear in a household survey answer, so a retail study that sizes spending from it in a farm county will overstate what households spend in town. A reviewer will want to know which of the two measures sits under the spending estimate.

Two more register values describe the people a housing project would serve: 499,834 Kansans are 65 or older, and the median gross rent is $1,060 [^69]. They frame a trade area without measuring one. A senior project's market is the older households within a reasonable drive who can carry its monthly charge, and an apartment defends its rent against named buildings nearby, so both statewide numbers belong in the context paragraph, never in the absorption schedule.

SBA divides Kansas between two offices. A project in Johnson, Wyandotte, Douglas or Shawnee County is reviewed by a district whose book also spans western Missouri, so the study should keep its Kansas evidence easy to separate from the Missouri side of the market.

The 7(a) channel is modest. GoSBA, which derives its counts from SBA's loan-level release, puts Kansas 7(a) volume at $243.6M in calendar 2025 [^10]. Among the four lenders in GoSBA's table, Live Oak Bank booked the largest dollar volume on the smallest loan count, the pattern of a national lender writing larger specialty loans, while Emprise Bank wrote the most loans for a smaller total. A community bank knows the town; a national specialty lender knows the asset class. The study has to satisfy both.

The register data give the longer view. Over FY2016 to FY2025, Kansas borrowers received 382 SBA 504 loans totaling $306.3 million, counted as approvals net of cancellations [^71]. Seasoned cohorts show where the losses fell: among Kansas loans approved FY2011 to FY2020, 6.4% of the 7(a) loans ended in a charge-off, counted loan by loan, and 2.3% of the 504 loans [^71]. The gap follows the structure: a 504 loan sits behind a senior bank lien with real estate collateral and more equity, while a 7(a) start-up often rests on projections. The thinner the collateral, the more of the credit argument the study carries.

For the eastern district the latest ranking found is FY2024: OakStar Bank with 107 loans and $91.3M, U.S. Bank with 86 loans and $16.7M and BayFirst with 37 loans and $5.6M, while Rural Missouri Inc., the busiest 504 development company, closed 25 loans for $30.4M [^11]. Those counts include the district's Missouri borrowers and cannot be read as Kansas totals; the Missouri page covers that side.

State and bond capital

Network Kansas administers the allocation Treasury approved in 2022 under its small business credit initiative (SSBCI), up to $69,596,847, over 80 percent of it for the GrowKS loan fund and the angel capital program [^6,7].

The bigger lever is bond finance. Counties forwent a potential $1.1 billion or so of property tax on industrial revenue bonds issued between 2010 and 2024, and annual issues rose from 39 to 82, according to Legislative Post Audit findings presented in July 2026 [^8]. An IRB usually carries a property tax abatement [^8], and the facility it finances can be exempt from sales tax on construction labor and materials [^82]. A study that models an abated tax line should state the term and show coverage after it ends, and the audit means a 2026 abatement request may face more questions than an older one did.

The full rule set, coverage floors, loan sizes and terms among it, sits with the practice covering both SBA programs; two rules need a Kansas reading here. The first is the steepest equity test. A company no more than two years old that finances a building of limited or special purpose through 504 contributes 20% of project cost under SOP 50 10 8.1 [^74]. In Kansas a file that would meet it is a newly formed company building a hotel near the Chiefs' future stadium in Wyandotte County ahead of its first season, where the demand is scheduled but not yet open. That injection is the largest cash test in such a file, so the study should trace it to documented sources and show revenue that carries the bank note and the debenture together. SBA's own power to ask for a study rests on 13 CFR 120.160(b), which says SBA may require one [^73]; the research note on that wording is the site's reading of the clause.

The second rule is fee relief for rural Kansas. For FY2027 SBA waives the upfront 7(a) guaranty fee when a rural business borrows $700,000 or less, one of several waiver categories [^75]. The SOP treats as rural a county that the U.S. Census Bureau has defined as at least 30 percent rural [^74]. Whether a given central or western Kansas county qualifies is therefore a matter of record, not of judgment, and the study should cite it and run sources and uses with and without the fee.

A USDA Business and Industry guarantee is decided parcel by parcel: eligibility comes from the agency's map for the exact site, and no county screen for Kansas turned up, so the map result belongs inside the study rather than in a cover letter. Garden City and Salina each serve a wide rural hinterland, and food processing, grocery and lodging there are the kinds of project the guarantee is built for. Applications run through the Topeka state office or the Hays, Iola or Newton area office [^76]. The agency also pays for energy savings: one January 2025 round gave $5,883,731 to 54 Kansas projects under its renewable energy program [^5], and a study for a rural plant or grocery can test whether such a grant belongs in its sources and uses. The USDA Rural Development practice sets out the five components a USDA reviewer expects.

The Kansas Development Finance Authority may issue bonds for industrial, agricultural, health care and housing projects, and cities and counties issue their own industrial revenue bonds, with the volume cap allocated by the Secretary of Commerce [^80,81]. Sales tax revenue bonds under the STAR program now carry the Wyandotte County stadium district [^45], which the research ranks first among the 2025 announcements over $100 million. A lender inside one of these districts needs the study to separate the project's own revenue from the district's tax increment: the first depends on the project's customers, the second on sales across the whole district. Bank and institutional loans outside the SBA and USDA channels are the subject of the conventional and institutional practice.

The research did not cover EB-5, and it found no Opportunity Zone or HUBZone tract lists for the eight markets. A sponsor considering EB-5 should expect the study to establish the targeted employment area from tract data and count jobs with a model the reviewer accepts; the EB-5 practice explains the count.

Senior housing and adult care

The research located no distinct memory care licence. A 2019 industry summary reports that HB 2520 raised the home plus cap to 16 [^87], so the cap a home plus is sized to has to come from the state aging agency in writing.

Child care

The 2025 HB 2045 created the Kansas Office of Early Childhood and set the licensure threshold at 35 hours of care per week [^12]; licensing moved to the new office on July 1, 2026 [^88]. Proposed K.A.R. 28-4 amendments, heard on July 13, 2026, were estimated to save providers about $763,000 per year [^13]. The direction is lighter regulation, but ratio and capacity tables were still under review, so a center's pro forma should cite the rules in force on the study date and model the staffing ratio for its planned age mix, because labor is the largest line.

Fuel stations and storage tanks

Kansas runs its own petroleum storage tank release funds, financed by a fee of $0.01 per gallon [^14,90]. They pay up to $2.0M per release less a deductible [^14,90] that starts at $3,000 and rises by $500 per tank added [^14,90], and they sunset on July 1, 2034 [^14,90]. Unobligated balances at June 30, 2025 were $6,378,855.15 for underground tanks and $4,752,930.40 for aboveground tanks [^15]; the underground balance had been $7.79M at the end of January 2025 [^16]. A gas station loan with a real estate term of up to 25 years outlives the funds, so the study should show private pollution coverage and an environmental reserve rather than lean on the fund.

Hotels and lodging taxes

Kansas has no state hotel tax. Lodging pays the 6.50% state sales tax plus local sales tax [^17] and a locally set transient guest tax. The statutory default is 2%, but 153 jurisdictions levy the tax [^18] and at least 102 cities exceed the default under home rule [^18]. Rates moved again in 2026: Miami County went to 6% on April 1 and Leavenworth County on July 1, both in the Kansas City metro outside the two counties covered below, and Mitchell County on July 1 [^29]. A hotel study should apply the rate that binds the subject's own parcel and check whether competitors sit in a cheaper jurisdiction, because a few points of tax move rate parity for group and government travelers.

Wages, rentals and the gaps

The state minimum wage is $7.25 per hour and the tipped cash wage $2.13 [^19], but a pro forma that costs labor at the floor will not match what local employers advertise, so wages should come from local postings or the county occupational survey. Short-term rentals are a city matter: a secondary 2026 guide reports no state preemption, with cities regulating under home rule, and says HB 2481 (2026) barred municipal caps on rental permits from May 15 to July 25, 2026, a claim the research could not check against the bill text. A study relying on short-term rental supply or demand should read the city ordinance and the enacted bill. No published rules turned up for liquor licence quotas, car wash water recycling or RV park licensing; a study for those uses should get them from the city and the licensing agency.

Kansas assesses commercial and industrial real property, and commercial machinery and equipment, at 25% of appraised value, against 11.5% for residential property [^20]. An apartment building is assessed as residential; a hotel, storage facility or medical office as commercial. A study that borrows a rate from a residential comparable, or from the owner-occupied effective rate the Tax Foundation reports at 1.21% for 2024 and 1.22% for 2023 [^21], will understate a commercial project's taxes. No effective commercial rate was found, so the tax line should come from the county mill levy and a defensible value. A proposed valuation cap, SCR 1603, has no confirmed status [^89] and belongs in a study only as upside.

Insurance is the other line. The state insurance department reports that storm claims for the 2025 season nearly doubled in two years [^22], and approved homeowners rate increases averaged 15.07% in 2024 and 5.17% in 2025, about +42% over five years [^23]. Those are homeowners figures; commercial premiums have no published rate filing series in the research, no insurer-of-last-resort data and no record of carriers leaving the state. The pro forma for any residential or senior project should price a premium a carrier has indicated for the subject, a wind and hail deductible from a current quote, and a roof reserve sized to hail exposure rather than age.

Building costs have no Kansas index in the research, and no impact fee schedule or state prevailing wage rule turned up either. Construction payrolls grew faster than any other sector between July 2025 and July 2026, so a bid priced before that hiring may not hold; a current contractor bid belongs in the study, with what an overrun does to coverage.

Twelve of the 17 declarations Kansas received over the five years ending September 2026 were for severe storms [^24]. Kansas logged five declarations of all types in 2025, one of them for fire management, and, in 2026 through September 3, at least two more that the research identifies individually [^24]. DR-4869, declared on May 21, 2025 after a March winter storm with flooding and wildfires, designated 28 counties [^25]. DR-4897, declared on December 19, 2025 for the July storms, designated 12 counties, among them Wyandotte, Saline, Sumner and Barton [^26].

Public Assistance repairs public facilities, not a private hotel or apartment building. A private borrower relies on insurance and, after a declaration, on SBA disaster loans with fixed deadlines. A Kansas study should list the declarations that touched the subject's county over those five years and show what each would have cost the project in deductibles and lost revenue. Fire enters the Kansas record through declarations rather than any exposure score the research could find: DR-4869 covered wildfires along with the storm, and the Yates Center fire drew a fire management declaration on March 14, 2025 [^24]. That declaration history, read beside the county's hail record and the flood panel for the parcel, belongs in the site section.

Kansas counted 4,200 licensed child care facilities serving 144,000 children in June 2025, at an average annual family cost of $15,156 [^27]. That average is a reference point for tuition, not a cap: a center pricing above it needs a trade area whose incomes support the premium, and one in a lower-income county needs subsidy-eligible families in its mix. The count shows the sector is large; it does not say how many licensed seats sit within a short drive of the site, which is what decides absorption.

Census County Business Patterns for 2023 put Kansas restaurant establishments at 5,634 [^72]. A statewide count says little about one corridor; a restaurant study needs the competitors inside its own trade area, by format and price point. No source the research reached counts Kansas hospitals, surgery centers, apartments, hotel rooms, storage space, warehouses, convenience stores, campgrounds or senior housing statewide. Current broker figures turned up only for Wichita and for Johnson and Wyandotte counties, and they sit in those market sections; in the other six markets a study has to build the competitive census itself rather than borrow a national average.

Growth

The city of Wichita grew 0.9% from an April 2020 base of 397,589 to 400,987 residents in July 2025 [^32]. The metro reached 663,809, up from 647,610 at the 2020 census [^33]. Annual average nonfarm employment rose from 308.6k in 2023 to 312.8k in 2024, a gain of 4.2k [^34]; no 12-month change for 2025 to 2026 was found. Growth this slow means a study must show where inside the four counties new households are forming, because a trade area drawn around the core can miss them.

Supply by asset class

A speculative 215,135 SF building delivered in Kechi during Q1 2026 [^35] entered an industrial market whose vacancy was about 9.0% at the end of 2025 and 8.07% that quarter [^35]. Retail vacancy stood at 10.24% in Q1 2026 [^35]. A new flex, warehouse or retail project needs a named tenant or a clear location edge, such as frontage on the stretch of Maize Road between 21st and 37th streets that a local broker calls a premier, competitive corridor [^83], rather than a market lease-up assumption.

HUD put multifamily vacancy at 8.4% in the third quarter of 2023, against 6.4% twelve months before [^36], and Apartment List's median asking rent was $1,029 in October 2026, down 1.1% on the year [^37]. The HUD reading dates from 2023 and the asking rent is falling, so an apartment pro forma should hold rents flat or lower through lease-up. Hotel performance was not found; short-term rentals give a partial read: in the twelve months ending August 2026, 911 listings were booked 67% of available nights, earning a $121 nightly rate on average and $81 RevPAR [^38]. New supply includes the 156-room Hilton Garden Inn and Homewood Suites at the airport, which HVS showed as planned for May 2025 [^39] and a hotel listing site shows as opened in November 2025 [^84], and a downtown AC Hotel that opened in July 2025 [^84]. A hotel study needs a competitive set report, not rental data.

Two megaprojects

The Panasonic battery plant in De Soto, in western Johnson County, cost $4 billion and covers 4.7M SF [^42]. At the July 14, 2025 grand opening the state said approximately 1,000 staff had been hired [^44]; two days later a local paper reported about 1,100 employees, with 1 of 8 production lines running [^43]. By February 2026 the plant had about 1,400 employees against 4,000 jobs promised, a target leaders had said in July 2025 it would reach within 18 months [^42]. A study that sizes K-10 corridor housing, retail or hotel demand from the target is projecting jobs that did not exist in early 2026. The current headcount is the base case; the target is an upside scenario with a date attached.

The Chiefs' domed stadium in Wyandotte County, announced on December 22, 2025 for the 2031 season, costs $3 billion, funded with $1.8 billion of state STAR bonds and $1.2 billion of private money [^45]. Entertainment districts at the stadium and the Olathe headquarters site add $1 billion, split 60% public and 40% private [^46]. The state projects a construction-phase impact of $4.4 billion and over 20,000 construction-phase jobs [^4]. The site covers about 236 acres near State Avenue and 126th Street, with about $450M of local sales-tax support approved by the county [^47]. For a hotel, restaurant or retail study near Village West, game days bring intense but infrequent demand; the base case is an ordinary week with nothing on the calendar, and debt service has to clear on that week alone.

The lodging tax spread is wide enough to move a hotel's position: a Kansas City, Kansas hotel in the Homefield district charges the highest rate in the table and a De Soto property the lowest, and a corporate travel manager sees the difference on the folio. A study should place every competitor in its taxing jurisdiction. The Olathe headquarters site is the open question nearby: a June 2026 report found its bonds unconfirmed [^86], and the research found no later confirmation, so a study within reach of it should treat it as unbuilt. The only lender figures touching the two counties are the district-wide FY2024 rankings in the capital section, and the research holds no finding on Opportunity Zone, HUBZone or USDA status for either county.

The Topeka metro covers Shawnee, Jackson, Jefferson, Osage and Wabaunsee counties, and the state is its employer of record. The city's 2023 financial report counted 9,919 State of Kansas employees in Topeka at December 31, 2023, or 8.7% of 113,871 city jobs [^53]. Stormont Vail Health employed 4,400 and Topeka USD 501 employed 2,500 [^53]. Statewide government employment fell over the year to July 2026, and a Topeka study should find out which agencies in the capital shed staff before counting state workers as tenants or customers.

Population is flat. The metro held 233,052 residents [^54]; the city's count slipped from 126,609 in 2020 to 125,795 in 2025 [^50]. Nonfarm employment was a preliminary 118.9k in August 2026 against 116.8k in March 2026 [^55], two months that do not make a trend. Without a published vacancy, rent or pipeline figure for the five counties, a Topeka apartment or retail case draws its strength from tenants tied to the agencies, the hospital system or the schools.

Lodging tax is the rule that is moving. The city and Shawnee County each levy 7.00% [^29]. In May 2026 the city voted to raise its rate to 8.5% from January 1, 2027 with no sunset, replacing a schedule that would have cut it to 6% in 2028; the Hotel Topeka district charges 8%, rising to 8.5% [^56]. Osage County raised its rate to 6% on April 1, 2026 [^29]. The revenue funds downtown anchors such as Evergy Plaza and the Jayhawk Theatre [^56], which helps event demand, but a hotel study written before May 2026 used the wrong future rate.

The Lawrence metro is Douglas County, and the University of Kansas is its economy: KU employed 8,845 people in December 2025, against 1,945 at LMH Health, the second employer [^57]. A town this dependent on one institution needs a study that separates student demand, which follows enrollment and the academic calendar, from the permanent household base, and that shows occupancy over the summer and in a weak enrollment year.

LMH Health ran an operating revenue budget of $448M in January 2025 [^58] and announced a $25M heart center project in May 2025 [^59]. Medical office, outpatient and senior housing studies can lean on that system.

Douglas County grew 1.8% between April 2020 and July 2025, from 118,785 residents to 120,920 [^60]; within it, the city of Lawrence reached 96,367 in 2025, from 94,941 in 2020 [^50]. The guest tax has been 8.00% since January 2026 [^29]. The open question is how much of the battery plant's workforce will live here: De Soto sits about twenty minutes east [^42], and no commuting count was found. A housing study that claims plant workers as demand should show where current employees live, from employer or commute-flow data, rather than assume it.

The Manhattan metro covers Riley, Geary and Pottawatomie counties and held 136,122 residents in July 2025 [^54]. Riley County, home to Kansas State University, held 72,598 [^54]; Geary County, with Junction City beside Fort Riley, held 36,338 [^54]; and Pottawatomie County held 27,186 [^54]. University and post headcounts were found only undated, so none is given here.

That concentration is the credit question. A unit moving in or out of Fort Riley, or a change in K-State enrollment, can shift rental demand faster than any developer can respond, so a study for apartments, hotels or retail should state the share of demand it draws from each and test a realistic reduction. That split is the first number a reviewer will check.

Private investment is arriving in Geary County: Camso Manufacturing's Junction City project carries $113,875,000 of capital investment and 181 jobs in the state's FY2025 budget narrative [^28]. Lodging tax differs across the market. Manhattan levies 7.50%, unchanged since July 2019, and Geary County moved to 7.00% on July 1, 2026 [^29]; Manhattan first levied the tax in 1978 at 1.5% [^18]. A hotel study should place the property on the correct side of that line and model football weekends separately from ordinary weeks.

The Salina micropolitan area covers Saline and Ottawa counties at a junction of interstates across central Kansas. Its recent investment dates from 2022. Schwan's announced an expansion bringing its cumulative investment to $600M, with a 140,000 SF distribution center and 225 jobs [^61]. Great Plains Manufacturing, a Kubota company, opened a $178M construction equipment plant with 150 jobs at opening and plans for more than 900 [^62]. No project over $50M was found for 2025 to 2026 and no current headcount for either plant, so a study should not assume the planned jobs were filled.

Population went the other way. Saline County fell 1.7%, from 54,302 in April 2020 to 53,377 in July 2025 [^63]; the micropolitan area held 59,121 [^54] and the city 46,094 against 46,881 in 2020 [^50]. Jobs without population growth usually mean commuters and tight housing: an apartment project may find plant workers who now drive in, while a retail project cannot count on a growing household base.

Guest tax runs 7.75% in Salina and 5.00% in Ottawa County [^29], and the downtown STAR bond district [^29] gives a hotel study an event and dining core to cite. The interstate junction supports fuel, travel center and limited-service hotel uses; with no traffic counts found, the study should take them from the state's count station nearest the site. Saline County was named in the July 2025 storm declaration, which belongs in any study's hazard section.

The Garden City micropolitan area is Finney County, where Tyson runs a beef plant. Tyson planned to relocate 200+ associates from its closed Emporia plant in 2025 [^65]. A prospect for a thermoforming plant of about 510,000 SF, with about 230 jobs and about $146M of investment, was reported in March 2025 [^64], from an automated meeting summary that should be checked against the minutes.

The population numbers run against the job news. Finney County's estimate fell to 37,505 in 2025 from 38,470 at the 2020 census [^66], and Garden City's fell to 27,402 from 28,158 [^50]. Local officials point to housing: people who would take the jobs cannot find homes. In September 2025 commissioners cited 853 housing units in design and a target of 3,000 units by 2030 [^67].

Garden City inverts the usual demand question. For an apartment or workforce housing project the open questions are construction cost, the rents local wages can carry, and the incentives the project depends on. A hotel or retail study faces the opposite problem: fewer residents and seasonal agricultural traffic. Finney County levies a 6.00% guest tax [^29], and the state's rate table lists no separate Garden City rate.

The Hays micropolitan area is Ellis County, the medical, retail and education center for a wide stretch of northwest Kansas. This section works at the county level. Local press reports that the regional hospital has recently operated at a loss [^68], so a medical office or senior housing study that depends on its referrals should read its latest financial statements, and a lodging or rental study near the university should count students who live in the county rather than enrollment.

Each city in the county sets its own lodging tax; the small city of Ellis levies 5.00% [^29]. A study here should rest on county and regional demand generators, the hospital, the university, interstate travel and agricultural business, and should list every competing project with its status and unit count, the benchmark a reviewer will measure absorption against.

    Before a closing timetable goes into the study, the Topeka office's statement of remaining authority should be recorded with its date; the research found no FY2025 state total to stand in for it.

  3. Hotel performance and pipeline

    No market here has a published occupancy, average daily rate or RevPAR series. A hotel study should include a competitive set report and a pipeline list from city permits.

  4. Traffic counts

    No state traffic count station was captured for any market. A fuel or travel center study should cite the nearest station by number and year.

  5. Tax and insurance quoted for the subject

    The costs section lists what the Kansas record lacks on both lines. The credit file should hold the county's current levy sheet and a carrier's dated quote for the building, with any IRB abatement shown beside the full tax due after its term.

  6. Care licensing details

    Memory care has no distinct licence, and child care ratio tables were under review in 2026. Confirm the rule with the licensing agency on the study date.

  7. Permits, impact fees and utilities

    No 2025 permit counts, impact fees, sewer moratoria or water records were found for any market. Permits come from the Census building permits survey; water, sewer and power capacity has to come from each provider in writing.

One other case touches Kansas from outside: the Mississippi grocery case under USDA cites Kansas State University's work on rural grocery closures in its competitive context, the store-loss question a small Kansas town raises. The full set is in the case library.
