Spokane County held 539,339 residents in 2020, 566,000 in 2025 and 570,600 in 2026 by OFM's April 1 estimates, which the office published on June 29, 2026 [^1]. In the last of those years the City of Spokane gained 1,200 residents, the largest of the four city gains the research lists, Liberty Lake 680 residents, or 4.82%, Spokane Valley 600 residents and Deer Park 430 residents, or 8.37% [^1]. The research gives percentage rates for Deer Park and Liberty Lake only, so a trade area that reaches either town can be described both ways, and one in the City of Spokane or Spokane Valley by its count of new residents.

The county's own count rose in each period OFM reports, from 539,339 in 2020 to 570,600 in 2026 on the April dates [^1], and the research gives no separate population count for Stevens County, the second county in the register's metro. A study that draws its trade area into Stevens County takes that county's figures from OFM's table for itself.

Housing construction slowed. Spokane County permitted 2,976 residential units in 2025 against 3,626 in 2024, down 18%, by WCRER's republication of the Census Bureau's permit survey; 1,375 were single-family and 1,601 multifamily [^4]. No count of units under construction was found, so an apartment study confirms the pipeline project by project.

The register's household figures cover the Census Bureau's Spokane-Spokane Valley metro, Spokane and Stevens counties. Its 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey estimates count 237,769 households, a median gross rent of $1,264 for renters who pay cash rent, and owner-occupied homes valued at a median of $405,300 [^8]. Its count of people 65 or older in the two counties is 107,705 [^8], the population a senior housing study cuts down to its own drive time before it counts competing beds. The survey's figures cover both counties over five years, where OFM's counts above are Spokane County's alone on single April dates, so the two are not set against each other.

The most recent headcounts found for Spokane County's employers are from November 2023, in SVN Cornerstone's reproduction of the Journal of Business list [^3]. The City of Spokane publishes the same top five, in the same order, from the Journal of Business's 2023 Market Fact Book [^5]. Its top five are a military base, a health system, the state, the city's schools and Amazon.

The list sorts into a few kinds of demand. Three of its nine employers are in health care: Providence Inland Northwest Washington with 6,775 employees, MultiCare Inland Northwest with 3,354 and the Mann-Grandstaff VA with 1,605 [^3]. For a medical office building, a senior housing community or apartments aimed at hospital staff, those are the counts a demand case starts from, each dated November 2023. Two are universities, Gonzaga University with 1,337 employees and Eastern Washington University with 1,158 [^3]; those are staff counts, not enrollment, which the research does not hold, so a student housing study takes enrollment from each university. Fairchild Air Force Base, at 7,404, is the largest count on the list [^3], and no newer figure for the base was found. Amazon, with 3,724, is the one private employer outside health care among the five [^3].

Payrolls moved down over the latest year. ESD's Spokane County profile counts 264,000 nonfarm jobs in the Spokane metro in July 2026, 2,400 fewer than a year earlier, a fall of 0.9%, with unemployment at 3.4% [^9]. The Seattle page reads King County from ESD's August 2026 profile.

BEA puts per capita personal income in Spokane and Stevens counties at $61,022 in 2024 [^10], a mean over all residents, and the 2020 to 2024 survey puts median household income at $77,488 [^8].

ACTIV8 Real Estate's second-quarter 2026 industrial report, built on CoStar data, counts about 402,000 square feet under construction across the Spokane industrial market, 78.6% of it pre-leased, with a rent of $9.59 per square foot and vacancy of 6.1%, about 20 basis points higher than a year earlier [^2]. The vacancy, the rent and the construction figures are all for the same quarter and the same report, so they can be read together as one reading of the Spokane market; the report's submarkets are in the next section. A speculative building competes for the space that is not pre-leased.

No apartment vacancy or rent for Spokane County was found. Kidder Mathews reads apartment vacancy for its Eastern Washington region at 7.7% for the second quarter of 2026, down 90 basis points on the year, and that is the nearest reading [^11]. It is a regional figure, not Spokane County's, and it comes from a different firm and a wider area than ACTIV8's industrial report for the same quarter.

For retail, the only reading is a tenant-representation site's summary of ACTIV8's figures, which the research could not tie to a dated edition, so its numbers are not printed here [^12]. Hotel occupancy and rate, self-storage space, senior housing occupancy and counts of RV parks and fuel stations were not found for Spokane. A hotel study here buys an STR set for its competitors, and a storage or senior housing study counts competing properties by name.

The Spokane record names places through its brokers rather than through a corridor study. ACTIV8 divides the industrial market into Spokane Valley, the West Plains and North Metro [^2]. Spokane Valley is also the city whose 600-resident gain in the year to April 2026 OFM records [^1]. For a warehouse, a flex building or a retail pad, the submarket is the first fact the study fixes, because ACTIV8 reads the market by those submarkets. OFM's city table carries no figure for the West Plains or North Metro as areas, so a trade area there is built from the cities and census tracts it covers.

No named road, interchange or exit and no WSDOT annual average daily traffic count was found for any Spokane location. A fuel, coffee, quick-service or retail site's traffic therefore comes from WSDOT's Traffic GeoPortal for the nearest counted segment, dated, and from a count of the subject's own frontage where the GeoPortal has none.

The Governor's wildfire emergency proclamation of August 2026 covered the whole state [^13], and after the Spokane Complex fires the Insurance Commissioner's office sent additional staff to Spokane to help people with claims, the Spokesman-Review reported on August 6, 2026 [^7].

Emergency Order 26-01 ran from August 3 to September 30, 2026 for the ZIP codes it listed. Under it insurers could not cancel for non-payment, premiums had a 45-day grace period, and the notice before a property non-renewal grew from 60 days to 120, and for auto cover from 20 days to 60 [^6]. Spokane County was not among the ten counties named in the Governor's January 2026 request for Individual Assistance after the December 2025 storms [^14].

The statewide record of insurer non-renewals and of the FAIR Plan is on the Washington page; the research holds no Spokane County count of either.

Impact fees, moratoria, overlays and zoning changes for 2025 and 2026, a short-term rental ordinance, a local hotel tax rate and news of water or sewer capacity were not found for the City of Spokane or for Spokane County. Each is confirmed with the city or county that would issue the permit, in writing, before a study relies on the site. The short-term rental gap is one the state does not fill: it issues no rental permit and does not preempt local rules [^15], so a Spokane rental study has to find the local ordinance itself.

The state rules do reach the site. Spokane is outside Seattle, so the state's combined lodging tax cap of 12% applies to hotel and short-term rental budgets in Spokane, under the House bill report on HB 2559 [^16]. The retail sales tax on business services since October 1, 2025, the 2026 and 2027 wage floors and the Certificate of Need list are on the Washington page.

The Certificate of Need list bears directly on Spokane's health systems, which hold three places on the county's employer list. A hospital, an ambulatory surgical facility that is not in-office, a nursing home adding beds, a dialysis center, a hospice or a home health agency needs a certificate under chapter 70.38 RCW before it can open [^17], and the Department of Health's project register shows whether a competing application is already under review [^18]. A medical office building with no licensed beds or surgery suites is outside that list, and its demand rests on the health systems' staff and patients instead.

SBA's Seattle District, which serves Spokane County, keeps offices in Seattle and in Spokane [^19,20]. For Spokane and Stevens county projects, SBA's FOIA files record 1,166 loans under 7(a) worth $493.6 million across fiscal years 2016 to 2025, and 98 loans under 504 with $79.5 million in debentures, net of cancelled loans [^21].

No Spokane ranking of SBA 7(a) lenders or certified development companies turned up; the Washington page holds the statewide 7(a) table for 2025. SBA can ask for a study under 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^22]; the program tests are on the SBA 7(a) and 504 page. Deer Park and Cheney need a USDA map check, the research notes, before a USDA Rural Development loan is assumed; Opportunity Zone and HUBZone status are open questions for every tract in the county.

No published case names a site in Spokane or Stevens County. The Washington coffee shop case, an SBA 7(a) case for a specialty coffee shop with a double drive-thru, mentions Spokane in two ways: it contrasts a suburban Puget Sound commuter corridor with Spokane and the rural Columbia Basin, and its sources list the Seattle District's offices in Seattle and Spokane. Its pro forma is not a Spokane pro forma, and none of this page's figures is applied to it. The Washington page lists the state's cases together.
