Jacksonville's population story starts in Duval County, the only county the research measures directly. The Census Bureau counted 995,569 Duval residents on April 1, 2020 and estimates 1,062,963 on July 1, 2025, a rise of 6.8% [^1].

St. Johns, Clay, Nassau and Baker have no population figure of their own in the research, so a site in Nocatee, Fleming Island, Yulee or Macclenny is sized from the county's own Census rows.

Permits trace the same households. Duval County issued permits for 6,017 housing units in calendar 2025 [^1]. At the metro level, the Census permit series that FRED republishes recorded 1,140 units in September 2025 and 1,124 in October, then 613 in November and 701 in December [^2]. The full-year metro total was not found, so the one turn the permit record shows is that late-2025 drop, and it is too short a run to call a trend.

The 2020 to 2024 five-year American Community Survey estimate puts the metro's median household income at $79,643 [^12].

The latest payroll reading is flat. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts 803.8k nonfarm jobs in the Jacksonville metro in August 2026, a preliminary figure, against 803.3k in August 2025 [^3]. BLS gives the August-to-August movement no significance flag [^3].

The Orlando page can point to a sector, leisure and hospitality, adding jobs beneath its flat metro total; the Jacksonville record has no sector series at all, so the flat total is the only employment evidence a study here can cite.

The one anchor record in the research, a secondary Wikipedia entry for the city, names JAXPORT, which it describes as Florida's largest seaport by volume, and the Marine Corps' Blount Island Command, and gives no headcount for either [^15].

Jacksonville sits where I-95, the East Coast's main north-south route, crosses the eastern end of I-10, which runs west toward Tallahassee. I-295 loops around the city and links both. Jacksonville International Airport sits on the Northside near the Ocean Way area, and the port's terminals line the St. Johns River east of downtown.

The industrial evidence splits by side of town. On the West Side, around the I-10 and I-295 interchange, Ironmark counts net absorption of 767,000 square feet and vacancy of 11.6% in Q2 2026 [^4]. In the Ocean Way submarket, on the Northside near the airport and I-95 north, the same report counts net absorption of 569,000 square feet and vacancy of 12.2% in that quarter [^4]. Cushman & Wakefield's metro-wide read puts vacancy 290 basis points higher in Q2 2026 than a year earlier [^5].

North of the city, I-95 crosses the state line onto the Georgia coast, which has its own page for Savannah.

FDOT counts are missing for every Jacksonville road in the research, the I-95 and I-10 interchange and the Northside exits included.

Self-storage: the most new space of any U.S. city

RentCafe's August 2026 report counts 10.5 square feet of self-storage per city resident and puts 705,437 square feet, about 6.6% of the city's inventory, due in the City of Jacksonville in 2026, more than any other U.S. city [^6]. Yardi Matrix data, as reported by Multi-Housing News, show a metro inventory of 15.8 million square feet, 10.8 square feet per resident, and 913,201 square feet under construction in December 2025, with further space planned beyond that, and the same report lists Jacksonville among the emerging storage markets of 2026 [^7]. The 705,437 square feet is space due in the city during 2026 and the 913,201 square feet space under construction across the metro in December 2025, so the two are not one pipeline; the two per-resident figures likewise cover the city in August 2026 and the metro in December 2025. The City of Miami's storage figures are on our Miami page. Our self-storage feasibility page sets out the competitor census.

Retail: occupied space fell in Q2 2026

Colliers reports average retail asking rent of $25.77 per square foot, vacancy of 5.0% and net absorption of minus 24,441 square feet in Q2 2026 [^8]. Occupied retail space in the metro shrank on net that quarter, so a new strip center opens into a market that was handing space back, not taking it up. The Census Bureau counted 691 establishments in the gas station and convenience store category across the metro in 2023 (County Business Patterns) [^11], the starting count for a gas station and c-store study. Our retail feasibility page covers the rest of the evidence.

Industrial: Northside

NAI Hallmark puts Northside industrial vacancy at about 11% in Q2 2026 [^18]. For the metro as a whole, Cushman & Wakefield reads Q2 2026 vacancy at 11.6%, 290 basis points up on the year, with about 1.5 million square feet under construction [^5], while Ironmark's metro figure for the same quarter is 9.4% [^4].

Multifamily and housing

No current apartment rent or units-under-construction count for Jacksonville came back in the research, so the renter base comes from the Census alone. The 2020 to 2024 five-year American Community Survey estimate counts 222,323 renter households in the metro, with a median gross rent of $1,513 [^12]. On the ownership side, the same 2020 to 2024 five-year American Community Survey estimate puts the median home value at $343,400 [^12]. With no pipeline count on record, the county and metro permit files above carry more weight in a Jacksonville multifamily study than the survey median does.

Hotels

Hotels and motels in the metro (casino hotels, inns and other lodging excluded) employed 7,819 employees in 2023 (County Business Patterns) [^11]. Beyond that workforce count and the county hotel tax rates set out under local rules [^17], the record holds no hotel figure: occupancy, average daily rate, RevPAR and the room pipeline are all absent, which leaves a Jacksonville hotel study without a published performance benchmark of any kind.

Senior housing and the empty classes

The 2020 to 2024 five-year American Community Survey estimate counts 288,035 residents aged 65 and over in the metro [^12], a demand base with no matching supply figure: senior housing inventory, office, medical office, RV parks and RV and boat storage have no Jacksonville number in the research.

Duval's 2026 hotel tax is 6%, and that rate already includes a 2% consolidated-county convention development tax; St. Johns, Clay and Nassau levy 5%, Nassau's not countywide, and Baker 3% [^17].

Impact fees are the other local rule in the Jacksonville record. A Florida Attorney General opinion, Impact Fees Increase, records that St. Johns County held its 2025 increase to the statutory maximum and concludes that Nassau County may not exceed that cap in the circumstances presented [^16].

The SBA's North Florida District, which covers the metro, described its fiscal 2025 lending as a record. Its release describes the district's reach as running from Orlando to the Panhandle [^9]. Duval County received 269 loans under the 7(a) and 504 programs combined, a count up 19% on the year, for $177.1M, and St. Johns County received 132 loans, up 31%, for $79.4M [^9]. St. Johns, the suburban county south of Duval, grew its loan count faster than the core county did. Those figures reached the research through a local chamber's repost of the district's announcement rather than an SBA dataset.

The SBA's loan-level data give a longer view. Across the metro, 7(a) lenders made 2,254 loans approved in fiscal years 2016 to 2025, net of cancellations [^10]. Their dollar volume over the same fiscal years 2016 to 2025, net of cancellations, was $1,391.2 million [^10]. The 504 program added $385.0 million approved in fiscal years 2016 to 2025, net of cancellations [^10]. The district announcement and the loan-level file differ in geography, period and method.

The rule behind a study request is 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^13], and a 7(a) loan is tested at a debt service coverage floor of 1.15x [^14]. In this market the storage pipeline and the flat payroll count are what that coverage test will be run against. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice page sets out what the program asks a study to prove, and our conventional and institutional lending page covers loans outside the SBA programs.
