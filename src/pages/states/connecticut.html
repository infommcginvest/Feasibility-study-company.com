The Department of Energy and Environmental Protection ran the Underground Storage Tank Petroleum Clean-up Program until June 23, 2025. The state budget closed it on that date, moved the money appropriated to it into the General Fund and cancelled every application the program had not yet decided [^1]. A gas station operator, a convenience store with pumps or a trucking yard that finds a release now pays for the work through its own resources and whatever environmental cover it carries; there is no state program to file with.

The reporting rules had changed first. A release discovered on or after May 7, 2025 is reported to the department on its REACT forms, and a release discovered before that date must show compliance by May 7, 2027 [^1]. The two dates split the releases at Connecticut fuel sites into a newer group, governed by the REACT filings, and an older group with a deadline that falls inside the first operating years of a project financed in 2026.

That split is where a lender's file on a Connecticut fuel site begins. For a purchase, the seller's release history decides which group the site belongs to, and a pre-2025 release brings the 2027 compliance date into the buyer's schedule and budget. For a new build or a rebuild of the tanks, the question is the environmental policy the borrower will hold, because the fund that once stood behind a cleanup cost is gone. Neither question is about demand, and both come before it: a forecast of gallons and inside sales is of little use to a credit officer who cannot see who pays if the ground under the canopy is contaminated.

The fuel and travel pages set out the demand side: gas stations and convenience stores and truck stops and travel centers. This page carries what is particular to Connecticut: the fund's end and the two reporting dates.

Connecticut's Certificate of Need program reviews new health facilities, major equipment such as an MRI unit, terminations of services and transfers of ownership, and in 2025 it was still run by the Office of Health Strategy [^8]. Public Act 25-168, signed on June 30, 2025, redefined a termination of services as a stop of more than 180 days in any two-year period, which makes a long pause in a service line a reviewable event of its own [^8].

The larger change came with the FY2027 appropriations act, approved on May 2, 2026. It eliminates the Office of Health Strategy and moves Certificate of Need authority to a three-member panel, the Commissioner of Public Health, the Secretary of the Office of Policy and Management and the Commissioner of Social Services, with the Department of Public Health supporting it. The law takes effect on October 1, 2026, the panel reviews applications filed on or after July 1, 2027, the closing of a hospital satellite facility is added to the actions that need review, and the consultant costs an applicant can be charged are capped at $100,000 [^2,3].

A sponsor planning a surgery center, an imaging suite, a behavioral health unit or the sale of a hospital service in 2027 therefore schedules the filing around the panel's start date, and a study that tests the opening date takes the decision-maker from the date the application will be filed. A closure is no longer outside the program either: a hospital system that plans to close a satellite facility files for the closing as well.

The supply count behind those decisions is short. The Office of Health Strategy's FY2024 report on hospital finances counts 27 short-term acute care hospitals in Connecticut, 24 of them 501(c)(3) organizations [^9]. For freestanding surgery the newest statewide inventory on record is the 2016 Supplemental Statewide Facilities Plan [^10], so a surgery or imaging study counts its competitors from the applicant's own service area rather than from a state table. No primary source in the record gives Connecticut's licensing classes or thresholds for assisted living or memory care; the assisted living page covers how a study handles that market, and the medical office and ambulatory surgery page and the behavioral health page cover theirs.

Liquor permits in Connecticut are issued by the state, through the Department of Consumer Protection's Liquor Control Division [^60]. A 2026 guide that quotes the department lists $1,450 for a restaurant full liquor permit, $2,000 for a café permit, $2,055 for a hotel permit and $500 for a provisional permit, and since January 1, 2026 every applicant has had to complete the Liquor Law Education Program [^14]. Neither a quota on permits nor a market price for buying an existing one was found, so a restaurant or hotel budget here starts from the state fee and the training, and a study that adds a transfer price needs a dated source for it.

Short-term rentals are a town matter. Since October 1, 2024 municipalities may regulate and license them by ordinance under section 7-148qq of the General Statutes, and no statewide registry had been enacted when the Office of Legislative Research reported in January 2026 [^61]. The registry bill of the 2026 session, HB 5536, would create one at the Department of Revenue Services, charge $100 a property and allow an optional municipal supplemental tax; its last recorded action, on April 16, 2026, was a favorable report tabled for the House calendar [^17]. An inn or rental portfolio is therefore checked against the ordinance of each town it operates in.

Housing law moved in a special session. HB 8002, signed on November 26, 2025, with most of its provisions in force from January 1, 2026, bars parking minimums for residential projects of fewer than 16 units and requires a parking needs assessment above that size, asks every town for a housing growth plan and creates incentives for transit-oriented development districts [^12,62,63]. For a small apartment building the change can remove a parking structure or a land assembly from the budget, and a study tests the site plan under the new rule rather than the town's old minimum.

Property tax supplied 35.3% of Connecticut's state and local revenue in FY2023, on the Tax Foundation's 2026 state page [^7]. The effective commercial rate for 2025 or 2026 was not found: the Yankee Institute, citing the Tax Foundation on March 6, 2025, gives 1.92% and ranks Connecticut third, behind New Jersey and Illinois [^64], while the Tax Foundation's 2026 page gives 1.54% on owner-occupied housing value [^7]. A commercial pro forma takes its tax from the town's mill rate and the parcel's assessment, not from either statewide figure. The state sales tax is 6.35%, with no local sales tax added [^7].

The minimum wage rose to $16.94 on January 1, 2026, from $16.35, with a tipped cash wage of $6.38 and a bartender cash wage of $8.23 [^13]; a further increase for 2027 is announced on a page that carries no date and is not given here. On public works, prevailing wage applies to new construction of $1,000,000 or more and to remodeling or repair of $100,000 or more under section 31-53, and the 2025 budget extended it to custom fabrication done off site [^11].

The state room occupancy tax of 15% applies to short-term rentals as well as hotels, and no local lodging tax was identified [^65].

The Insurance Department's advisory of February 6, 2026 names the Connecticut Property Insurance Placement Facility, the FAIR Plan, as Connecticut's insurer of last resort [^66].
