The Executive Office of Housing's 2025 Integrated Housing Report counts 3,778 housing units permitted in Rhode Island during calendar 2025, which it reports as the most since the late 1980s [^1]. A state total of that kind says that builders were busy; it does not say where. The report answers it in municipal tables, and the market sections below are written from them.

For each city and town the report gives the units permitted in 2025, split by building size from single-unit houses to buildings of five or more units, and the certificates of occupancy issued in the same year [^1]. Beside them sit three readings from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey: the share of housing units vacant and offered for sale or for rent in the 2020 to 2024 survey, the median gross rent in the 2024 five-year release, and that rent's change since 2020 [^1]. A fourth table sets each municipality's 2024 share of low- and moderate-income housing against the state's 10% standard [^1].

No municipal apartment vacancy rate is published in Rhode Island, so the for-sale-plus-for-rent rate stands in for one, and it is a different measure. It sets the vacant units on the market, owner and rental alike, against every housing unit in the town, and it pools five years of survey responses rather than describing a quarter. A low reading still tells a lender something definite: little of that town's housing sat empty and available over those years. Where the report gives the counts behind a rate, the market section prints them beside it.

The affordable housing table bears on supply. A municipality at or above the standard keeps its comprehensive-permit autonomy, and one below it is exposed to comprehensive permits [^1]. A lender reading a pipeline in a town below the line therefore has two questions, what the town has permitted and what a comprehensive permit could still add, and the permit table answers only the first. On the 2024 chart Providence, Newport, Woonsocket, Pawtucket and Central Falls are above the line; Warwick, Cranston, North Kingstown, South Kingstown, Narragansett, Cumberland, Lincoln and Westerly are below it [^1].

For the state as a whole, the Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 count is 1,114,521 people at July 1, 2025, and the base it starts from, April 1, 2020, is 1,097,357 [^3]. The Department of Labor and Training released the same vintage for each city and town in May 2026, and every population pair in the market sections comes from that release [^2]. The register's statewide survey medians for 2020 to 2024 are in the table; each market section carries its own county's.

On the housing report's 2024 chart, 5.78% of Warwick's housing counted as low- and moderate-income housing, 1,607 units short of the standard, and 6.23% of Cranston's, 1,283 units short [^1]. Both cities met the 2024 test only through the alternative formula, which was repealed with effect from January 1, 2026, which leaves both exposed to comprehensive permits [^1]. For a multifamily or mixed-use project in either city the change works on supply from two sides: it may open a comprehensive permit route to the sponsor, and it may open the same route to the next project that would compete with the subject.

The permit and completion counts point the same way for Warwick's larger buildings. Of the 416 units Warwick permitted in 2025, 297 units were in buildings of five or more; the city's certificates of occupancy that year covered 119 units, and not one was in a building that large [^1]. Cranston permitted 158 units and issued certificates for 48 units [^1]. No unit in a large Warwick building was finished in 2025, so the 297 were all still to be delivered at the year's end, and a lease-up schedule for a new apartment building there sets them against its own opening date rather than counting them as stock already absorbed.

Very little housing sat vacant and on the market in either city over the 2020 to 2024 survey: 383 for-sale and for-rent vacant units among Warwick's 37,516 housing units, a rate of 1.02%, and 169 among Cranston's 33,834, or 0.50% [^1]. The survey's median gross rents were $1,363 in Warwick and $1,375 in Cranston [^1]. Those medians describe the whole renter stock, older units included. No asking rent series for either city was found, so a new building's rent is set from its own competitive set and read against these medians, not taken from them.

Both cities have grown since 2020. The Vintage 2025 town release puts Warwick at 84,187 and Cranston at 84,647 for July 2025, up from 82,831 and 82,939 in April 2020 [^2].

Neither city has a register geography of its own, because the register keys Rhode Island by county and the two cities sit in different counties [^2]. The table gives Kent County's median household income, which covers Warwick and the rest of Kent County but not Cranston. Providence County's register figures take in Cranston, Providence and the Blackstone Valley towns together, and they are printed once, in the Providence section.

Providence permitted 778 units in 2025: 90 in single-unit buildings, 248 in two-unit buildings, 38 in buildings of three or four units and 402 in buildings of five units or more [^1]. Certificates of occupancy that year covered 727 units, 470 of them in the largest building class [^1]. Both counts are the city's own, and that matters, because the larger pipeline count on this page, CoStar's, is drawn for a region.

CoStar reported on March 12, 2026 that more than 1,800 multifamily units were under construction in what it calls the Providence region in the first quarter of 2026, its highest quarterly total since mid-2022, and that 92% of them were suburban [^9]. The same article said apartment vacancy in the region had climbed while leasing slowed over the prior year [^9]. For a new building in the city, the regional count and the city's permits answer different questions: the first is the competition CoStar sees across its region, most of it in the suburbs, and the second is what the city itself approved in 2025.

Inside the city, the 2020 to 2024 survey counts 3,072 vacant units for sale or for rent among 79,423 housing units, 3.87% [^1]. The median Providence renter household paid $1,408 in gross rent in the 2024 five-year release, a rise of 38.7% since 2020 by the housing report's reckoning [^1]. A project with a heavy wastewater load, a laundromat, a food plant, a brewery or a large hotel among them, asks its sewer authority in writing for the current charges and any change it plans, and the operating budget carries the answer.

Rhode Island counted 515,100 nonfarm jobs in August 2026, seasonally adjusted, 300 more than a year earlier, and 450,600 private jobs, up 1,500 [^5]. Since January 1, 2026 a room stay of 30 days or less in Rhode Island carries 14% in tax: the 7% sales tax, the 5% state hotel tax and a local hotel tax of 2%, up from 1% [^21]. The local rate is set statewide, so it does not vary from town to town [^21]. Whole-home short-term rentals pay the sales tax and the local tax plus a 5% whole-home rental tax, and their operators register with the Division of Taxation [^22]. Campground rentals are taxed as tourist camps where the campground supplies the structure, tents included, which reaches an RV park or campground budget that rents out its own structures or tents [^24].

Demand had a record year. Rhode Island Commerce's 2025 annual report counts 29.4 million visitors in fiscal 2025 [^25]. At the Rhode Island Hospitality Association's breakfast, as What's Up Newp reported on September 23, 2026, CoStar's Jason Weinstein put coastal Rhode Island RevPAR on pace for a 12.9% gain in 2026 and forecast 3.2% for 2027, while Providence and Warwick were expected to slip slightly in 2026 and gain 2.5% in 2027 [^26]. Short-term rentals are the town's open rule question. Westerly already regulates short-term rentals and has raised its annual registration fee once by amendment; a draft the council has been deliberating would confine unhosted rentals to a coastal village overlay, an approach modeled on Newport's ordinance, The Westerly Sun reported [^28]. The council's agenda of June 2, 2025 carried a short-term rental registration ordinance beside a proposed sewer use charge of $356 per unit for fiscal 2025 to 2026 and a $100 water infrastructure improvement fee [^27]. A lodging or rental project in the town reads the ordinance in force on its own approval date, and its budget carries both charges.

Rhode Island's FY2027 budget, signed on June 12, 2026, narrowed Certificate of Need review [^29]. A new hospital (unless it treats behavioral health only), nursing facility, hospice, inpatient rehabilitation facility or free-standing emergency care facility still needs a certificate; home nursing care, home care, surgical centers, multi-practice physician and podiatry ambulatory surgery centers and changes in bed capacity no longer do [^29]. Review of services now covers neonatal intensive care, organ transplantation, cardiac catheterization, open-heart surgery and particle-accelerator radiation therapy, and capital expenditure is reviewed only above $50 million [^30]. Before the change the equipment threshold stood at $3,247,713 and the construction threshold at $7,577,998, each adjusted annually [^31].

For a surgery center or a home care agency, a Rhode Island file written after June 12, 2026 no longer budgets the time or cost of a certificate; a nursing facility or a hospice still does, and its schedule starts from the application.

The Department of Health licenses assisted living in two layers. The fire safety class is F1 where residents cannot evacuate themselves and F2 where they can; the medication class is M1 where the residence gives medications and M2 where it assists residents who take their own [^32]. A residence that advertises dementia care or separates residents with dementia needs a dementia care license and must be F1-M1, and a Limited Health Services endorsement requires F1 [^32].

The state's wage floor is $16.00 per hour for 2026, up from $15, and becomes $17.00 on January 1, 2027 [^33]. A project that opens in 2027 carries the second step in its first operating year, and a staffing budget built on 2026 rates is a year out of date by then.

The Rhode Island FAIR Plan is the Rhode Island Joint Reinsurance Association, which the General Assembly created in 1969 and in which all basic property insurers participate; it writes commercial property [^34]. Its rates are filed by the association and approved by the Insurance Commissioner, and they must be self-supporting [^35]. In testimony on February 11, 2026 the association told a House committee that the state's property insurance market was in improving health [^34].

For commercial cover, neither an approved rate change nor a carrier's exit was found for 2025 or 2026, so a commercial project near the water is priced from a quote for its own parcel and its own flood exposure.
