The warning came first. On May 30, 2025 Central Iowa Water Works asked its customers to cut lawn watering by 25% of their own accord, saying its treatment facilities were working near capacity [^8]. The request became an order less than two weeks later: from June 12 lawn watering was banned across the utility's area, after 55 days of nitrate removal, and on June 16 the Des Moines River carried 15.6 mg/L of nitrate and the Raccoon River 15.3 mg/L, against the EPA's limit of 10 mg/L [^1]. The restrictions ended in early August [^3].

For a lender the summer matters less than the arrangement it exposed. Each of the utility's 12 member entities has an agreed capacity limit, and a member that exceeds its limit repeatedly is exposed to the cost of added treatment facilities; once the ban lifted, member communities began reviewing their water rates [^3]. While the ban was in force the utility announced a long-range plan to raise treatment capacity by 25%, with the stated aim of preventing future watering bans [^9]. The research holds no date by which that capacity would be in service, so a project financed in the meantime draws on the limits as they stand.

The member structure is what makes the record specific to a site. A parcel in a member community is served through that member [^3], so the question a study puts is about that member's headroom, not the regional plant's.

Water in greater Des Moines is therefore a quantity a member community has to find inside its own allocation. A study of a heavy user in one of those communities, an express car wash, a hotel with an on-site laundry or a food plant, asks the member utility in writing what share of its limit the project would take and how near its summer peaks have come to that limit. The 2025 order also shows how fast a supplier moved from a request to a ban, which is the scenario an irrigation-dependent use, a nursery or a sports complex, tests in its operating case.

The data-center builders met the same question in Cedar Rapids, on another system. QTS's campus there uses waterless cooling, and Google, the utility and the state geologist indicated that supply was adequate for Google's campus, which was expected to rank among the city's largest water users [^10]. A smaller project that would join the same municipal system near those campuses reads the city's own statement of its capacity rather than the Des Moines record, which describes a different utility.

Two other capacity records a credit officer might expect were not in the research for any Iowa market: a sewer moratorium and a published queue of large electric loads. Neither appears on this page as a constraint, and neither is assumed away; the serving utility confirms each for the parcel.

Each of the eight markets below is a section of this page, and each names the counties its figures cover. What sets them apart is what employs them: insurers, banks and a grocer in Des Moines, data-center construction and aerospace in Cedar Rapids, a university health system in Iowa City, tractor and meat plants in the Cedar Valley and Sioux City, and Iowa State University in Ames. Three of them sit on a state line, and for those the section stops at the Iowa county: Scott County for the Quad Cities, Woodbury County for Sioux City, and for Council Bluffs the Iowa counties of the Omaha metro, with the register's figure for Pottawattamie County alone. The Quad Cities section links the Illinois page, and the Council Bluffs section the Nebraska page. The table that closes each section carries one federal series for its county, from the Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey or the 2024 county income accounts of the Bureau of Economic Analysis [^11,12].

QTS announced on August 8, 2025 a campus of seven buildings on 612 acres in Cedar Rapids, a planned investment of $10 billion, and broke ground that month [^18]. Google's Iowa build-out of $7 billion, covering Linn and Pottawattamie counties, includes a Cedar Rapids campus, and in October 2025 the company was reported to be looking at up to six more data centers near the Duane Arnold nuclear site in unincorporated Linn County [^19].

Linn County then wrote its terms. Its board approved a data center ordinance on February 18, 2026, and on July 1, 2026, by a 2-1 vote as The Gazette reported, an 18-month moratorium on accepting new applications to rezone property into the EU-3 large-scale data center zoning district in unincorporated Linn County [^20]. The pause is narrow by its own wording: it stops applications for one zoning district outside the cities, so a warehouse, a hotel or a supplier's plant in the county is checked against the ordinance text for its own parcel rather than assumed to be held up.

Manufacturing adds a second dated project. Sub-Zero Group's expansion in Cedar Rapids, $196 million with 312 jobs, was approved for state credits in May 2026 [^21]. The established anchor is Collins Aerospace, which a trade magazine called the region's largest employer in April 2026, at about 7,000 jobs [^22]. A study that sizes demand from construction crews at the campuses treats that demand as temporary, and one that sizes it from Collins confirms the company's headcount at the date of the file.

SBA serves Iowa from a single district office in Des Moines; its Cedar Rapids branch office is listed as indefinitely closed, and the research found no closure date [^26]. In fiscal 2024 the district approved 421 loans under 7(a) for $146,022,000 and 55 loans under 504 for $49,907,000, $195.9 million across the two programs, figures the Corridor Business Journal reported from the district office on December 19, 2024 [^34]. The monthly releases that followed survive only in part in the research; the latest it holds, for August 2025, reported 19 loans for $6,650,500 [^35].

Counted from the agency's FOIA loan file, approvals from fiscal 2016 through fiscal 2025, net of cancellations, come to 3,388 7(a) loans for Iowa borrowers [^36]. The district's fiscal 2025 totals and its lender and CDC rankings sit in SBA's year-end activity workbooks, which the research could not read filtered to Iowa, and the third-party rankings it did find disagree between versions of the same web page, so no lender is ranked here. Iowa Business Growth Company, based in Johnston, is the statewide 504 development company the research finds cited most often, though no fiscal 2025 volume for it was verified.

The rule a lender cites when it asks for a study is 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^37]. The rule is read clause by clause in the site's research note on it, while the equity, coverage and term tests of the program are explained on the practice page for 7(a) and 504 lending.

State credit and bonds

Iowa's share of the second State Small Business Credit Initiative was $96.10 million, approved in October 2022 and split between two administrators, the Iowa Economic Development Authority and BioConnect Iowa, across four programs: one loan participation, one collateral support and two venture capital [^6]. For a borrower short of collateral, the collateral support program is the one an Iowa lender may raise; its terms are on a current page the research does not date, so they are not repeated here.

Larger private projects can borrow through the Iowa Finance Authority's conduit private activity bonds, under the state ceiling the authority allocates and for which it posted a 2026 volume cap allocation [^38]. The state's newest incentive is the Business Incentives for Growth tax credit, first awarded by the Iowa Economic Development Authority's board on February 20, 2026 [^17]; the Des Moines, Cedar Rapids and Quad Cities sections name the projects awarded there, and outside the eight markets Cambrex's plant in Charles City drew $10,132,500 of credits in May 2026 [^21].

USDA Rural Development's Iowa obligations, a HUD multifamily office for the state and any Iowa EB-5 project did not appear in the research. A project on one of those channels starts from the USDA Rural Development, conventional and institutional or EB-5 practice pages and adds the Iowa county's own record.

Iowa sets no quota on liquor licenses, and its Class C retail fee scales with the population of the city that issues it, as does the fee for the Special Class C beer and wine license [^39]. The research found no record of a market in which licenses change hands, so a restaurant study budgets the state fee for its city's size and not a purchase price.

Cities cannot regulate short-term rentals. Iowa Code section 414.1(1)(e), from HF 2641 of 2020, bars any regulation, restriction or ordinance relating to them, a conditional use permit requirement included, and bars license fees aimed at them; it also requires that they be classed as residential property for zoning [^40]. A 2024 bill, SF 2123, would have limited that preemption to cities over 75,000 people; its enactment was not confirmed [^40]. A hotel study in any of the eight markets therefore counts short-term rentals as supply that no city ordinance can cap.

The state's petroleum tank fund was repealed in 2024, and tank owners now carry private financial responsibility for the life of the system [^41]. The Department of Natural Resources' fees, updated in May 2025, are $10 a tank to register and $65 a year for each tank of 1,100 gallons or more [^42]. A fuel station acquisition study therefore obtains the seller's current insurance or other proof of responsibility, because the state fund that once stood behind a release is gone.

Health facility review moved in 2025. HF 972, signed on May 28, 2025, abolished the State Health Facilities Council and moved Certificate of Need to the Department of Health and Human Services from July 1, 2025 [^43]. Birth centers left the program the same day under HF 887, and the window in which a nursing facility needed a moratorium waiver before applying closed on June 30, 2026 [^44]. A nursing facility filing made after that date falls outside the window, and its study confirms with the department which conditions now apply before it sets a schedule. The program reaches a new or changed institutional health service, and psychiatric medical institutions for children have been exempt since July 1, 2024 [^44]. A study of such a service dates its review path to the department's process, not the abolished council's.

Assisted living is certified by the state, program by program, under Iowa Code chapter 231C, and each certification runs for two years. A dementia-specific program under section 69.1 of 481 IAC is, among other tests, one that serves fewer than 55 tenants and has five or more tenants with dementia at stages 4 to 7 of the Global Deterioration Scale, and its staff need eight hours of dementia training within 30 days [^45]. Those thresholds set the staffing line of an assisted living and memory care pro forma before any rent is assumed.

Child care centers are licensed by the same department under 441 IAC chapter 109, with ratios of 1:4 for children from two weeks to two years and 1:7 for two-year-olds, and staff counted in ratio must be at least 16 [^46]; SF 2268 raised the ratio for three-year-olds to 1:10 in 2022 [^47]. Those ratios set the staffing cost of each classroom in a childcare pro forma before tuition is priced. The department's January 2024 count was 1,496 licensed centers and 2,034 registered child development homes, provider counts rather than slots [^48].

The main cities of all eight markets levy a local hotel and motel tax of 7% on top of the state's 5%, according to a Department of Revenue rate table dated January 1, 2007, the newest version the research could open [^49]. The department's current rate file was not readable, so the rate a lodging study carries for its city is checked with the department before the revenue line is set.

The state sales tax carries a local option on top, local option sales tax does not reach room rentals, and a separate excise applies to construction equipment sold or used in Iowa under Iowa Code 423D.1 [^50,51]. The excise belongs in the hard-cost review of any budget that buys construction equipment for the build. How sales tax treats materials a contractor buys was not confirmed in the research, so the contractor's bid states it.

Iowa's minimum wage is $7.25 an hour, unchanged since January 1, 2008, with no increase scheduled and local minimum wages preempted since 2017 [^52]. The tipped minimum is $4.35 an hour in a 2026 wage guide [^53]. The floor is the same in every county, so the wage a service study prices is the one its competitors pay to hold staff in that market, which the study surveys.

Iowa's emergency management agency names severe weather as the state's lead hazard: heavy rain and flooding, tornadoes and high winds, ice storms, blizzards and heavy snow [^54].

The two recent major disaster declarations were narrow ones. The first, declared on May 21, 2025 for the winter storm of March 19, granted Public Assistance to Crawford, Harrison, Monona and Woodbury counties [^29]. The second, declared on September 1, 2026 for the storms and flooding of July 1 to 4, granted it to Jasper, Polk, Story and Warren counties, and private nonprofits there had until November 2, 2026 to apply to SBA for physical loss loans [^32]. Both were limited to Public Assistance as the research records them. Three of the four counties in the 2026 declaration, Jasper, Polk and Warren, are in the Des Moines metro and the fourth is Ames's county; the 2025 list's Woodbury is Sioux City's county and its Harrison one of the Omaha metro's Iowa counties. The agency also lists hazardous material spills among the state's hazards [^54].

A declaration names counties, not parcels. For a site in one of those counties the seller's damage and repair record for the dated event goes into the file, since a county list says nothing about a given lot.

Travel Iowa's May 2025 report of STR data groups ten counties into an Iowa Urban market: Polk, Johnson, Scott, Dubuque, Linn, Story, Pottawattamie, Woodbury, Black Hawk and Dallas [^4]. In May 2025 that market's hotels ran at 58.4% occupancy on an average daily rate of $118.19, for revenue per available room of $69.04. For the year to May the average rate was $108.59 and revenue per available room $54.28, across 37,763 rooms in 416 properties [^4]. May's occupancy of 58.4% and rate of $118.19 both stood above the year-to-date occupancy of 50.0% and rate of $108.59 [^4], so a pro forma built on a single spring month runs ahead of the year-to-date record.

Beyond hotels and the Des Moines apartment pipeline, the research found no dated vacancy, rent or pipeline series in any Iowa market for fuel stations, RV parks, senior housing, retail space, industrial buildings or self-storage.

The hotel series is itself a blend, so a hotel study in Ames or Sioux City sets it beside its own competitive set's STR report and reads the gap between the two as the market's own position.
