{{case:glance}}

Opelika's stretch of I-85 carries uneven traffic, and the large-format chains have gone where it is heaviest, between Exits 62 and 60 [^3]. A single branded store on this corridor sells to two streams of customers: drivers leaving the interstate and Opelika residents commuting along the parkways that meet it. The city those commuters live in grew 15.3% between April 2020 and July 2025, well ahead of Lee County's 8.9% [^1,2]. That growth is the strongest demand fact in the file, and it explains why an established single store on the corridor still trades.

What the corridor does not offer is room to take share. At Exit 64, three branded stations sit within 0.21 mile of the ramps, two of them open 24 hours [^7,8,42]. At Exit 62, a RaceTrac on a 6.35-acre lot with 15 semi-truck spaces and a QuikTrip that opened in late 2025 now hold the large-format, food-and-fuel end of the market [^9,11,12]. A QuikTrip travel center with six diesel bays, announced for a 2022 opening, now operates at Exit 58, and Buc-ee's Auburn, with 120 fueling positions, opened at Exit 50 in April 2023 [^16,18]. A single store with a modest forecourt does not win the price fight against that set. It keeps the customers for whom the nearest pump and a quick stop matter more than a few cents a gallon.

The loan question is therefore narrow. The buyer here pays {{case:v.in_price}} for land, building, fuel equipment and business, a price set at a multiple of the seller's illustrative earnings rather than taken from any offering, and asks for an SBA 7(a) loan with the minimum injection of 10%. Because this is an initial acquisition, SOP 50 10 8.1 measures it on the seller's record, at 1.25x on historical EBITDA. No seller statements are public, so this page runs the test on an illustrative two-year history built from benchmark store economics, labelled as such everywhere it appears. On an illustrative FY2025 EBITDA of {{case:v.hist_ebitda}}, the proposed structure covers its debt service {{case:v.hist_dscr_proposed}}. It fails.

The engine restructures by adding equity until the seller's last year carries the loan at the floor: {{case:v.equity_pct}} down, {{case:v.loan_total}} of debt and {{case:v.hist_dscr}} coverage. That structure passes the rule. The study does not stop there, because the rule looks backward and the market moved. QuikTrip's opening announcement for its Exit 62 store came on November 21, 2025, so the seller's FY2025 contains at most a few weeks of that competitor [^12]. Our projection of the buyer's first year carries a full year of it and covers the restructured loan {{case:v.dscr_y1}}: above SBA's general 7(a) standard of 1.15x, below the acquisition floor.

The conclusion: the market supports an operating store on the corridor, and it supports this purchase at the restructured equity, provided 2026 interim statements show the store held its volume after the QuikTrip opened. If they show a decline beyond the one projected here, the price should fall rather than the equity rise again. This is the acquisition counterpart to the new-build Cartersville case on I-75, where the test is a start-up projection instead of a seller's history; the two show how differently the program treats an existing store and a new one at an interstate exit. For the format itself, see our page on interstate and highway fuel sites and the broader gas station and convenience store underwriting guide.

The traffic evidence is the weakest part of the demand case and the most important. The only counts with segment detail date from 2010. They show the interstate carrying about 34,670 vehicles a day between Exits 64 and 62, against 47,570 between Exits 62 and 60 [^3]. Exit 64 sits on the lighter stretch, which is consistent with the large-format chains choosing Exit 62 and Exit 58. A 2020 figure of 51,887 vehicles a day for I-85 at Opelika suggests strong growth since 2010, but its source does not say where it was counted [^4]. A current ALDOT count with station identifiers for I-85 at the subject's interchange and for the road the store fronts is a lender condition below, and so is any measure of truck volume, which the research did not find.

The employment base is anchored by a hospital, which is steady around the clock, and by manufacturers in the 200 to 999 employee range [^5]; our Alabama market page sets that base against the state's other metros. Shift workers are good convenience customers. The Baxter closure, 459 jobs in November 2023, is a reminder that a plant can leave quickly [^6]. Seasonal demand from Auburn football weekends and Gulf Coast travel on I-85 is real in kind but was not quantified in any source, so the model carries no seasonal premium.

The station nearest the Exit 64 ramps could not be confirmed and therefore sits outside the table. A Marathon-branded store at 2104 West Point Parkway, about 0.09 mile away and listed in directories as Joe's Grocery, was open 24 hours and posting regular at $3.899 on October 15, 2026 [^7]. Directories disagree on its truck parking and none links to an operator page, so its format and volume are unknown. JK Foodmart, an independent about 2.25 miles west of Exit 64, showed regular at $3.999 on the same check, flagged by iExit as more than a day old [^7]. At Exit 62 and Exit 60, fuel-price directories also list stations at 1100 and 1001 Columbus Parkway (with conflicting 76 and Marathon branding), an Eagle 280 store and a Shopper Stop #37; we found no operator page or county record for any of them. Each of these is a lender condition.

The posted prices that are dated tell a simple story. At Exit 64 on October 15, 2026, the Marathon and the Valero both posted regular at $3.899, ten cents under the Circle K [^7]. A single store anywhere on the corridor has to price with the cheapest station at its interchange or lose the price-led driver, which caps the margin it can take. The operators with the most pricing power, QuikTrip and RaceTrac, do not post prices on their store pages, so a 30-day price survey across both exits is a lender condition rather than a model input.

The pattern of entry matters more than any single store. Every confirmed opening since 2021 has been a large-format chain store at an exit other than 64: RaceTrac after a 2021 conditional use approval and rezoning, QuikTrip's travel center at Exit 58, announced for 2022, Buc-ee's at Exit 50 in 2023 and QuikTrip at Exit 62 in 2025 [^10,16,18,12]. No planned or permitted station at Exit 64 was found, which suggests the chains see it as the weaker interchange; the pipeline at the other interchanges is a lender condition. One city action could change that over time: Opelika awarded a $974,902.42 contract for I-85 sanitary sewer improvements on October 6, 2026, and sewer capacity is what highway commercial sites usually wait for [^22].

The subject is a hypothetical operating branded fuel and convenience store on Opelika's I-85 corridor, inside the city. Its interchange, brand, size and age are not specified. The store profile in the model (gallons, inside sales, trading hours and staffing) is an assumption built from industry benchmarks, not a description of any station on the corridor. For an acquisition, the site questions are less about whether the use can be built and more about whether it can keep operating and transfer cleanly.

Zoning

No zoning district was found for any station parcel on the corridor. The relevant precedent is RaceTrac's Exit 62 store, which needed a Planning Commission conditional use approval on May 25, 2021 and the rezoning of a residential lot to C-3 before its plat was approved on October 26, 2021 [^10]. If a fuel station is conditional in the district that covers the subject, the existing store may be operating under an older approval or as a legal nonconforming use, and either status limits what a buyer can rebuild after a casualty. A zoning verification letter from the City of Opelika is a lender condition. The Planning Commission meets on the fourth Tuesday of each month at 3 p.m. [^25].

The city's package liquor rules adopted on October 6, 2026 set a 1,500-foot separation, a 2,000 square foot minimum and a requirement that package stores sit in centers of three or more tenants with at least 14,000 square feet; they do not apply to beer-and-wine-only sellers [^22]. A single convenience store cannot add a liquor package department, so the beer and wine license is the alcohol revenue the store has.

Utilities and taxes

  • Water: the Water Works Board of the City of Opelika serves the city; service to the specific parcel was not confirmed.
  • Sewer: the City's Wastewater Division operates the system. The sewer access fee rose to $1,700 per equivalent average connection from $1,250 in May 2023 [^24]. An operating store already holds its connection; the fee matters only if the buyer expands.
  • Electric: Opelika Power Services and Alabama Power both serve parts of the city, per the 2021 Planning Commission record [^10]; the provider for the parcel was not confirmed. No natural gas provider was found.
  • Sales tax: 9% combined inside Opelika in 2026 [^23], collected from customers and not a store expense.
  • Property tax: no dated millage table and no tax bill for any station parcel on the corridor were found. The model carries a placeholder of {{case:v.in_tax_rate}} of the real estate allocation, and the tax bill is a lender condition.

Gallons. The model carries {{case:v.in_gallons}} gallons in Year 3, below the 1.0 million gallons Yesway's legacy 3,400 square foot stores averaged in 2025 on 10 dispensers [^28]. The model assumes fewer fueling positions than that format and 15 trading hours a day against 24-hour competitors at every Opelika interchange, and it enters Year 1 with a full year of the new QuikTrip at Exit 62. Growth after Year 1 is slow, in step with national driving: vehicle-miles rose 0.9% in 2025 [^35], and Opelika's population growth adds a little to that.

Fuel margin. The model uses {{case:v.in_fuel_margin}} a gallon. NACS reports that retail fuel margins averaged over 40 cents in 2025 [^30]. The listed chains earned between 35.1 and 52.6 cents in the second quarter of 2026 after a supply shock, and the five-year national averages to September 2026 were 39.3 cents for gasoline and 54.3 cents for diesel [^32]. Forty cents is a mid-cycle number, not a 2026 peak. The store sells mostly gasoline, so the higher diesel average is not applied.

Inside sales and margin. Inside sales stabilize near {{case:v.in_inside_sales}} in Year 3, below Yesway's $1.7 million legacy-store average [^28]. The store has no food program; across the industry, foodservice was 28.5% of in-store sales and 38.9% of in-store gross profit in 2025 [^29], so a buyer who adds a food counter has real upside. None of it is in the model, because it would need capital and a permit the case does not budget. The inside margin of {{case:v.in_inside_margin}} is Yesway's fiscal 2025 merchandise margin [^28].

Two things are left out on purpose. Lottery commissions and ATM fees are small and were not evidenced for any Opelika store. Leaving them out understates revenue slightly; the sensitivity table shows that the two drivers that move coverage are gallons and margin, not these.

{{case:uses}} {{case:sources}}

The allocation of the price drives three program rules at once. The model places {{case:v.in_re_alloc}} of the {{case:v.in_price}} price on real estate (land, building, canopy and tanks) and the rest on business assets, dispensers, fixtures and goodwill. No published norm for that split was found; for a special purpose property, the going-concern appraisal sets it.

  • Business Purchase Price. SBA defines it as the purchase-agreement price less the appraised value of owner-occupied commercial real estate. On the case allocation it is above $350,000, the level at or below which the lender may value the business itself, so an independent business valuation is needed.
  • Quality of Earnings. The QoE trigger is a Business Purchase Price of $3 million, far above this deal, and the SOP exempts Owner Buyout, ESOP & Cooperative, and any acquisition of an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property in any case. The diligence burden is the valuation, the appraisal and the seller's tax returns.
  • Buyer cash that counts. The SOP counts the out-of-pocket cost of the business valuation and QoE as equity, so part of the closing-cost line the buyer pays early is injection, not an extra cost.

The other uses are modest: {{case:v.in_inventory}} of fuel and merchandise inventory counted at closing, a {{case:v.in_working_capital}} reserve and {{case:v.in_closing}} of closing costs. The SBA upfront guaranty fee is financed. Casey's has said an acquired store costs it about $1 million less on average than a new build with the same return requirement [^33]. For an independent buyer the same logic holds at smaller scale: buying an operating store avoids construction risk, and the price is what has to be underwritten instead. Sellers like this one are the norm, not the exception: at the end of 2025, 95,672 of the country's 151,975 convenience stores, 63%, belonged to companies with ten or fewer stores [^34].

{{case:loan}}

A change of ownership loan under SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, effective October 1, 2026, amortizes the business portion over no more than 10 years. The real estate portion may run to 25 years as a separate piece or a blended loan. That split is why the structure above has two tranches with very different debt service per dollar: the business portion costs far more each year than the real estate portion.

Special purpose status offers a way around the split that this case cannot use. When 85% or more of the use of total project cost is real estate and the owner-occupied special purpose property is integral to the business, the whole loan may amortize over 25 years. Gas stations are on SBA's special purpose list. Here real estate is well under that share of project cost, because the price carries goodwill and the project carries inventory and working capital. A buyer who negotiated a price closer to appraised real estate value would pay less and gain the longer term at the same time.

The case prices the loan at its ceiling, Prime of 7.00% from the H.15 release plus SBA's maximum spread, or {{case:v.rate}} [^40]. Alabama's 7(a) loans averaged 10.4% across 626 loans and $352.7 million in calendar 2025 [^27], so the model is not pricing below the market. With a loan above $150,000, SBA guarantees 75% [^37], and the bank rolls the FY2027 upfront guaranty fee into the project [^39].

Appendix 15 also caps the debt: total acquisition debt, including seller debt not on full standby, is capped at the business valuation; any excess must be equity [^38]. The restructured loan of {{case:v.loan_total}} is larger than the {{case:v.in_price}} price, because it also funds the inventory, the working capital reserve, closing costs and the guaranty fee. This case therefore carries the test as a stated condition: the going-concern appraisal and the business valuation must support total debt of at least {{case:v.loan_total}}, and any shortfall becomes buyer equity before closing.

Three ownership and injection rules bind the buyer. The injection is 10% of total project cost and cannot be reduced or eliminated. A seller note on full standby, other full-standby debt and minority investors together may supply no more than half of the required injection. And the buyer's ownership must pass the March 1, 2026 rule: direct and indirect owners and guarantors who are U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals residing in the country, with no foreign share [^36]. Fuel supply agreements also need attention: SOP 50 10 8.1 treats dealer, jobber and supply relationships covered by the Petroleum Marketing Practices Act as franchises [^38], so the branded supply contract, its transfer terms and any image requirements are part of eligibility review, a point developed on our branded fuel section of our gas station guide.

Lee County has a deep bench of SBA lenders: 526 7(a) and 504 loans totalling $239,450,372 since FY1991, eight of them to gasoline stations with convenience stores, with PNC Bank (90 loans) and AuburnBank (17) among the most active [^26]. The same data show a 23.6% charge-off rate on the county's FY1991 to FY2015 vintages, 55 of 233 loans [^26]. Our 7(a) and 504 lending practice explains how lenders weigh county loss history against a single deal.

{{case:dscr}} {{case:determination}} {{case:verdict}}

Appendix 15 sets the rule plainly. For an initial acquisition the floor is 1.25x, measured as historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two. And projections are reviewed but cannot meet the floor, except an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports, where the floor may be met on projections within two years.

As proposed. The illustrative seller earned {{case:v.in_seller_ebitda_fy2024}} in FY2024 and {{case:v.in_seller_ebitda_fy2025}} in FY2025. Both years are invented for this example from benchmark economics, and the page says so wherever they appear. The model tests the last year, the lower of the two, because the store's market has been moving against it. On that basis the proposed {{case:v.loan_total_proposed}} loan, with {{case:v.ds_proposed}} of annual debt service, is covered {{case:v.hist_dscr_proposed}}. The proposal fails. The two-year average is higher and would clear the floor at this debt by a small margin, so the choice of basis decides the request. Appendix 15 allows either; for a store whose earnings are falling toward a new competitor's opening, the later, lower year is the better guide, and a lender that accepts the average is lending on a year the market has already left behind.

As restructured. At {{case:v.equity_pct}} equity ({{case:v.equity}}), the loan falls to {{case:v.loan_total}} and debt service to {{case:v.ds}}, which the FY2025 figure covers {{case:v.hist_dscr}}. The structure meets the equity rule, the size limit and the coverage floor. The extra cash buys nothing but compliance: the store is the same store.

What the projections add. The special purpose exception would let projections carry the loan if the appraised value fully supports the price. It does not help here for two reasons. The price includes goodwill that real estate value alone does not support unless the going-concern appraisal says otherwise, and the projections themselves sit below the floor: the restructured loan is covered {{case:v.dscr_y1}} in Year 1 and {{case:v.dscr_y3}} in Year 3. The projection is the study's measure of the store after the new QuikTrip; the history is the program's measure before it. When the two disagree, the lender should trust neither until 2026 interim statements arrive. If those statements run near the projection, the honest response is a lower price, not a third round of equity.

The research note on what SOP 50 10 8.1 changed for feasibility studies explains why the acquisition floor rose under the new SOP and why projections lost their standing in these deals.

{{case:breakeven}}

On the restructured loan, Year 3 NOI equals debt service when gallons fall to {{case:v.breakeven_value}}, a cushion of {{case:v.cushion_pct}} below plan. That is a reasonable margin for an operating store with a long record. The last line is the warning: to cover the debt at the acquisition floor in Year 3, the store would need {{case:v.breakeven_floor_value}} gallons, more than the plan. On its projections the store lives between the general 7(a) standard and the acquisition floor, which is why the 2026 statements carry so much weight in the conditions below.

A small store's break-even is more sensitive to gallons than its size suggests, because every lost gallon also loses a share of the inside trip. The model holds inside sales to their own path; a buyer who loses fuel customers to the QuikTrip will lose some of their inside purchases too, so the true cushion is somewhat thinner than the table shows.

{{case:sensitivity}}

Coverage in Year 3 ranges from {{case:v.sens_min}} to {{case:v.sens_max}} across the grid. The two drivers are chosen because they are where a single store on this corridor is exposed. Gallons carry the competitive risk: the large formats at Exits 62 and 58 compete on price and food, and every driver they win is a fuel sale lost. Margin carries the cycle risk: second-quarter 2026 margins of 35.1 to 52.6 cents at listed operators reflect a supply shock, not a steady state [^32], and same-store gallons fell at three of five listed chains in the same quarter [^32].

The useful reading is the diagonal. A store that gives up a tenth of its gallons and a tenth of its margin at the same time, which is what a price war at the next exit looks like, slips just below the point where NOI covers debt service on the restructured loan. A store that holds volume and earns a stronger margin than 40 cents clears the floor comfortably. The lender's real exposure is a combination of lost volume and a return to the low end of the margin range, and that is why the margin assumption is set at mid-cycle rather than at 2026's peak.
