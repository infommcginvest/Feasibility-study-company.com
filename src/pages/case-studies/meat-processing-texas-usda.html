{{case:glance}}

Elgin, on US 290 at the eastern edge of the Austin metro, is the strongest place in the six-county area for a new USDA-inspected plant. The Texas Legislature named it the Sausage Capital of Texas, and its smoked-meat trade goes back to 1882 [^28]. Three federally inspected plants and one state-inspected plant already operate in town [^10,11,13,16]. The city's I General Industrial district permits food products processing and packing [^5], though the text the research found does not name livestock slaughter. And the population is growing: Elgin had 13,327 residents in July 2025, up 37.4% from 9,700 in April 2020 [^1].

The supply side supports a beef plant. Texas held 12.1 million head of cattle on January 1, 2026, the same as a year earlier, while the national herd of 86.2 million was at a 75-year low [^26]. Capacity at the big packers is shrinking: Tyson closed its Lexington, Nebraska beef plant on January 20, 2026 and laid off 3,200 workers [^58]. Small producers who sell beef direct need inspected processing, and Texas cannot ship state-inspected product across state lines [^27].

What the market cannot show is price. The six-county trade area holds 21 registered processors, from four federally inspected plants to small custom-exempt shops [^10], and none of them posts a processing price sheet. The research found no kill fee, no cut-and-wrap rate, no yield and no capacity figure for a small Texas plant. So the revenue build in this case is an assumption from top to bottom, labelled as such, and the first lender conditions are a fee survey and signed throughput commitments.

On those assumptions the case is close. A hypothetical {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} sq ft plant harvesting up to {{case:v.in_head_day}} head of beef a day, with a sausage and smoked-meat room, costs {{case:v.tpc}}. At the sponsor's {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} equity, Year 3 income covers debt service {{case:v.dscr_y3_proposed}}, short of the {{case:v.floor}} floor this case's lender uses. Restructured to {{case:v.equity_pct}} equity, the {{case:v.loan_total}} guaranteed loan covers {{case:v.dscr_y3}} in Year 3. The plant loses money in Year 1 and does not cover debt service in Year 2, so a funded reserve is part of the structure, and the break-even sits only {{case:v.cushion_pct}} below the underwritten utilization. That last number is the real finding: a small meat plant's lender is underwriting throughput, and throughput must be contracted before closing.

For a processing plant, the trade area is a supply and customer catchment, not a retail radius. This case uses Bastrop County and the five counties around it: Travis, Williamson, Lee, Fayette and Caldwell. Live animals come from the ranches in those counties; customers include the Elgin sausage makers, direct-market producers, restaurants and the Austin metro's retail buyers. No drive-time demographic run was available, so the figures below are for Elgin and the counties.

Three kinds of demand matter. The first is the local processing trade. Meyer's says wholesale "remains the largest part of the operation" and that it has shipped nationwide since it gained federal inspection in 2002 [^12]. Southside Market makes fresh sausage in Elgin daily for its restaurants and wholesale customers [^14] and sells wholesale in 50 lb and 200 lb increments [^15]. A federally inspected co-packer can sell capacity to that trade when the established plants are full or when smaller brands need an inspected kitchen. The 39th annual Hogeye Festival on the fourth Saturday of October [^29] is a reminder that the town's identity is built on the product.

The second is direct-market beef. Small ranchers who sell by the quarter or the box need a slaughter and fabrication slot, and when capacity is short they wait. In North Carolina, the typical wait for a processing appointment went from about 30 days before COVID to 210 days in 2021 [^56]; no Texas wait-time figure was found, and a local slot survey is a lender condition. Federally inspected cattle slaughter in January to May 2026 ran at 91% of the same months in 2025 [^53], which says the big plants are processing fewer cattle, not that local demand has fallen.

The third is population. Bastrop County had about 118,900 residents in 2025 [^2] and added about 2,970 in a year, a 2.6% gain [^3]. Neighboring Caldwell County grew 4.18% and Hays and Williamson more than 3% each, while Texas added 391,243 people between July 2024 and July 2025 [^4]. The register reads very differently once activity is separated from count. Most of the 21 only process meat; they buy boxed product or carcasses and cut, grind, cure or cook it. Beef harvest under inspection is rare. Only Weimar Meat Company holds a federal grant that includes meat slaughter, and only Smithville Food Lockers, H. W. Doyle and Willie Joe's harvest livestock under state inspection; the rest of the slaughter capacity is custom-exempt, which can serve an animal's owner but cannot produce meat for sale. Pasture Raised Foods slaughters only its own poultry. For a rancher who wants to sell federally inspected beef in Austin or beyond, the choice in six counties is narrow.

That narrowness is the opportunity, and it cuts both ways. A plant that offers inspected beef harvest and fabrication fills a gap the register shows. But the established Elgin plants are processors with their own supply chains, not obvious harvest customers, and the small state-inspected harvest plants compete on price for local ranchers. The research could not establish any competitor's fees, capacity or waiting list. Three state plants and one federal plant that already harvest beef will respond to a newcomer, and the case cannot say how.

The wider Texas market is adding capacity at both ends. Producer Owned Beef received a $253 million building permit in Amarillo on August 8, 2025 for a plant planned at 3,000 head a day and 1,600 employees at peak [^20], part of a $670 million project on 1,108 acres [^21]. Amarillo's economic development corporation gave the project 610 acres and $11.1 million of incentives, and the Texas Enterprise Fund added a $12.2 million grant [^21]: the state's new large plants arrive subsidized, and a small independent plant competes with them for cattle without that help. Standard Meat's fifth plant in Fort Worth received its federal grant on February 12, 2026 as a large establishment [^22], reported at 182,000 sq ft [^23]. At the small end, East Texas Slaughter and Packing in Laneville received a very small FSIS grant on July 18, 2025 [^24]. Texas gained nine federally inspected slaughter plants in 2025, more than any other state [^54]. None of these is in the trade area, and no new plant was found in the six counties in 2025 or 2026.

Zoning

Elgin's I General Industrial district is the natural home for the plant. The district text in a 2020 city packet permits "food products processing and packing" and the processing or packaging of food products in an enclosed building, provided no noxious odor, fumes or dust leave the property [^5]. It does not name livestock slaughter, and the research found only the proposed text, not the final adopted version. The city has been moving parcels in and out of industrial zoning: a February 4, 2025 hearing rezoned two parcels from C-3 to I and one from I to C-2 [^6]. Utilities, flood and environment

Water, sewer, electric and gas service at the lot, and the providers, are conditions to confirm; CenterPoint Energy's gas transportation rate schedule appeared on a City Manager report in February 2025 [^6], so gas is present in the city. {{case:loan}}

A B&I loan is made by a commercial lender and guaranteed by USDA Rural Development. Under the FY2026 notice, published March 9, 2026 and effective for the fiscal year that began October 1, 2025, the guarantee is 85% for loans under $5 million and 80% for loans of $5 million to $25 million, with a 3.0% initial fee, an added 0.50% when the loan note guarantee issues before construction is complete, and a 0.55% annual retention fee [^38]. A plant guaranteed at closing and then built pays both parts of the up-front fee. USDA announced the 85% tier on March 27, 2026 for loans obligated in FY2026 [^37]. This loan is above $5 million, so the case uses the 80% guarantee.

Those are FY2026 terms, and FY2026 is over. The research found no FY2027 fee notice, no FY2027 guarantee percentage and no enacted FY2027 program level; the FY2027 budget request proposed a B&I program level of zero [^47]. A lender working this deal now must confirm what USDA will charge and guarantee when it obligates the loan. That is the first program condition, and it could change the fee, the guarantee share and whether funding is available at all.

The rate is negotiated between lender and borrower, fixed, variable or a mix [^43]; the research has no rate, so the model uses {{case:v.in_bi_rate}}, an assumption. The term may run to the useful life of the collateral, up to 40 years [^43]; the model amortizes over {{case:v.in_bi_amort_years}} because process equipment wears out long before the building. USDA's equity rule, as amended in December 2025, reads as 25% of project cost for a new business whose guarantee issues before construction is complete [^68], so the model tests {{case:v.in_bi_equity_min_pct}}; the research has no B&I coverage rule, so the {{case:v.floor}} floor is this case's lender's own assumption. The program caps a loan at $25 million [^38].

The feasibility study is not optional here. A B&I guaranteed loan of more than $1,000,000 to a new business needs a study by an independent qualified consultant acceptable to the Agency [^44], covering economic, market, technical, financial and management feasibility [^45]. The lender must evaluate that study in writing [^46], and the projections must cover at least two years at full capacity or stable operations [^44]. A plant with more than 50 new direct jobs on a loan above $1,000,000 would also go to the Department of Labor [^44]; this plant stays below that count. Our note on the five USDA feasibility components sets out what each component must prove, and the USDA Rural Development practice explains how we prepare them.

Eligibility starts with the site: the project must be in an area outside any city or town of more than 50,000 people [^37]. Elgin's population is far below that, but the USDA eligibility map was not checked for a site and is a condition. A city of under 25,000 earns five priority points [^44]. The program is busy in Texas, which took $289.2 million of B&I obligations in FY2025, 13.7% of the national total [^47], and USDA removed ten lenders in May 2026 whose portfolios held about $620 million of delinquent loans, about 47% of Rural Development's delinquencies [^48]. A lender with a clean B&I record matters. Banks may also receive CRA consideration for B&I loans [^39]. The nearest Texas precedent in the research is a $4,950,000 Food Supply Chain guarantee in February 2023 to refinance the purchase of a custom meat plant in Amarillo [^40].

SBA is the obvious alternative and works differently. A meat plant is a manufacturer, so its 504 debenture could reach $5.5 million rather than $5 million, and FY2027 fees are waived for manufacturers [^65]. SBA does not mandate a study: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^67]. SBA's 90% grocery guarantee, announced in June 2026, does not list meat processing among its eligible industry codes [^61], although a trade report described it as open to food processing [^42].

On the restructured loan, the plant covers its debt service at {{case:v.breakeven_value}} harvest utilization in Year 3, a cushion of only {{case:v.cushion_pct}} below the underwritten level, and meets the floor at {{case:v.breakeven_floor_value}}. {{case:sensitivity}}

The two drivers that matter most are harvest utilization and the fee per head, both unsourced. Across the grid, Year 3 coverage runs from {{case:v.sens_min}} to {{case:v.sens_max}}. A 10% shortfall in utilization at the underwritten fee leaves the plant below debt service, and so does a 7.5% lower fee at the underwritten volume; a fee 7.5% higher clears the floor comfortably. A lender can use the grid to set conditions. If the fee survey supports the underwritten fee and commitments cover most of the Year 3 volume, the restructured loan stands. If either comes in lower, the loan has to shrink or the sponsor has to add equity or reserve.
