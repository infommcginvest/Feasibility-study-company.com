Feasibility Study Company prepares cold storage feasibility studies for owners, developers and their lenders. A cold storage feasibility study consultant or company for an Anchorage, Alaska project would produce four things, and this page shows them for one hypothetical building financed with an SBA 504 loan: a market read, a supply count, a priced build and a coverage test. We can run these steps on your own site.

The subject is generic: an owner-operated refrigerated building of about {{case:v.in_bldg_sf}} square feet on about {{case:v.in_land_acres}} acres in the airport-area industrial corridor, with about {{case:v.in_positions}} pallet positions, a dock, and two refrigerated trucks for deliveries to grocery and food service accounts. It is meant to serve three flows the market talks about: air cargo, seafood and the grocery supply chain. Market figures are dated public records. The case engine produces the model figures from a closing input table whose every line names a source or says assumption. The building belongs with our cold storage practice, the city is in Alaska, and the program's rules are explained on our SBA 7(a) and 504 page.

{{case:glance}}

The location answer is yes. Ted Stevens Anchorage International Airport moved a record 3.82 million tonnes of cargo in 2025 [^8], and the Don Young Port of Alaska moved a record 5.7 million tonnes [^8]. A municipality of 289,221 residents [^1] with 183.4 thousand nonfarm jobs [^3] buys food every week, and it is the supply point for much of the rest of the state.

The volume answer is not available. The research found no dated figure for seafood landings or processing in Southcentral, none for the grocery tonnage that moves through Anchorage warehouses, and none for how much air cargo is stored locally and how much simply changes aircraft. Those three gaps are the center of the demand case, so this page turns each of them into a lender condition and does not paper over them with a number.

The supply answer is odd. The Census Bureau's County Business Patterns series, read through the site's market register, lists no refrigerated warehousing establishment in the Municipality of Anchorage or in the metro area (see the supply table below). That is either an empty market or a disclosure gap, and the page explains why the second reading is the more likely one. The SBA's loan records add a second fact: the program has financed no cold storage building in Alaska in the ten approval years to 2025, against a national count that is well above zero. A lender and a Community Development Corporation here would be pricing a building type they have not seen locally.

The financing answer follows. Under a {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} equity proposal the project costs {{case:v.tpc_proposed}}, the debenture is {{case:v.debenture_proposed}} and Year 2 coverage is {{case:v.dscr_y2_proposed}} against a floor of 1.15x. Restructured, the sponsor brings {{case:v.equity}}, {{case:v.equity_pct}} of cost, and coverage reaches {{case:v.dscr_y2}} in Year 2 and {{case:v.dscr_y3}} at stabilization. A building that earns well once full needs a large contribution to survive the fill.

Cold storage demand follows product that must stay cold between a producer and a plate. Three flows matter in Anchorage: freight that passes through the airport, fish that comes out of Alaska waters, and the food that feeds the people who live and visit here. Air cargo

The cargo record is the headline and also the trap. Two reports give slightly different totals for 2025: 3.82 million tonnes in an Alaska Business account of the Anchorage Economic Development Corporation's 2026 forecast [^8], and more than 3.85 million metric tons in Alaska Public Media's account of an airport system report [^9]. Both are secondary relays, neither opened the underlying report, and they are two accounts of the same 2025 total. The more useful signal is direction. Cargo at the Anchorage and Fairbanks airports together ran 11% ahead of the same period a year earlier in the first half of 2026 [^9], and Anchorage had handled nearly 2 million metric tons by June [^9].

What none of the reports say is how much of that freight is stored in Anchorage. Aircraft that stop here to refuel or change crews carry cargo that never enters a local building. Perishable freight that does stop needs a refrigerated dock and short-term holding, which is a different service from a month of pallet storage. The research holds no share, no perishable tonnage and no handler volume, so the model does not take its occupancy from the cargo total. It takes it from a ramp assumption and asks the lender to see signed agreements first. The transportation sector gives a mild corroboration: the Economic Development Corporation forecast 450 added transportation jobs in 2026 after 550 in 2025 [^8].

Seafood

The research could not retrieve a dated harvest, landing or processing-volume figure for Southcentral Alaska. The state fish and game harvest page returned an error, and the one seafood search in the research was for Kodiak and found nothing dated. The port record cargo of 5.7 million tonnes in 2025 [^8] shows a busy marine gateway, but it covers all cargo and says nothing about frozen fish. A seafood-led storage business would be built on contracts with processors and exporters, and none of those are in the public record this study can use. The model therefore does not rely on seafood volumes. Frozen seafood accounts are one of the customer types a lender should see under contract.

The grocery supply chain and the people it feeds

The consumer base is stable rather than growing. The municipality had 289,221 residents on July 1, 2025 [^1], inside a state of 738,737 [^12]. The state added 1,649 people (0.2%) between 2024 and 2025: natural increase of 3,389 outweighed a net loss of 1,740 people to other states [^12]. Population is therefore flat, and a new warehouse cannot count on growth to fill it. It has to win share from existing arrangements.

Household spending power looks mixed. Anchorage covered employment stood at 145,080 in March 2026, down 1.6% from a year earlier as printed, while the average weekly wage was $1,579, up 5.3% over the year [^4]. The county held 8,754 establishments in the first quarter of 2026 [^4]. Prices rose 3.3% over twelve months to August 2026 on an index of 282.910 [^6]. A local consumer confidence reading was 40.5 out of 100 in July 2026 [^7]. Every Alaska resident who applied could expect a 2026 dividend of $1,200, a $1,000 base plus $200 of energy relief, and nearly 600,000 people applied [^18], which is a seasonal injection of cash into grocery spending. The military is a further buyer: the Economic Development Corporation expects about $2 billion of near-term investment at Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson and about 2,700 new personnel and families [^7].

Visitors add a second, seasonal food market. Municipal tourism tax collections passed $57.5 million in 2025 [^10], and Southcentral cruise capacity of 492,000 in 2025 was projected to rise to 526,000 in 2026 [^10]. Hotels, restaurants and cruise provisioners buy chilled and frozen food in the same months. A tourism-heavy customer mix, however, concentrates a building's occupancy in a few months, and the model's annual occupancy averages over that swing.

Housing and tourism add texture. The municipality reported about 400 new dwelling units permitted in 2025 and 330 in the first half of 2026, and it puts the housing need tied to JBER at 2,500 homes over five years [^11]. The average Anchorage home price was $481,000 in 2026 [^7]. Statewide, a record tourism season brought 33,000 more visitors (1.1%), yet winter visitors numbered fewer than 400,000, down 5.5% [^22]. Winter is the season in which a building depends on residents and not on visitors.

Headwinds

The labor market is soft. Anchorage nonfarm employment of 183.4 thousand in September 2026 was 1.0% below a year earlier, and the civilian labor force of 200.5 thousand sat below the 204.0 thousand of April [^3]. The municipality's unemployment rate was 3.4% by the state's measure and 3.6% by the federal one in September, preliminary and from different vintages [^2,3], against 4.3% for the state as a whole [^13]. Statewide, the federal series shows seasonally adjusted nonfarm employment of 340.7 thousand, up 1.2% over twelve months [^14], while the state's own count, not adjusted, rose by only 300 (0.1%), with federal employment down 1,300 and private employment up 2,200 [^13]. Anchorage federal employment fell 8.4% in the first half of 2026, against a statewide decline of 11.7% [^7]. One bright spot is that the same state release reports transportation, warehousing and utilities up 900 jobs [^13]. Anchorage nonfarm employment was 185.3 thousand in August and the unemployment rate 3.1% [^3]. Private employers added 30,293 jobs and lost 23,914 in the last quarter of 2025, a net gain of 6,379, after a net loss of 6,477 in the third quarter [^15], so the state economy churns even when the headline is flat.

The state economy produced $73,634.8 million of output in 2025, up from $69,130.8 million in 2024 [^17]. That is the base on which the project's customers operate, and it depends heavily on energy. The pipeline project now under discussion would need a peak workforce of about 6,600 workers for construction alone [^16]. If it proceeds it would lift food demand for construction camps and compete with a cold storage operator for skilled labor and contractors; if it does not, the state loses a source of growth. The study treats it as an upside that a lender should not underwrite.

A lender expects a competitor table of named operators, each tied to its own website or assessor record, and this study cannot build one. No document in the research names a cold storage operator in Anchorage, and nothing here may be fetched to find one. The census therefore becomes a lender condition: a list of named operators, with their own websites or the assessor's records for their sites. The industrial and warehouse class is a proxy: general warehousing and lessors of nonresidential buildings are the nearest industries to industrial property, and no code isolates it. The cold storage class is an exact match to refrigerated warehousing and storage.

How to read the zero

The register counts every industry cell that has an establishment, and it treats a cell that is absent as a true zero. On that rule Anchorage holds no refrigerated warehousing establishment, and neither does the metro area. We do not believe the market is empty. A city that handles a record volume of air cargo and feeds the state's largest population is unlikely to have no refrigerated storage at all, and the Census Bureau withholds establishment cells where publishing them could disclose a single firm's data. The register's own state-level cell for Alaska is flagged for that reason: it carries a lower bound of zero with some establishments possibly withheld. The honest reading is that the published data show none, that withheld cells may hide some, and that private operators, grocery chains' own distribution centers and seafood processors with their own cold rooms may not appear as a separate class at all.

That last point matters most to the subject. Much cold storage in a market like this one sits inside larger operations: processors, grocers and freight handlers that store their own product. Their capacity competes with a third-party building without appearing in a competitor list. A new owner-operated building must win customers who either rent from an incumbent or build their own, and the census a lender requires should name which.

The industrial base and the customers

The proxy count of industrial and warehouse establishments in the table includes ordinary dry warehouses. It shows how small the local logistics stock is, and it suggests that a refrigerated building would be a rare specialty among them. On the customer side, the table counts grocery store establishments for the municipality and the metro area, with the paid employees of the municipality's grocery stores. A modest number of grocery buyers is both good and bad news for a delivery line: it concentrates the sales effort on a few accounts, and it makes one lost account a large share of revenue.

{{case:loan}}

The 504 program funds a project in three layers, and the rules in force from October 1, 2026 (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates) set the split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^23]. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or for limited or special purpose property, and to 20% when the project is both [^23]. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built, and its example list names Amusement parks, Bowling alleys, Car wash businesses, Cemeteries, Cold storage facilities where more than 50% of total square footage is equipped for refrigeration, Dormitories, Farms, including livestock and dairy facilities, Funeral homes with crematoriums, Gas stations, Golf courses, Hospitals, surgery centers, urgent care centers, and other health or medical facilities, Hotels, motels, and other lodging facilities, Marinas, Mines, Nursing homes, including assisted living facilities, Oil wells, Quarries, including gravel pits, Railroads, Sanitary landfills, Service centers (e.g., oil and lube, brake, or transmission centers) with pits and in-ground lifts, Sports arenas, Swimming pools, Tennis clubs, Theaters and auditoriums, Wineries [^23]. {{case:inputs}}

Every input is an assumption. Where a public record bears on one, the reasoning cites it, as the wage inputs do with the Anchorage mean hourly wage and the bank rate does with Prime. The research for this town offers market evidence and program rules but no cold storage unit economics, so assumptions carry the model. Check occupancy first, then the storage charge, then building cost, then the power price.

  1. The anchor customers do not sign

    The occupancy ramp is an assumption and no volume record in the public data reaches this building. If processors, grocers and freight handlers keep storing their own product or renting from an incumbent, the building fills slowly and fails the Year 2 test.

  2. The supply picture is wrong

    The register shows no refrigerated warehousing establishment in the county, but withheld cells and in-house cold rooms may hide competitors. A named census could show a market with more capacity than the data suggest.

  3. Air cargo is a transit market

    The cargo record of 3.82 million tonnes [^8] is a total for the airport and not a measure of stored freight. If most of it never enters a building, the cargo leg of the demand case is thin.

  4. A flat population and a soft labor market

    The state grew by 1,649 people in 2025 [^12], Anchorage jobs were down 1.0% over the year [^3], and federal jobs in the municipality fell 8.4% in the first half of 2026 [^7]. A grocery customer base that is not growing gives a new building no tailwind.

  5. Energy cost and reliability

    Power is the largest cost after payroll, and the model's rate is an assumption. A gas utility warned of a possible 18-day shortfall in the coming winter [^20]. A cold building without backup power risks product loss and insurance disputes.

  6. Construction cost and labor

    No Anchorage cost index was found, and a pipeline project would draw about 6,600 workers at its peak [^16]. If it proceeds, bids for a refrigerated building could run above the assumption, and every dollar of overrun adds debt the building can only just carry.

  7. A first-of-kind loan file

    Alaska recorded no cold storage loan under either SBA program in the ten approval years to 2025. A lender and a CDC without local precedent may ask for more equity, a longer reserve or a different structure than the engine produces.
