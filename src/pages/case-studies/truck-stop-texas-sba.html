{{case:glance}}

Terrell is where Dallas's eastward growth meets the I-20 freight corridor. The city grew 35.5% between April 2020 and July 2025 [^4], Kaufman County was among the ten fastest-growing counties in the country in the year to July 2025 [^5], and the industrial pipeline is concrete rather than announced: Amazon has filed for a distribution center of 1,160,399 square feet due in August 2027, and VanTrust is building two speculative logistics buildings at US 80 and Spur 557 [^28,30]. Every one of those facts points to more trucks on the roads that feed a truck stop at FM 148, although no current TxDOT count for I-20 at the exit was retrieved.

The supply side is thinner than the demand story suggests, and that is the case for a new site. The one full-service travel center in the corridor, TA Terrell #0233, sits at Exit 503 on Wilson Road with 300 truck spaces and five service bays [^8]. At Exit 498 itself, the ONE9 dealer at FM 148 lists five fuel lanes and 12 public parking spaces, and Buc-ee's, the largest fuel retailer at the exit, does not admit 18-wheelers [^9,15]. No Love's, Pilot-branded or Flying J truck stop was verified inside Terrell, and no new truck stop was found in the 2025 or 2026 pipeline. A mid-size independent with five diesel lanes and about 80 truck spaces would fill a real gap at the interchange where trucks leave I-20 for FM 148 and US 80.

The case stops on cost, not demand. Land near a Buc-ee's interchange sits at the top of the corridor's price range; the case assumes the sponsor buys about {{case:v.in_acres_used}} acres near the exit at {{case:v.in_land_per_acre}} an acre, an assumption set inside the land evidence the research holds for Terrell [^3,23], and builds at 2026 prices, for a total project cost of {{case:v.tpc}}. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, a new truck stop is a new business on special purpose property, so the borrower's contribution is 20%. At that contribution the bank loan and the debenture together cost {{case:v.ds}} a year, and the truck stop earns {{case:v.noi_y2}} in Year 2: coverage of {{case:v.dscr_y2}} against a 1.15x standard. More equity does not solve it, because in a 504 project extra cash shrinks the debenture and leaves the bank's first lien, half the project, in place; that lien alone needs more coverage than Year 2 provides.

The determination is that the market supports a truck stop at Exit 498 but does not support this loan. The lever is the land: the I-20 tract at Exit 509 that was marketed for a truck stop sold in March 2026 after listing at $1,300,000 for 16.63 acres [^23], a fraction of the per-acre price assumed here. A sponsor who can secure interchange land near that basis, or a smaller parcel at Exit 498, has a project worth re-testing. For the asset class itself, see the truck stop and travel center guide; for the metro context, the Dallas-Fort Worth market page and the Texas state page.

Two other cases frame this one. The Hankamer travel center case on I-10 finances a comparable asset with a USDA Business and Industry guarantee in a rural county, where the program and the land both work differently. The Cartersville gas station case looks at an interchange where two national travel centers already serve diesel and concludes that a newcomer should stay out of the truck business; Terrell is the opposite case, where the truck customer is underserved and the obstacle is price.

Truck-stop demand comes from the interstate, not from the residents of the town that hosts it, so the primary trade area is the I-20 corridor from Exit 498 to Exit 509, about ten miles, plus the local distribution centers that send trucks onto it. FHWA's national model sizes truck parking demand from truck-hours of travel on a highway segment and assigns 77% of it to commercial truck stops, the rest to public rest areas [^44]; hours-of-service rules, an 11-hour driving limit after 10 hours off and a 30-minute break after 8 hours of driving, decide where drivers stop [^60]. Neither input was available for I-20 at Terrell: the truck share of the corridor's traffic was not found. The demand case therefore rests on generators and growth.

The generators are what make Terrell different from a typical interstate exit. Cross-dock buildings exist to move trailers in and out; the Amazon center and the two VanTrust cross docks will add trucks that start and end their trips within a few miles of Exit 498. The three VanTrust buildings and Amazon's ITX7 alone add millions of square feet of logistics space within a few years [^28,30]. Local trucks buy diesel and park between loads, and many of their drivers want food and showers without driving east to TA at Exit 503.

Two discounts apply. First, the Amazon figures conflict: the state filing shows 1,160,399 square feet, the newspaper roughly 1.2 million, and an earlier filing 933,656 [^27,28,29]. The study uses none of them as a volume input; they establish that a large facility is coming, not how many trucks it sends. Second, speculative buildings fill on the market's schedule, not the developer's. The model therefore phases diesel volume up over three years and reaches its stabilized level only after both projects are due to finish.

For scale on what a destination fuel stop sells, Buc-ee's representatives said in June 2026 that a typical Buc-ee's travel center does $25 million a year in gross sales and sells 18 million gallons [^32]. That store excludes trucks, and its number is a company-wide claim, but it shows how much of the car traffic at Exit 498 is spoken for. Seasonality and events specific to the corridor were not found; the model treats freight demand as steady.

Zoning and entitlement

Terrell's zoning ordinance, as amended by Ordinance No. 3138 on September 30, 2025, lists Use #416, Truck Stop and Fuel Station, with an "S": a Specific Use Permit is required, and the use is permitted by right in no district [^1]. The same chart marks some uses "R", prohibited within 700 feet of the IH 20, US 80 and Spur 557 rights-of-way in the Highway Corridor district [^1]; whether Use #416 carries that mark was not determined, and it matters for any site near the interstate.

Two recent hearing cycles show the timetable. A Specific Use Permit for a wrecker and salvage use went to the Planning and Zoning Commission on September 25, 2025 and to Council on October 7 [^24]. {{case:loan}}

SBA 504 splits a project three ways. The October 1, 2026 rules (SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, in force since October 1, 2026) set the standard split at Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20%. The borrower's contribution starts at 10%, rises to 15% for a new business or a limited or special purpose property, and to 20% for both [^50]. SBA defines a special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built, and gas stations are on its list of examples. A truck stop with tanks, canopies, a scale and a truck court fits the definition, and a business that has not opened is new. The sponsor's proposal of {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} reads the rule as if only one surcharge applied, and it fails the equity test.

At {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} the proposal also fails the size test. The debenture would be {{case:v.debenture_proposed}}, above the $5 million limit for a project that is not a small manufacturer. At {{case:v.equity_pct}} the debenture falls to {{case:v.debenture}}, {{case:v.debenture_position}}, and the bank lends {{case:v.tpl}}, {{case:v.tpl_share}} of cost.

The debenture rate is {{case:v.in_debenture_rate}}, the 25-year effective 504 rate from the October 8, 2026 pricing, which folds the ongoing program fees into the rate [^54]; the actual rate is set when the debenture is sold. The bank rate is an assumption of {{case:v.in_tpl_rate}}, Prime of 7.00% plus 1.25 points for a special purpose start-up [^57], with the 10-year Treasury near 5% in September 2026 [^59]. Both amortize over 25 years. The bank may well ask for a shorter term on a truck stop, which would only raise debt service.

Two more program rules bear on the sponsor. SBA's 504 jobs standard is one job per $95,000 of debenture; the case's staffing of about 26 full-time equivalents falls short of that ratio on a debenture of this size, so the CDC would have to qualify the project under a community development or public policy goal. And under the ownership rule in force since March 1, 2026, the borrower must be wholly owned by U.S. citizens or nationals living in the United States, a test its guarantors share [^37]. How CDCs and banks split the risk on a project like this is set out in our practice note on SBA 7(a) and 504 lending.

As proposed, at {{case:v.equity_proposed_pct}} equity, the project fails all three tests: the contribution is below the tier, the debenture is above the limit, and Year 2 coverage is {{case:v.dscr_y2_proposed}}. The engine then looks for an equity share that would clear the standard and finds none. That is a structural result, not a rounding one. In the 504 split, cash the sponsor adds above the tier reduces the debenture; the bank's first lien stays at half the project. On this project, the bank's annual payment alone is more than Year 2 NOI can cover at the standard, so even a sponsor who paid for the entire debenture portion in cash would not pass. The only paths to a passing structure are a smaller project, a cheaper one, or a store that earns more in its second year than the market evidence supports.

The determination is therefore that the market does not support this loan. That is not the same as saying the market does not support a truck stop. The demand evidence for Exit 498 is strong: a growing city, a growing county, three logistics projects and only one full-service competitor in the corridor. What fails is the basis: interchange land near the top of the corridor's range and a 2026 build cost, financed with half the project in a bank loan that needs coverage from the second year. The lender's answer is to send the sponsor back to the land.
