Burger restaurants are limited-service chains built around the hamburger, and the large systems are mostly franchised, freestanding drive-thru units. The franchisor fixes the menu, the box and the fee load, so what a study has left to prove is the site's sales, the full occupancy cost and the borrower's capacity to carry the debt. This page sits under the quick-service restaurant hub, which covers SBA loan data for limited-service restaurants as a group, drive-thru and grease rules, net lease pricing and the other menu categories; here the subject is the burger brand and what it discloses.

The brand choice settles much of the credit before the site is studied. Trade-press unit volumes for 2025 put McDonald's at $4,088 thousand and Wendy's at $2,001 thousand, with Burger King at $1,660 thousand [^1]. Three other systems sit near or above McDonald's: Culver's at $4,040K, Whataburger at $3,914K and In-N-Out at $6,032K [^1]. Dairy Queen reported $5.01 billion of systemwide sales [^22].

The disclosure with legal weight is Item 19 of the franchise disclosure document, and it shows how far one unit can sit from the average. Wendy's 2026 FDD averages $1,993,657 of gross sales across 5,347 franchise restaurants for fiscal 2025, from a weakest unit at $391,533 to a strongest at $7,726,390, and fewer than half reached the average [^2]. Company restaurants averaged $2,250,384 across 367 units [^2]. A year earlier the figures were $2,108,454 for franchise units and $2,326,904 for company units [^3], so both groups fell. Culver's discloses $4,142,737 of average sales across 988 franchised restaurants [^13], and Jack in the Box a franchise average of $1,913,335 for fiscal 2025 against $1,986,186 the year before [^19].

The decline at Wendy's came from fewer visits, not lower prices: its 10-K attributes the fiscal 2025 sales change to a traffic decline partly offset by a higher average check [^5]. No public source in the research publishes a burger menu price change for 2025 to 2026, or a ramp benchmark for a new unit, so any price growth or opening-year curve in a pro forma is the study's own assumption and should be labelled as one.

Three cautions apply. First, these are company units, run at a scale a single-unit borrower does not have. Second, "before rent" means the land and building are not yet paid for, through a lease or real estate debt service, so the $364,193 cannot be set against the loan payment as it stands [^2]. Third, a franchisee carries the brand's fee load, which Wendy's 10-K sets at a 4.0% royalty plus 3.5% national and 0.5% local advertising [^5]; the study has to establish which of those the disclosed P&L already carries and add the rest.

Labor is the line most exposed to local law. Wendy's restaurant labor rose from 32.1% of company sales in fiscal 2023 [^10] to 32.4% in fiscal 2025 [^5], while Jack in the Box reports payroll and benefits at 38.6% of company restaurant sales [^18]; the two filings define the line differently, so the study should start from the subject brand's own ratio and adjust it for the wage schedule at the site. The research found no franchisee EBITDA margin and no real estate NOI margin for any burger brand, so the Wendy's company P&L stays the reference and its limits belong in the study.

An Item 7 range is the franchisor's estimate of what a franchisee spends to open, written wide enough to hold across the system. Its use is a reasonableness test on bids and equipment quotes; its trap is what it leaves out. Wendy's 2025 range assumes a cash buyer and excludes real property [^3], and the 2026 summary gives a range of $1,580,457 to $3,105,000 without restating what it leaves out [^4], so the exclusion has to be read from the FDD. Burger King's top end fell from $4,705,600 in the 2025 FDD to $3,320,600 in 2026 [^15], so the edition a study cites matters, and its 2025 sources disagree on the site: the summary reports the 2025 range as stated excluding real estate [^15], while a breakdown of the same Item 7 lists real property and occupancy at $300,000 to $1,500,000 [^16]. A Burger King budget therefore has to be read against the FDD itself before land is added or left out. Jack in the Box excludes land from its prototype range [^14], and at the top of that range the build alone costs more than a year of the system's average franchise sales [^19]. Culver's prices land on its own line beside a building line that reaches $4.391 million [^17], so a Culver's project is a land-and-building purchase in a way the others are not.

Who owns the real estate changes the loan. McDonald's owned about 56% of the land and 80% of the buildings for restaurants in its consolidated markets at the end of 2025 [^12], so its franchisees typically operate on sites the franchisor controls and borrow against the business; the brand also asks for a 40% down payment on a new restaurant and $500,000 of liquidity [^14], well above the SBA floor. Wendy's is a landlord on a smaller scale, carrying $386.2 million of land, $541.2 million of buildings and improvements, $480.3 million of leasehold improvements and $395.5 million of equipment, $1,803.3 million gross, at December 28, 2025 [^5]. The study should say whether the borrower buys the site or leases it from the franchisor or a third party, and price occupancy to match.

The research found no primary construction cost per square foot for burger restaurants, no published building size, acreage or crew size for McDonald's, Wendy's or Burger King, and no total project cost for the unit size SBA most often finances. Those come from the subject's site plan, bids and the franchisor's prototype drawings.

The table shows two directions. McDonald's added US restaurants through 2025 [^11]. Wendy's opened 109 US units in the year [^6], then in February 2026 set a closure target whose low end was 298 units, after same-restaurant sales fell 5.6% for fiscal 2025 and 11.3% in the fourth quarter [^8]; its franchised US count was down to 5,385 by March 29 [^7]. Burger King ended 2025 with fewer units than it started with [^1], and Jack in the Box closed 86 units in fiscal 2025 [^21].

A closure program is not the same signal for every subject. Closure programs aim at low-volume units, so a subject in the upper part of its brand's Item 19 range may gain from a nearby closure, while one near the bottom is the profile being removed. It should also record the franchisor's hold over the borrower: Wendy's generally keeps a right of first refusal on transfers, and its relationship agreements restrict ownership changes or refinancing without its consent [^5]. Lenders have noticed; a 2026 survey of franchise lenders found fewer planning new Wendy's loans than hold them today [^30].

A burger franchise borrows under SBA rules only if its brand is listed in the SBA Franchise Directory, a condition in place since June 1, 2025 [^32]. A ground-up unit financed with 7(a) carries an equity injection of at least 10% and must show 1.15x coverage within two years of opening, within a 7(a) limit of $5 million [^25]. Where the franchisee buys or builds the real estate, SBA 504 is the usual fit, with a borrower contribution of 10%, 15% for a new business or a special purpose property, and 20% when both apply [^25]. SBA defines special purpose as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^25]; whether a burger building falls in that class is the CDC's reading of the property, as the hub's capital routes section sets out for every menu. On a 504 franchise loan the CDC's closing counsel now signs off on the legal sufficiency of the franchise agreement in its opinion [^31].

Buying an operating burger restaurant is a different test. For applications received by SBA on or after October 1, 2026, an initial acquisition needs 1.25x on the seller's historical cash flow, read from one or two fiscal years, and a quality-of-earnings report applies at $3 million or more [^24,25]. The SOP makes one narrow exception to the projection and quality-of-earnings rules, for an owner-occupied special purpose purchase, set out with the special purpose question above. Whether a study is ordered at all is the lender's call under SBA's rule, 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^26], and our note on the feasibility study myth explains when lenders ask for one anyway. What our burger files contain for SBA lenders and CDCs is on the SBA 7(a) and 504 practice; bank loans outside SBA are on the conventional practice.

The research found no typical loan term or rate for burger units and no 2024 to 2026 burger project with a named lender.

  1. EBITDA before rent is treated as cash for debt service

    The Wendy's disclosure stops before occupancy [^2]. A file that sets that figure against the loan payment without first deducting rent, or the real estate share of debt service, overstates coverage by the full cost of the site, and any fees the company P&L does not already carry come off as well [^5].

  2. A Burger King budget taken from the summary range

    The 2025 Burger King range is reported as excluding real estate [^15], yet a breakdown of the same Item 7 lists a real property and occupancy line [^16], and the top of the range fell between the 2025 and 2026 editions [^15]. A sources and uses built from the summary either counts the site twice or leaves it out; only the FDD edition the borrower signs settles which.

  3. A Wendy's subject is not placed against the closure program

    Wendy's closed 289 US units in the first half of 2026 [^9], against a closure target of 298 to 358 units set in February 2026, which counted 28 closings from late 2025 [^8]. A closed or listed Wendy's in the trade area belongs in the file, together with the subject's place in the Item 19 range that shows why it is not the next unit on the list.

  4. The operator's other debt is not examined

    The burger failures of 2025 and 2026 were franchisee balance sheets: Meritage Hospitality, a large Wendy's operator, filed Chapter 11, and its credit facility had been declared in default [^27]; Consolidated Burger, a Burger King franchisee, filed in April 2025 [^28]; and two Hardee's operators, Superior Star and ARC Burger, failed in 2025 and 2026 [^29].
