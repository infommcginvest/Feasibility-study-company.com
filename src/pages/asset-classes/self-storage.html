A self-storage facility earns its debt service from hundreds of small tenancies, most of them month to month, so revenue reprices continuously and the building has almost no second use. The lender is not underwriting a lease; it is underwriting how fast empty units fill against nearby space that opened recently, at the rate a new customer will pay today. Yardi Matrix data put the national street rate at $16.91 per sq ft in July 2025 [^1] and at $16.22 in April 2026 [^2], and advertised rates in August 2026 were 1.9% below a year earlier [^3].

Supply is the other half of the read. The national under-construction pipeline stood at 43.8 million net rentable square feet in August 2026, or 2.1% of existing stock [^4], and the forecast for 2026 completions was 52.93 million square feet [^5]. In the CMBS market the storage numbers point two ways at once: the delinquency rate was 0.05% in July 2026, and 84.4% of the 2023 vintage's balance was on servicer watchlists [^6].

SBA lends on storage through both 7(a) and 504, and a clause that can decide a storage file is the management agreement: an owner who hands a branded manager sole discretion becomes passive, as the capital section explains. The sections below follow a storage file in the order a credit officer reads one, from what the building costs to who else is building nearby. Six formats and ownership models are treated in the subtypes section, vehicle storage has its own hub, and the worked cases, two live and two in preparation, apply the method under SBA 504 and USDA.

The two kinds of evidence measure different things, and a lender should not average them. Builder guides price the structure: a 2025 guide puts a single-story building at $50 to $65 per sq ft before land and site work, and a multi-story building at $90 to $130 [^7]. A 2026 guide splits the hard cost into shell and doors at 50% to 60%, site work and utilities at 20% to 30%, and gates, technology and signage at 5% to 10%, with soft costs adding 8% to 15% on top [^8]. None of these ranges carries land, financing or lease-up carry, and none is tied to a region.

The delivered figure comes from Public Storage, which reports a total delivered cost for its developments and expansions. It delivered 2.1 million NRSF of developments and expansions in 2025 for $408.9 million [^9], which is $194.7 per NRSF by our calculation. Its pipeline at year-end held developments of 2.6 million NRSF budgeted at $479.5 million and expansions of 0.9 million NRSF at $130.4 million [^9], or $184.4 and $144.9 per NRSF calculated. By June 30, 2026 the pipeline had grown to about $691.7 million for 4.0 million NRSF, with $431.6 million still to spend [^10]. U-Haul's five-year program, $5.8 billion for 296 new locations and 26.2 million NRSF [^11], mixes conversions of existing buildings with ground-up sites and land, so its average says little about either.

The published figures cannot reconcile the building price with the delivered cost, because the 2025 guide's building prices leave out land. The study should therefore show each line of its own budget with its date.

Spending and tariffs

Census data show private mini-storage construction running at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of $6,331 million in July 2026, against $6,505 million a year earlier [^12]; the May and June 2026 readings were $6,364 million and $6,361 million on the same series [^12]. Costs moved the other way. The steel and aluminum tariff under Section 232 went to 50% from 25% on June 4, 2025 [^13], steel prices were up 33% from the start of 2025, and CBRE estimated the effect on construction cost at up to 5% [^14]. In a storage budget that exposure sits in the shell, which a 2026 builder guide puts with the doors at 50% to 60% of hard cost [^8].

What is not published

No source gives development cost per square foot by U.S. region, the land share of total project cost, the total cost of a project financed through SBA or USDA, or current lead times for doors and HVAC equipment. For storage the gap that matters most is rentable efficiency: every per-foot benchmark on this page divides by gross or by net rentable area, so a budget should state both side by side, and its interest carry should run for the lease-up the study projects.

A broker's second-quarter report says Public Storage's machine-learning staffing model cut staffing hours by more than 30% [^20], a tool no single-site owner can copy. Those margins come from portfolios with call centers and revenue management software. An independent facility carries its manager, insurance and marketing on one revenue line, and no public source prints its expense ratio because the trade association's survey is sold to members. A study should build expenses line by line and explain any margin near the REIT figures.

Two rates, and why the difference matters

The achieved rate a REIT reports is what its existing customers pay. In the first quarter of 2026 CubeSmart achieved $23.39 per sq ft, Public Storage $22.05, SmartStop $20.10, Extra Space $19.92 and National Storage Affiliates $15.88 [^19]. The street rate is what a new customer is quoted: $16.22 per sq ft nationally in April 2026 [^2], with advertised rates 1.9% below a year earlier by August [^3]. The achieved and street series come from different samples, so we do not net one against the other. A facility in lease-up rents almost all of its space at the street rate, and Public Storage says in its 10-K that it prices fill-up space aggressively [^24]. Its own lease-up pool held 606 facilities and 54.1 million NRSF at year-end 2025, 24% of its rentable area [^9], so the largest operator is itself competing for move-ins in many of the markets an SBA borrower enters. How a study carries the two rates through a pro forma is the subject of our research note on effective and asking rent.

Occupancy and its seasons

Average REIT occupancy was 92.5% at June 30, 2026 [^20], against 90.6% a year earlier on another tracker, which had recorded a fall of 80 basis points [^21]. Extra Space reported 94.2% at mid-2026 against 94.4% a year before, with same-store revenue of $690.192 million up 2.4% and NOI of $496.078 million up 3.5% in the second quarter [^17]. CubeSmart's quarter shows the squeeze at its plainest: occupancy of 91.0%, revenue up 0.8%, expense up 4.4% and NOI down 0.7% [^18]. Brokers forecast a national average asking rent of $1.18 per sq ft a month in 2026, down 0.8% [^23]. A projected stabilized occupancy above the REIT averages needs its own evidence.

A broker's ranking of 2025 SBA storage lenders adds what the federal file does not label: purpose. It counts 53 start-up loans for $91.7 million, 30 loans to existing businesses for $39.0 million and 37 acquisitions for $52.4 million, and finds one lender holding 62 loans and 61% of the dollars among 33 active lenders [^26]. Its year basis is not stated, so we do not set it beside the register's counts. The concentration matters: a file declined by the dominant storage lender has few specialists to try next.

The CMBS view

Storage has historically been one of the best-performing property types in CMBS. A rating agency put its delinquency rate at 0.1% in September 2025, against 6.6% for all CMBS [^25], and Trepp data showed 0.05% in July 2026 across $23.7 billion of exposure, about 4,800 loans and about 15,600 properties [^6]. The stress is in the watchlists. Some 84.4% of the 2023 vintage's balance and 56.6% of the 2024 vintage's were being watched, against 26.6% of the $5.1 billion 2021 vintage and 4.7% of the roughly $4.3 billion written in 2025; only 0.84% of balances were below 1.0x coverage, and the financed pool's weighted occupancy was 85.12% [^6]. Atlanta led the metros at a 50.31% watchlist share with pool occupancy of 85.49%, followed by Chicago at 48.4% (88.09%) and Miami at 38.79% (88.94%) [^6].

The pattern is not failure but thinner coverage on loans sized at 2022 and 2023 rents and cap rates. A new loan in those metros meets the same street rates. Research found no Inspector General report, SBA notice or USDA finding specific to self-storage between 2023 and 2026, so the CMBS data are the closest evidence of where reviewers should look.

Eligibility: an operating business, if the owner runs it

Trade press dates storage's standing as an operating business to SOP 50 10 5(C), effective October 1, 2010 [^32]. The June 2025 edition of the rules still bars businesses primarily engaged in owning real estate and leasing it, naming apartments and mobile home parks, and does not name self-storage among them [^33]. The clause that bites is the one on management agreements. An owner who hands a third-party manager sole discretion over the facility becomes an ineligible passive business; the owner has to keep meaningful oversight by approving the budget and large capital spending, controlling the bank accounts and overseeing employees [^33]. The agreement belongs in the file. The same edition lets exterior space count toward occupancy, naming outdoor storage yards for moving and storage companies among its examples [^33]; the occupancy floors are 51% for an existing building and 60% for new construction. A lender should confirm both clauses in the October 2026 edition.

7(a) for a new facility and for a purchase

For a start-up facility, 7(a) asks for 10% equity and projected coverage of 1.15x within two years [^30], over a real estate term of up to 25 years. That window is tight for storage, because Public Storage says its new facilities can reach stabilized occupancy as early as two to three years after completion [^34]: the projection has to show occupancy and rent at the end of month 24, not at stabilization. A purchase needs 10% equity and 1.25x on historical results, and projections are reviewed but cannot meet the floor, except an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property acquisition whose price the appraised value fully supports, where the floor may be met on projections within two years [^30]. Whether a storage facility counts as special purpose property therefore decides the acquisition test as well as the 504 tier.

504, and the special purpose question

SBA's 504 tiers read as follows: 10% as the standard contribution, 15% for a New Business (two years or less) or Limited or Special Purpose Property, and 20% for a project that is both, with the financing stacked as follows: Third Party Lender 50%, debenture 40%, borrower 10%; debenture 35% at a 15% contribution and 30% at 20% [^30]. SBA defines special purpose property as a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built. Its example list in the 504 chapter does not name self-storage, while its acquisition guidance names storage units among the purchases where special purpose real estate fully secures the loan [^30]. Development companies read that differently: one CDC writes that SBA does not treat storage as special purpose, so 10% down is possible [^35], and another that SBA typically does, so 15% applies, or 20% for a start-up [^36]. A start-up facility sits in the 15% tier or higher either way, as a new business. For an established operator the answer decides between the 10% and 15% tiers, so it should be settled in writing early. SBA's cap on the two programs held together became $10 million on July 4, 2026, for a borrower whose 7(a) loan is approved before its 504 loan [^37]. Our note on special purpose property risk explains what the classification does to recovery after a default.

USDA Business and Industry

The B&I program has accepted storage since its OneRD final rule of December 10, 2021 removed the ineligibility of self-storage facilities [^27]. For fiscal 2026 the Federal Register notice sets the guarantee at 85% for a loan under $5 million and at 80% for one from $5 million to $25 million, with a 3.0% initial fee and a 0.55% annual retention fee [^38]. Those terms apply to fiscal 2026 obligations, and the research records no fiscal 2027 notice. The December 2021 rule sets a minimum tangible balance-sheet equity in a table at 7 CFR 5001.105(d) [^27]; none of the sources behind this page gives a percentage under the current rule, so the lender should state it for the subject. No published USDA storage project was found. Our USDA Rural Development practice area covers the program, and the Bardstown case, in preparation, will apply it.

Banks, debt funds and the REITs as lenders

A broker's rate sheet dated August 10, 2026 quoted fixed storage loans at 6.74% for five years, 6.88% for seven and 7.04% for ten, at up to 75% of value and coverage of about 1.25x to 1.35x [^28]; a 2026 acquisition guide gives 1.20x to 1.30x and 65% to 75% [^29]. The benchmarks under those quotes stood at 4.44% for the 10-year Treasury, 4.33% for the 10-year swap and 3.63% for 30-day average SOFR on June 30, 2026 [^20], after the Treasury peaked at 4.67% in mid-May [^39]. Construction money costs more than it did: a 2025 trade report put storage construction loans at 7% to 9%, against 3% to 4% before [^13]. Banks and credit unions provided roughly 70% of storage lending volume in 2023 and closer to half in 2026, with debt funds, government programs and CMBS taking the rest [^23].

The public operators have become lenders themselves. The third step is where most studies go wrong, because the national figure depends on who counts. JLL used 6.32 sq ft per person in 2026 [^49], StorageCafe 7.6 for 2025 [^50] and RentCafe 7 in February 2026 [^51]. An older almanac put the national average near 5.4 sq ft for 2018, with Idaho at 10.76 and the District of Columbia at 1.84 [^52]. The number also says nothing alone. The pipeline is shrinking, slowly

The under-construction pipeline ended 2025 at 54.46 million NRSF [^58] and fell through 2026: 50.4 million and 2.5% of stock in January, with 2,759 projects in all stages [^59]; 46.5 million and 2.3% in March [^60]; 45.3 million and 2.2% in June [^61]; and 43.8 million and 2.1% in August, when 594 properties were under construction and 1,499 more planned [^4]. Openings follow with a lag. About 51 million sq ft opened in 2025, 2.4% of inventory [^50]. Yardi expects 52.93 million NRSF of completions in 2026, almost 19% below 2025, and 45.25 million in 2027, after first-half 2026 completions of 22.27 million fell 27.7% [^5]; a second tracker counted nearly 27.8 million sq ft in the same half, the lowest since 2014 [^23]. The trackers disagree on levels and agree on direction.

National totals hide the markets where new space bunches up. Phoenix had 6.2% of its stock under construction in August 2026 and Orlando 4.6% [^4], and a September 2025 report had street rates falling 2.2% in Las Vegas, 1.7% in Phoenix and 1.6% in San Diego [^54].

Cities that stopped approving storage

The documented regulatory risk in 2025 and 2026 is local land use. Chicago's council removed storage warehouses as a by-right use in its B3, C1, C2, C3 and DX districts in May 2025, leaving existing facilities as legal nonconforming uses [^62,63]. Atlanta's mayor halted new storage applications by executive order on June 24, 2026, and the council adopted a 180-day moratorium unanimously on July 6, with officials citing few jobs, truck traffic and the displacement of other commercial uses [^64,65,66]. Rockdale County, Georgia adopted a 120-day development moratorium covering storage in January 2026 and extended it [^67], Yonkers passed a 180-day citywide moratorium in September 2026 [^68], and Coos Bay, Oregon adopted a moratorium of up to 12 months in August 2025, describing 13 facilities as more than twice the typical supply for its size [^69]. A moratorium protects an existing facility from new competitors and can strand a site that lacks its approvals, so the zoning letter and permit status belong in the file. Our Georgia and Illinois pages cover the state rules around those cities.

Storage formats overlap more than the labels suggest. Extra Space describes most of its sites as hybrids, with drive-up buildings and multi-floor buildings on the same parcel [^55], so the line between single-story and multi-story runs through many facilities rather than between them. A building format answers what the project costs and how it leases. A management model answers who runs it and whether the owner still counts as an operating business. An investment plan answers whether the loan rests on today's income or on an improvement. One facility can sit in all three, and the study should say which question it is answering.

Two formats are built here: drive-up and single-story facilities and climate-controlled multi-story buildings. Two are ways of getting space without building from the ground: conversions of vacant buildings and portable and container storage, which is a fleet business with warehouses behind it. Two are about ownership: third-party management and value-add purchases. Storage condominiums, where owners buy units of 1,000 to 2,000 sq ft and inventory is estimated at under 5% of conventional storage [^53], are too small a market for a section of their own.

Own hub

RV and Boat Storage

Vehicle storage rents parking stalls, canopies and large enclosed bays to owners of motorhomes, trailers and boats. Its demand comes from vehicle ownership and local parking rules rather than household moves, and its land need per dollar of revenue is far higher, so it has its own hub and its own cases.

How it earns. A drive-up facility rents exterior-access units by the month. It suits customers who load from a vehicle, contractors and small businesses, and it carries little operating cost per foot because there are no corridors, elevators or conditioned space. It was long the majority format: in the trade association's 2015 survey, about 6,000 of more than 10,000 facilities were single-story, and the average facility had 546 units, the median 517 [^70]. Those figures are old and cover all formats, but no later public survey replaces them.

What it costs. The 2025 builder range of $50 to $65 per sq ft for a single-story building, before land and site work [^7], is the lowest in the development table, and older trade guidance put the land need at two to four acres [^71]. That is why rural and exurban storage is so often single-story. No source publishes a cost or revenue per unit for the format, typical staffing, or a confirmed drive-up project with a named lender, so a study has to build the budget from the subject's own bid and site plan.

How lenders underwrite it. A small-town drive-up facility is the typical SBA and USDA storage file: an owner-operator, modest cost, a trade area of a few thousand households. The lender reads supply per person, the distance to the next facilities and how many new sites could be approved nearby, because a format this cheap invites a second builder. Rural sites can also use USDA's guarantee, described in the capital section.

Where it fails review. The weak file counts existing competitors but not the parcels already zoned for storage. The study should show the entitled sites nearby and coverage if one more facility opens during the lease-up.

How lenders underwrite it. For SBA the agreement is an eligibility document, as the capital section sets out: a manager with sole discretion turns the owner into an ineligible passive business, so the owner must approve the budget and large capital spending, control the bank accounts and oversee the staff [^33]. The managers are lenders too: Extra Space originated $140.6 million of mortgage and mezzanine bridge loans in the second quarter of 2026 and assumed $1,475,000,000 of bridge loans outstanding in its full-year guidance [^90]. Managed facilities also reach institutional debt: two New Jersey properties, one of 113,640 sq ft and 855 units and one of 157,599 sq ft and 1,348 units, sat in a branded portfolio refinanced in 2025 [^91].

Where it fails review. An SBA file fails when the agreement gives the operator the accounts or the budget, or when the pro forma uses the manager's margins without its fee. The study should quote the oversight terms and show coverage if the brand leaves.

How it earns. The value-add buyer earns the gap between how a facility is run and how a platform would run it. Public Storage put that gap in numbers when it bought National Storage Affiliates: a same-store operating margin of 78% against 70% historically for the acquired portfolio [^10]. Its pool of acquired and developed facilities outside the same-store set, 441 facilities and 39.3 million sq ft, grew revenue 25.6% and NOI 21.5% in the second quarter of 2026 [^93], which is what lifting occupancy and rates on bought assets looks like at scale.

What it costs. Prices are lower than at the peak, about 20% below 2022 per square foot in 2026 [^94], and buyers are back: transactions were up about 20% and deal volume about 50% from a year earlier through mid-2026 [^23]. Public Storage bought 20 facilities with 1.5 million NRSF for $222.5 million in the second quarter, or $148 per NRSF by our calculation, and 44 facilities with 3.2 million NRSF for $454.9 million in the year to date including contracts [^10]; about 70% of its purchases were sourced off-market [^92]. Extra Space bought 17 stores plus a joint venture interest for $90.7 million in the quarter [^17]. No source defines a Class B storage segment, prices it per unit, or names a value-add purchase with its lender, and no breakdown of value-add capital spending was found.

How lenders underwrite it. This is where the SBA rules and the business plan collide. A 7(a) change of ownership is tested at 1.25x on the seller's history, and projections count only for a special purpose property [^30]. Whether a storage facility qualifies is the open question set out in the capital section. Without it, the loan is sized on the income the buyer is promising to fix, and the improvement has to come from equity or a later refinance. Bridge lenders, including the REITs' own programs, finance the plan instead and expect a takeout once occupancy rises.

Where it fails review. The value-add file fails when the purchase price capitalizes the improved income, when the plan's rate increases ignore the street rate a new customer will pay, or when the seller's statements are read without the month they were taken. Marcus & Millichap says the watchlisted loans, mostly 2021 to 2024 originations, could bring more facilities to market through 2027 [^23]; a buyer of one of those facilities inherits rents that already failed to cover a loan sized at the peak, and the study should show why its plan differs.

  1. Lease-up priced at portfolio rents

    The pro forma carries the achieved rate of a seasoned REIT portfolio into a new building's first two years. A new facility fills at the street rate, which in August 2026 stood 1.9% below a year earlier [^3], and the largest operator prices its own fill-up space aggressively. The rent schedule should come from dated competitor street quotes by unit size.

  2. A pipeline counted nationally but not locally

    National trackers show the pipeline shrinking, which says nothing about the two projects opening across the road in the subject's first year. Newly opened space can sit nearly empty: a 2025 portfolio refinancing included a Detroit property of 78,187 sq ft and 645 units on 6 acres at 5% occupancy [^91]. The study should list every permitted and under-construction project in the trade area with its expected opening and size.

  3. Supply per person from mismatched sources

    The inventory comes from one vendor and the national benchmark from another. With 6.32 sq ft per person from JLL and 7.6 from StorageCafe both in circulation [^49,50], the mismatch can make an average market look undersupplied. Inventory and benchmark should come from one named source.

  4. Stabilized coverage only

    A 7(a) start-up must show 1.15x within two years, and storage often takes longer to fill. A study that shows coverage at stabilization in year three or four, but not at month 24, has not answered the program's test.

  5. A management agreement that makes the owner passive

    A branded management contract that gives the operator sole discretion over budgets, accounts or staff makes the borrower an ineligible passive business under SBA's rules. Reviewers read the agreement; the study should quote its oversight terms and carry the fee as an expense.

  6. The 504 equity tier assumed, not settled

    Sponsors budget the 10% standard 504 contribution while the CDC treats storage as special purpose and asks for 15%, or 20% for a new business. The study and the CDC should settle the classification before the sources and uses are fixed.

  7. Expenses copied from REIT margins

    A single facility projected at a public operator's NOI margin has borrowed payroll savings it does not have, and often a pre-sale property tax bill. Taxes belong at the reassessed value.

  8. A site without its approvals

    Chicago removed storage as a by-right use in several districts, and Atlanta, Rockdale County and Yonkers paused new approvals. A study that counts a site's supply advantage without confirming its zoning, or counts a competitor's permitted project that a moratorium has since frozen, misstates both sides. The zoning letter and the permit status belong in the file.

  9. A conversion read as a new building

    A converted shell loses rentable area to corridors and elevators and hides structural, roof and fire-protection costs. A conversion budget at ground-up efficiency, without an inspection and a contingency, fails on the first site visit.
