On a credit memorandum a quick-service restaurant can pass for a small real estate loan: a pad near a signal, a box of a few thousand square feet, a drive-thru lane and a pylon sign. The debt is repaid by something else. It is repaid by a franchised operating business whose menu, prices, fees, remodel cycle and right to transfer are set by a system the borrower does not control. Limited-service restaurants, the NAICS 722513 code for places where guests order and pay before they eat, took in $358.9 billion in the 2022 Economic Census and employed 4,965,080 people in March 2023 [^5]. Most of that business runs through brands. The 500 largest chains sold $451.5 billion in 2025 [^6], and the limited-service chains among them sold $361.5 billion [^2].

The national total is the least useful number in the file, because the industry stopped moving as one in 2025. Menu prices rose 3.8% while the Top 500 grew sales 3.0%, the median chain's sales fell 1.3% in real terms, and 48% of the chains added no units or shrank [^6]. Chicken, coffee and beverage concepts opened most of the new restaurants [^7], while older burger, pizza and sub systems closed hundreds of theirs. A study that applies an industry growth rate to a single unit has averaged a brand that is adding stores with one that is closing them, and the lender inherits whichever one the borrower signed with.

The questions a lender is really asking are narrow and mostly brand-specific. Is the brand listed in the SBA Franchise Directory, so the loan is eligible at all [^40]? Does the brand's Item 19 support the sales this trade area can produce, at a statistic the study names? Does the Item 7 budget, plus the land, site work and other costs Item 7 leaves out, reconcile to the sources and uses? Does coverage hold under the rules that took effect on October 1, 2026, including the historical floor of 1.25x for an initial acquisition [^9]? And can the site be entitled with the vehicle queue and the grease interceptor the local code requires?

This page answers those questions for limited-service, counter and drive-thru restaurants, most of them franchised. Full-service dining, fast casual as a class, independents and ghost kitchens are covered on the restaurant hub, and coffee shops, from kiosks and cafes to drive-thru coffee chains, on the coffee shop hub. Burger systems have their own burger restaurant page; chicken, Mexican, pizza, sandwich and the multi-unit franchisee are treated in full below, and the page ends with two worked SBA cases.

Item 7 of a franchise disclosure document is a table of low and high estimates for the money a franchisee spends to open: the initial franchise fee, leasehold improvements or construction, equipment and signage, opening inventory, training travel, deposits and an allowance for the first months of operation. The two ends are not a mean and a spread. They are the franchisor's statement of the cheapest and the most expensive openings it expects, and the categories it chooses to include decide whether the total looks like a project budget or a fraction of one.

The inline and delivery formats folded into this page sit at the low end. A traditional Domino's store carries an Item 7 range of $231,450 to $743,500 [^10], and a non-traditional store, the kind placed inside another business, $107,450 to $709,500 [^4]. A Jersey Mike's runs $436,176 to $1,162,228 [^11,12]. A Taco Bell Express, the small-format version of the brand, runs $287,950 to $857,700 [^13]. These formats are usually leasehold builds: the franchisee finishes a space someone else owns, so the range prices the fit-out and equipment, not land or a shell building. A freestanding drive-thru is a different project, and the brand ranges for the burger systems that build most of them (Wendy's, McDonald's, Burger King, Jack in the Box and Culver's) are set out on the burger restaurant page.

Brands that do not franchise, or that do not publish an Item 7, leave only filings and permits. Chipotle expects capital spending of about $834.1 million in 2026, including $531.8 million for new restaurants and $266.9 million for existing ones, after $666.3 million in 2025 [^14]. A Chick-fil-A permitted in November 2025 in North Jacksonville, Florida covers a 5,033 sq ft building with a dual drive-thru on 1.39 acres [^15]; a state environmental filing for an Escondido, California unit shows 3,124 sq ft [^16]; and a Raising Cane's approved in Grandville, Michigan in October 2025 is 3,077 sq ft with two drive-thru lanes [^17]. Permit valuations price the building under the permit, not the land, site work, kitchen package or soft costs.

Cost inflation in 2025 and 2026 came through metal. The Section 232 tariff on steel and aluminum rose to 50% in June 2025 [^18], and producer prices for steel mill products were 23.4% higher in August 2026 than a year before [^19]. Canopies, structural steel, walk-in boxes and hood systems all carry that exposure, so a contractor quote from early 2025 understates a 2026 build.

Several things a lender would want are simply not published. No public source in our research gives quick-service construction cost per square foot by region from a primary or trade-association source; contractor marketing pages that do were left out. No source publishes land or equipment as a share of total project cost, equipment lead times, or a total project cost for the unit size SBA and USDA lenders finance most often. Item 7 ranges for Popeyes, Raising Cane's, Papa Johns, Pizza Hut, Subway, Jimmy John's, Firehouse Subs and a traditional freestanding Taco Bell were not found.

A defensible budget therefore stacks four layers and labels each one: land or ground rent, with the appraisal or lease behind it; site work and the building, from a dated bid or a permit valuation adjusted for what the permit excludes; the franchisor's Item 7 categories, taken line by line rather than as a total; and working capital, where Item 7 already gives a floor (Domino's estimates up to $73,000 of additional funds for the first three months [^20]). Contingency and any remodel the franchise agreement will require within the loan term belong in the uses, not in a footnote.

Two kinds of sales figure circulate, and a study must name the one it relies on. Trade rankings such as the QSR 50 publish an average unit volume that brands submit themselves, usually across company and franchised units of every age and format. Item 19 is the franchisor's financial performance representation under the FTC rule: a defined sample, a stated period, and often a median and a share of units above the average. The two can differ by more than a lender's coverage cushion. Chick-fil-A's ranking average is $7,700 thousand [^23], while its Item 19 for freestanding and drive-thru units averages $9,161,239 [^22], because the second figure leaves out mall and other small formats. A study that pairs a drive-thru site with the all-unit average understates it; one that pairs a mall unit with the Item 19 figure overstates it.

The gap between mean and median shows how skewed a system is. Chick-fil-A's freestanding median sits only a little below its mean [^22], which says the distribution is not skewed by a few very large units. Domino's franchised traditional stores averaged $27,267 a week against a $26,059 median [^4], up from $26,467 and $25,160 a year earlier [^20]. A new unit in an average trade area should be underwritten nearer the median than the mean, and a study that uses the mean should show why the site sits in the upper half.

Costs are disclosed far less often. Domino's publishes a cost table in its 2026 disclosure document, and the low-volume rows are the useful ones for a lender: for stores selling under $15,000 a week, variable costs come to 79.7% and pro forma EBITDA to only 1.5%, both measured against royalty sales [^4]. That is the shape of the downside case for any delivery-led store, since the fixed costs do not fall with the sales. Elsewhere the evidence is company-level. Yum expects Taco Bell's U.S. restaurant-level margin to run at 24% to 25% in 2026 [^26], and a large Wendy's franchisee reported prime cost, food and labor together, of 66.1% in 2025 [^27]. Across the industry, 42% of operators told the National Restaurant Association their restaurants were not profitable in 2025 [^28].

The fixed charge that replaces a management fee in franchised quick service is the royalty and the advertising fund. Domino's charges a 5.5% royalty and a 4% advertising contribution [^20]. Both come off the top line before rent and debt service, so a study should take them from the franchise agreement, including any early-year incentive that later expires.

Labor is the other line that moved by statute. California's fast-food minimum wage under AB 1228 has been $20.00 an hour since April 1, 2024, unchanged through 2026, and it applies to chains with more than 60 establishments nationally [^29,30]. A California study needs a wage line built on that rate and on the Fast Food Council's power to raise it; our research found no Council wage decision for 2026.

The gaps are as important as the figures. No public source publishes a real-estate-basis NOI margin for a quick-service unit, unit-level margins for Chick-fil-A, Raising Cane's or Chipotle, a margin benchmark for sandwich franchisees, or menu-price changes by category for 2025 to 2026. Third-party management fees in the hotel sense are not a standard structure here. Where a pro forma shows a margin, the study should name the system and the year it came from.

The register figures above come straight from SBA's 7(a) and 504 loan-level files, with loan status as of June 30, 2026. Two definitions matter. The register's quick-service class pools limited-service restaurants (NAICS 722513) with snack and nonalcoholic beverage bars (722515), so it also holds coffee shops, which the coffee shop hub covers, and juice and snack bars that no quick-service study would count as competitors. Two things put its counts above tabulations of 722513 alone: the pooled codes and a longer window. The register count covers ten fiscal years, FY2016 to FY2025, while one data site reports 11,949 limited-service 7(a) loans from FY2020 to the end of 2025 at an average of $473K [^1], so the gap between the two totals is not a measure of the snack and beverage codes. And the charge-off rates are measured only on approval cohorts from FY2011 to FY2020, each at least five full years old, so they describe loans that have had time to fail.

The count-based rate and the dollar-based rate tell different stories, and a credit officer needs both. The count rate on the seasoned 7(a) cohorts is 8.6%; the share of approved dollars charged off is lower. When the loss rate by dollars sits below the rate by count, the smaller loans are the ones failing more often. A study supporting a small single-unit loan should therefore be read against the count rate, not the dollar rate. The 504 count rate is far lower, consistent with a program that lends against owner-occupied real estate behind a first lien.

The secondary analyses circulate widely and conflict. One analysis of SBA data puts the default share of resolved limited-service loans at 18.9%, across 121,027 loans originated from 2000 [^32]. Another sets a benchmark of 10.3% across 108 limited-service franchise brands for the FY2010 to FY2019 cohorts [^33]. An accounting firm's older sample found restaurant charge-offs at 10.6% against a 3.9% sample average [^34]. The spread comes from denominators. A rate on resolved loans excludes every loan still performing; a cohort rate depends on which years it pools. None of them is wrong in its own terms, and none should be quoted in a credit memorandum without its definition and period.

Rate exposure is the other half of performance. A broker's sample of 5,406 limited-service 7(a) loans shows the middle half priced between 9.24% and 10.99% [^31]. SBA caps the spread a lender may charge over the base rate, with the lowest cap on the largest loans [^35], and a variable-rate loan moves with the base, so a pro forma built at today's Prime of 7.00% should also show coverage after a rise. The brand-level loss histories, which are often more informative than any class rate, are discussed with each menu system below, and the note on SBA default economics shows how the measurement basis moves the answer.

No SBA, Inspector General or GAO publication gives limited-service charge-off or purchase rates by fiscal year, or a restaurant-specific Inspector General finding for 2023 to 2026. The register supplies the count and the rate; the cause of each loss is in no public file.

SBA lending to franchised quick service was reset twice between June 2025 and October 2026. The June 2025 edition of the SOP (50 10 8) restored a minimum equity injection for start-ups and complete changes of ownership and let a seller note count toward it only on full standby for the life of the loan and for no more than half of the injection [^8,36]; the edition now in force keeps the injection at 10% for a start-up and 10% for an initial acquisition [^113]. The June 2025 edition reinstated the SBA Franchise Directory, so a brand that meets the FTC franchise definition must be listed before any of its franchisees can borrow [^40], and it cut the small-loan threshold from $500,000 to $350,000 [^46]. The edition now in force, SOP 50 10 8.1, took effect on October 1, 2026. It rewrote the change-of-ownership rules, set coverage at 1.25x for initial acquisitions and 1.15x for business expansions, measured on the last fiscal year or the average of the last two, required a quality-of-earnings report on initial acquisitions and business expansions at $3 million or more, and closed 7(a) Small to acquisitions [^9,37,38,113]. Our note on what SOP 50 10 8.1 changed for feasibility studies follows each rule into the study.

For a new unit, 7(a) fits a franchisee who leases the pad and needs equipment, leasehold work and working capital in one loan of up to $5 million. For an acquisition, the historical test now carries the file: projections can no longer meet the floor [^37]. The SOP makes one exception, the owner-occupied special purpose purchase that the appraised value fully supports, where projections may meet the floor within 2 years, and it exempts Owner Buyout, ESOP & Cooperative, and any acquisition of an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property from the quality-of-earnings report [^113]. Whether a brand-specific drive-thru qualifies is a question about the property, taken up with the 504 tiers below. For every other purchase, a study's role shifts from forecasting the buyer's plan to testing whether the seller's reported results are durable in that trade area.

The 504 program fits the owner who buys the land and the building. The borrower's contribution is 10% for an established business in an ordinary building, 15% when the business is new or the property special purpose, and 20% when both apply [^39]. Our research found no SBA text that classifies a quick-service building as special purpose, so the tier depends on how the CDC reads the property, and a drive-thru built to one brand's trade dress is the kind of building that invites the question. A 504 borrower must be wholly owned by U.S. citizens, nationals or permanent residents, and from January 1, 2026 the franchisor's executed certification is required [^40]. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, CDC closing counsel also certifies that the franchise documents are legally sufficient [^38]. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice covers both programs.

USDA's Business and Industry guarantee reaches rural pads that a bank will not hold alone. In FY2025 it guaranteed 80% of a loan, charged a 3% initial fee (1% for qualifying projects) and a 0.55% annual fee, and lent from $200K to $25 million in places under 50,000 people [^41]. The FY2025 notice set the program level at $3,272,644,830 [^42]. Accommodation and food services drew nearly $4 billion of core B&I financing from 2015 to 2024 [^41], but we found no named B&I loan to a quick-service restaurant. HUD has no program that fits this asset.

Conventional lending is the route for established operators. No bank disclosure or lender survey we could read publishes loan-to-value, loan-to-cost, coverage or recourse terms for quick service. At platform scale the borrower's debt is priced like any leveraged company's; the multi-unit platform section shows what that looks like. Our conventional and institutional practice handles those files.

The net lease market prices quick service in two tiers, and the gap between them is the market's view of who is really paying the rent. In the third quarter of 2026, leases backed by a corporate parent asked 5.90% nationally, while leases guaranteed by a franchisee asked 6.87% [^44]. Both tiers drifted up through 2026, and by the end of September the 10-year Treasury yield stood above 5.00% [^44], so a lease priced in early 2025 is not today's market.

Listing data tell a slightly different story because inventory is weighted toward long corporate leases. Listed quick-service net lease properties averaged 5.71% in the second quarter of 2025, 5.69% in the third and 5.68% at year-end [^48,49,45], and Chipotle leases with ten or more years remaining averaged 4.81% at the end of 2025 [^45]. Those are asking prices for a bond-like income stream, which a franchisee's SBA-financed pad is not.

The going-concern evidence is thin and recent history argues for caution. Jack in the Box sold Del Taco for $119 million at close, after paying $575 million to $585 million for it in 2022 and 2023 [^50,51], a reminder that the brand portion of a restaurant's value can vanish while the real estate holds. The only published franchisee-group multiple is Restaurant Brands' purchase of Carrols at 6.6x estimated EBITDA [^52], a 2024 transaction, and it priced a large franchisee network rather than a single store. No published source gives a price per unit or per square foot for quick-service real estate, and none states how a sale price divides between land and building and the business itself.

The general case for valuing the lease and the business apart is made on the restaurant hub; what quick service adds is the two-tier spread above, so a corporate-tier cap rate should never support a franchisee's collateral. Our note on special purpose property risk covers what happens to recovery when the brand leaves the building.

The limited-service industry is large, local and mostly franchised. County Business Patterns, as tabulated by a data vendor, counts 4,965,080 employees in NAICS 722513 [^5], and Census nonemployer statistics add 52,005 limited-service businesses with no employees at all [^53]. Secondary tabulations of the employer establishment count for 722513 alone disagree, so this page uses the register's County Business Patterns count for the class, shown in the supply section, and says what the class contains.

Ownership is concentrating. Across all franchised sectors, the International Franchise Association's outlook forecasts that multi-unit and multi-brand operators will own 58.8% of franchised locations in 2026, as a franchisor's post reports it [^54]. In restaurants the 200 largest franchisees ran 33,635 units on 2025 data, up from 33,128 a year earlier [^3,55]. Wendy's had 203 franchisees operating its U.S. restaurants at the end of 2025 [^56]. For a single-unit borrower that structure matters twice over: the platforms bid for the same pads, and they set the wage a new unit must match. The franchisor's hold over transfers, which also shapes who can buy the unit if the borrower fails, is set out with Wendy's own terms on the burger restaurant page.

Growth is narrow. Technomic's 2026 Top 500 counted more than 3,400 net new chain locations, 45% of them from five brands [^57], and 83% of openings came from beverage and snack, chicken and coffee concepts [^7]. The other side of that ledger is closure: Black Box Intelligence judged 4% of limited-service units at risk of closing in 2026, against 9% of full-service units, counting a unit at risk when its sales run 30% below its peak year [^58]. A study should place the subject's brand on that map before it places the site.

The national demand signal for 2025 and 2026 is flat traffic carried by higher checks. U.S. foodservice traffic fell 0.3% in 2025 while average spending per visit rose about 3% in the fourth quarter [^59]. In the quarter to June 2025, total restaurant traffic fell 1% while value-menu traffic rose 1% [^60], which is the pattern of a customer trading down rather than disappearing. Census monthly sales for food services and drinking places, seasonally adjusted, were $100,241 million in November 2025 and $99,722 million in January 2026 [^61]. The National Restaurant Association forecasts only 1.3% real growth for 2026 [^62], and 29% of limited-service operators expected higher sales against 27% expecting lower [^63,64].

A flat pool means a new unit's sales come from someone else's register. The closest published evidence of how much moves is company commentary on closures: Noodles & Company expected to keep about 30% of a closed unit's sales at nearby stores, and Jack in the Box reported a lift of about 30% at units near a closure [^65]. That is a transfer rate, not a capture standard, but it is the only published number on how sales redistribute inside one trade area, and it argues against assuming a new unit adds to the market without taking from it.

The method a lender can follow

  1. Draw the trade area by drive time and daypart. A breakfast drive-thru on a commuter route and a dinner unit next to a supermarket draw from different places.
  2. Build the demand pool from public inputs: population and households from Census estimates and the American Community Survey, food-away-from-home spending from the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, and traffic counts from the state transportation department. These are site-level inputs, and no published quick-service benchmark replaces them.
  3. Inventory the competition by brand, format and daypart, including the units that closed in the last two years, since their sales are now somewhere else.
  4. Estimate the subject's share, then convert dollars to transactions with the brand's average check and test the peak hour against the drive-thru lane the site can hold.
  5. Reconcile the answer to the brand's Item 19: name the percentile the site lands on and explain it. Item 19 samples usually exclude units open less than a full year, so the ramp to that level is a separate assumption; no public benchmark for months to stabilization exists.

The order matters. A study that starts from the brand's average and works backward to a trade area that justifies it has assumed its conclusion; the note on why the trade area is the study walks through the other direction.

That last point explains the top of the table. The District of Columbia's density is measured against residents only, while its restaurants also serve commuters and visitors who live elsewhere, so a study there that compared units with residents would read a crowded market where the demand pool is simply larger than the census. California holds the largest count, a function of its size; a count says nothing about density until it is divided by residents. Minnesota sits near the bottom of the state ranking, which by itself says nothing about whether a given Minnesota trade area is underserved.

State density is a screen, not a conclusion. Several websites rank the states, but none states the year of its data, so those rankings were dropped. A study should compute density for the subject's county from the same County Business Patterns release and then move to a named competitor inventory, where the real supply evidence lives. Our note on absorption forensics shows how to test a supply claim against what opened and closed.

Most new quick-service units are built around the drive-thru, and cities are writing that lane into their codes. Saint Paul, Minnesota proposed raising its fast-food stacking minimum from 4 spaces to 12, with 14 for coffee, setting lanes at least 60 feet from residential uses, and it considered prohibiting new fast-food and coffee drive-throughs outright [^67,68]. A Burbank survey of nearby cities found Glendale requiring 200 feet of queue for fast food with a drive-through [^70].

The lane sets the site, and the site sets the cost. The dual-lane chicken pads in the cost table show how a long stack turns into acreage. A study that forecasts drive-thru sales should first show the site plan meets the local stacking rule and then test peak-hour throughput against the queue the plan actually holds.

Wastewater is the other entitlement item. Most sewer authorities require an outdoor gravity grease interceptor for new food-service establishments and size it themselves: an older Chino Hills, California sheet set limits of 500 and 2,500 gallons [^72]. The interceptor is a line in the budget, a constraint on the site plan and a recurring cost in the pro forma.

Several site norms were not found in any primary source: parking ratios, minimum acreage and frontage, utility loads, and the authority for a Phase I environmental assessment, health permit or certificate of occupancy on a quick-service loan. Those belong on the lender's checklist as items to confirm locally rather than as published standards.

Technomic, the industry's reference classification, segments limited-service chains by menu, with fast casual tracked as its own tier. The menu matters to a lender because each category carries a different box, cost, volume and SBA history, and in 2025 the categories moved in opposite directions. The multi-unit franchisee is a borrower type that spans every menu, and its credit question is leverage, not a site.

Five sections follow on this page: chicken, Mexican, pizza and delivery, sandwich and sub shops and the multi-unit platform. Coffee shops and drive-thru coffee chains are covered on the coffee shop hub.

Menu system

Burger Restaurants

The largest category by sales and the one with the most published unit economics, from brand Item 7 ranges to company P&L disclosures, alongside the most visible franchisee distress of 2025 and 2026. The page covers how a burger unit earns, what it costs to build and how lenders read the brand's SBA record.

Chicken is where the industry's volume records are. Chick-fil-A ran 3,287 U.S. units in 2025, 3,219 of them franchised or licensed, on systemwide sales of $23,918 million and an average unit volume of $7,700 thousand [^23]. Its 2026 disclosure counts 2,863 company-operated and franchised restaurants, with licensed units on top [^74], and the freestanding and drive-thru group of about 2,302 units averaged $9,161,239 in sales against a median of $9,087,673 [^22]. Raising Cane's ran 913 units on $5,487 million and an average of $6,580 thousand; Popeyes ran 3,196 units on $5,698 million [^23]. Further down the scale, Chicken Salad Chick averaged $1,496K [^24], and KFC's sales in the Technomic ranking fell 5.2% in 2024 to $4.9 billion [^75].

Those numbers describe systems, not the unit a lender is asked to finance. Neither Chick-fil-A nor Raising Cane's publishes an Item 7 range that our research could find, so their costs are visible only through permits, and their volumes are not a benchmark for the chicken brands most franchisees actually operate. Wingstop's domestic average is about $2.0 million [^76], a fraction of the leaders'. A study that cites the category's headline volumes for a mid-tier franchise has borrowed someone else's sales.

Wingstop shows the growth model and its strain. The brand added 382 net U.S. units in 2025 [^23] and a record 493 net openings worldwide [^77], and digital orders were 73.2% of system sales in the fourth quarter [^78]. Domestic same-store sales fell 5.8% in that quarter [^78], and 2026 guidance was revised to a range of up to 485 net new units worldwide [^79]. Wingstop puts its average investment at about $580,000, excluding real estate and pre-opening costs, and more than 90% of its franchised openings come from existing franchisees [^76]. The borrower is therefore usually an operator with other units, and the study should read the new unit against them.

The SBA record is thin and split across data entries. One aggregator shows Wingstop at $71.9 million of SBA volume and a separate entry for the brand at $81.9 million with no charge-offs; the first entry carries 292 loans and an 11.9% charge-off rate [^80]. A credit memorandum that quotes either number alone has quoted half a brand. The study should reconcile the entries or pull the brand from SBA's own files.

Chicken sites are large. The permitted Chick-fil-A in the cost table sits on 1.39 acres with a dual drive-thru [^15], and Raising Cane's builds with two lanes as well [^17]. Two lanes need a long stack, which is where the local queuing code in the site section starts to decide the parcel. No named lender appears in any 2024 to 2026 chicken project record we found.

Mexican quick service is mostly one system. Yum's Taco Bell Division ended 2025 with 9,030 units worldwide [^81], and the U.S. business had 7,784 restaurants, 7,124 of them franchised [^26], on sales of $17,247 million [^23]. Yum expects a U.S. restaurant-level margin of 24% to 25% in 2026 and has set a target of a $3 million average unit volume by 2030 [^26]. The traditional freestanding Taco Bell has no Item 7 in the evidence we found, and a competing franchisor reports that Taco Bell's Item 19 gives no average unit sales [^54]; the only Item 7 available is for Taco Bell Express, at $287,950 to $857,700 [^13].

That disclosure gap shapes the lending. Yum owns only 8% of U.S. Taco Bells [^81], and many of the rest are held by large multi-unit franchisees, several of them shown in the multi-unit platform section, and our research found no SBA loan data for any Mexican brand. A single-unit Taco Bell loan is therefore unusual, and a study supporting one has to build the sales case from the trade area and the competitor set because the franchisor gives it no published distribution to land on.

Chipotle is the rival every Mexican study meets, though it is fast casual and franchises nothing. It ran 3,938 restaurants in 2025 on $11,679 million of sales [^23], opened 334 [^82], and guided to as many as 370 openings in 2026, most with a drive-up pick-up lane [^83]. It funds the build from its own cash, with capital spending of $666.3 million in 2025 [^14]. For a lender the relevance is competitive and valuation-related: a Chipotle opening near the subject changes the trade area, and Chipotle leases with long terms remaining were listed at an average 4.81% at the end of 2025 [^45], a reference point for the corporate end of the lease market rather than for a franchisee's pad.

The smaller Mexican systems show where the category breaks. Moe's Southwest Grill ran 568 units in 2025 [^24]. Qdoba, also fast casual, had 841 restaurants in the first quarter of 2026 and raised $442.2 million through asset-backed notes [^84]. Del Taco is the cautionary one: Jack in the Box bought it for $575 million to $585 million in 2022 and 2023 and sold it for $119 million at the close in December 2025 [^50,51], after a franchisee's Chapter 11 had closed 18 of its Colorado units [^85]. A franchisee lender who underwrote a Del Taco unit on the strength of its parent was lending against support that was sold at a fraction of its cost.

Pizza and delivery is the one category where the leading brand publishes nearly everything a study needs. Domino's had 7,186 U.S. stores in 2025 and added 172 [^23], though its own disclosure counts 7,236 U.S. outlets and 6,974 franchised ones at December 28, 2025 [^20]; the two counts are taken on different bases and should not be mixed in one table. U.S. sales were $9,953 million [^23]. Item 19 covers 6,655 franchised traditional stores, which averaged $27,267 a week against a median of $26,059 [^4], and it adds the cost structure of the weakest stores, as the operating benchmarks show.

The build is the cheapest in quick service, $231,450 to $743,500 for a traditional store [^10], with up to $73,000 of additional funds for the first three months [^20]; the 2025 FDD put furniture, fixtures and equipment at up to $145,000 [^21]. A low build is a mixed blessing: almost all of the cost is leasehold work and equipment inside someone else's building, so a failed store leaves used ovens and a lease. Without owned real estate, a 504 loan has nothing to finance, and a 7(a) loan rests on the operator's cash flow and guarantees. Third-party delivery also competes for the same orders [^6], which is why weekly sales, not square footage, is the sizing measure.

The segment as a whole is shrinking. Pizza and delivery chain sales fell 0.3% in 2025, a third straight year of deterioration, while Domino's grew and Pizza Hut's sales fell 8.2% [^6]. Papa Johns identified about 300 underperforming North American restaurants to close by the end of 2027, targeting units more than ten years old with volumes under $600,000 [^86,65], and Pizza Hut was closing about 250 U.S. units under its turnaround plan [^65], and Yum agreed in June 2026 to sell the brand [^87]. A study on a pizza site has to show where the subject sits against those closure profiles, because an older, low-volume store in the same system is the comparable a lender will reach for.

The SBA record is the deepest of the folded categories. Domino's franchisees took 51 SBA loans for $63.0 million in 2025 at an average rate of 9.21% [^88]. Smaller brands show the spread: Jet's Pizza at $43.9 million of SBA volume with an 8.5% charge-off rate, and Rosati's Pizza at $39.7 million with 25.4% [^80]. A brand with a charge-off history several times its peers' is a credit fact in its own right, separate from anything the study finds about the site.

The category's center of gravity is moving between two brands. Subway ended 2025 with 18,773 U.S. stores, all franchised, after a net loss of 729 during the year [^89,90], and it has closed a net 8,345 U.S. stores since 2016 [^89]. Its average unit volume was $490 thousand in 2025 [^25]. The franchisor's profit rose while its royalty base shrank: franchise revenue fell from $818 million in 2024 to $767 million in 2025 [^91], and net income reached $688 million in 2025 [^90]. Most closures come at lease or franchise-agreement expiry, at about 4% of domestic locations a year [^92].

Jersey Mike's is the growth side. It ended 2025 with 3,227 U.S. stores after adding 238, on sales of $4,217 million [^25,93]. Its 2026 Item 7 puts the initial investment at $436,176 to $1,162,228, of which up to $49,250 is paid to the franchisor [^11,12], and it financed the system with a $760 million whole-business securitization in February 2026 [^94]. Jimmy John's ran 2,777 U.S. stores after adding 88, and Firehouse Subs 1,276 [^25]. Arby's counted 3,265 U.S. stores in the ranking [^25] and 3,458 restaurants with about $4.4 billion of systemwide sales in the collateral for its 2026 securitization [^95].

For a lender the format is the issue. Most sandwich units are inline storefronts with modest volumes, so the collateral is a leasehold and the loan depends on the operator. A study has to show that the subject's sales clear the franchise fees, rent and debt service with a margin, and it has to check the lease term against the franchise term, since Subway's pattern shows that the date a lease expires is the date many stores close.

The SBA histories are long and they diverge. Subway franchisees have taken 6,172 SBA loans [^80], and Subway loans in 2025 averaged a rate of 9.29% [^88]. Jimmy John's shows 881 SBA loans for $419.1 million and Firehouse Subs $197.1 million since FY2010 [^96]. At the far end, one aggregator lists Cousins Subs: 121 loans, $21.4 million of SBA volume and a 24.6% charge-off rate [^80]. Jason's Deli averaged $2,745K per unit [^24], a reminder of how widely volumes range inside one menu category.

The largest franchisees are companies in their own right. Flynn Group led the 2026 Restaurant 200 with $4.938 billion of revenue, including 1,321 Pizza Hut and 340 Wendy's restaurants [^97]; the 2025 edition's print list had put its 2023 revenue at $4.6 billion [^98], and the trade editions disagree with one another on its 2024 figure. KBP Brands, fourth, had $1.670 billion of revenue and 673 KFC restaurants [^97]. Haza Group ran 557 units, 414 of them Wendy's; Diversified Restaurant Group ran 328 Taco Bells [^99]; and MIC Glen ran 356 Taco Bells [^3].

Their capital structures look like any leveraged buyout's. When Bain Capital agreed to buy Sizzling Platter in 2025 [^100], the financing was first marketed as a $905 million package, including a $505 million first-lien term loan and a $175 million revolver [^101]. It closed as a $425 million first-lien term loan at SOFR plus 500 issued at 97 and an $80 million delayed-draw term loan, alongside senior secured notes [^102]. By the first quarter of 2026 the company's leverage was 6.4x as adjusted EBITDA margins contracted [^27].

The distress cases of 2025 and 2026 came from this tier. A large Wendy's operator and Hardee's and Burger King operators filed for bankruptcy in those two years [^103,104,107,108]; the burger restaurant page names the operators and what their filings mean for a borrower's other debt. FAT Brands filed with $1.45 billion of securitized debt in January 2026 [^105], and its liquidating plan eliminated about $1 billion of debt when it took effect in July [^106]. Whole-business securitizations show where the structural tripwires sit: cash is trapped at coverage below 1.75x and 1.50x, and rapid amortization starts below 1.20x [^109,84].

A study for a platform lender asks which units carry the portfolio and which drain it, how exposed the platform is to one brand's decline, and whether cash flow after remodel and development commitments covers the debt at today's rates. When a platform sells units to a smaller franchisee, the unit-level history the platform reports is the seller's history for the coverage test under SOP 50 10 8.1, so it needs the same scrutiny as any seller's books. Our note on franchise and non-franchise credit sets out how the brand relationship changes the lender's recovery.

  1. The trade-press average used as the subject's sales

    A ranking's average unit volume spans every format and age the brand runs, and it is submitted by the brand. GAO found that 74 of 170 loans to one franchise's franchisees defaulted, that a loan agent had given borrowers exaggerated revenue projections, and that at the time franchise disclosures were not required to show first-year revenue [^110]. The fix is the brand's Item 19, at a named percentile, reconciled to the trade area.

  2. Item 7 treated as the project budget

    Item 7 stops at the boundary the franchisor draws. Land, shell construction, site work, impact fees and the grease interceptor are often outside it, and metals tariffs moved costs after mid-2025 [^18]. A budget needs each excluded layer named and priced from a dated source.

  3. One aggregator's brand loss rate quoted as fact

    Secondary analyses of the same SBA files put limited-service defaults at 10.3% and 18.9% [^33,32], and one aggregator splits a single brand into two entries with different loss histories [^80]. A rate without its denominator and cohort is not evidence; the register and SBA's own files are.

  4. Brand eligibility assumed

    The Directory listing and franchisor certification described under capital routes decide eligibility before any market work [^40]. A study that skips them can be thorough and still describe a loan SBA will not guarantee.

  5. A purchase argued from the buyer's forecast

    The historical coverage test set out under capital routes leaves projections no room to carry an acquisition outside the owner-occupied special purpose exception [^9,37,113]. A study that leads with the buyer's growth case answers a question the rule no longer asks.

  6. Drive-thru sales forecast on a lane the code will not allow

    A forecast built on a site plan that fails the local rule, or that cannot hold the peak hour, overstates the unit before it opens.

  7. A lease cap rate used to value an operating business

    Corporate-backed leases price near 5.90% and franchisee leases near 6.87% [^44], and a brand can lose most of its value while the buildings stand, as Del Taco's sale showed [^50]. Capitalizing an owner-operator's income at a corporate lease rate overstates the collateral.

  8. The system's direction left out

    Subway closes stores mainly at lease and agreement expiry [^92], and Papa Johns is closing older, low-volume units [^86]. A study that does not place the subject against its own brand's closure pattern misses the most likely way the unit fails.

  9. Size and eligibility not documented

    The Inspector General found SBA had not validated size eligibility on 48 of 64 loans it reviewed [^111], and SBA agreed to flag 7(a) loans approved from August 2023 to May 2025 for eligibility review at guaranty purchase [^112]. A multi-unit borrower near the size standard needs that evidence in the file, because a denied guaranty purchase falls on the lender.

The checklist falls into three groups. Eligibility comes first and is binary: the Directory listing, the franchisor certification, size and ownership, all confirmed before any trade-area work. The franchise documents come second, because they hold the cost range, the sales distribution, the fee load and the obligations that fall due during the loan. The site documents come last and are local: the stacking rule, the interceptor and the lease.

A Phase I environmental assessment, a health permit and a certificate of occupancy are left out of the table on purpose: our research found no program authority that makes any of them a required document for a quick-service loan, so the lender's own policy decides whether they join the file. The appraisal row asks for two values because no published norm divides a restaurant's price between property and business.

Both cases put a freestanding franchised drive-thru with a dual lane on a commuter arterial and test it for an SBA 7(a) lender, in the same sequence: the Franchise Directory listing and the franchisor's executed certification, trade-area demand, the Item 19 and franchise-load read, an explicit ramp, and coverage against the SBA floor. The Arkansas case is a single-unit franchise of a national drive-thru brand in an outer-ring Northwest Arkansas submarket, on the smaller of the two budgets. The Georgia case is a franchisee-sponsored new build in an exurban submarket of metro Atlanta, on a busier road and a larger budget.
