A hotel has no lease. Its income is set one night at a time by the rate it can hold against the hotels down the road, and its expenses, labor above all, do not fall when occupancy does. A lender is underwriting special purpose real estate with little alternative use and an operating business that depends on a flag, a manager and a competitive set. The feasibility study joins the two: it has to show that the subject can take a defensible share of a measured pool of room nights, at a rate that its segment supports, with a margin that leaves coverage after franchise fees, labor and reserves.

The national numbers explain why lenders read that argument closely in 2026. Room revenue was flat to falling for much of 2025, and hotels nationally ended the year with their first full-year RevPAR decline since 2020 [^4]. Then the first half of 2026 turned: U.S. hotels sold 11.4 million more room nights than in the first half of 2025 and took in $5.4 billion more in room revenue [^5]. A national average that swings that way says little about one site; the subject's competitive set, penetration path and list of competing openings say a great deal.

This page sets out the national evidence such a study rests on as of October 2026, from cost per room and margins to loan performance, capital routes, the penetration method and supply. Seven segments, from roadside limited-service to luxury and resort hotels, are treated in the subtypes section, and the worked cases are listed under cases.

Tariffs moved from threat to schedule

Trade policy reaches a hotel budget through furniture, fixtures and equipment. A procurement firm reported tariffs of 145% or more on China-origin hotel FF&E at the April 2025 peak [^8]. The regime that followed was narrower and more durable: a Section 232 proclamation set a 25% tariff on upholstered wooden furniture and on kitchen cabinets and vanities from October 14, 2025, with scheduled increases to 30% and 50% respectively deferred to January 1, 2027 [^9], and softwood timber and lumber carry a 10% duty under the same action [^10]. For a project opening in 2027, the FF&E line should be priced at the scheduled rates unless the order is placed and landed before they apply. The hard-cost contingency hotel lenders expect in 2026 is set out in the Bastrop case; a tariff exposure the contingency must absorb should be named.

Margins: three years of compression

CBRE's Trends sample of hotel operating statements shows gross operating profit slipping in 2025 from 35.1% of revenue in 2024, with EBITDA down too [^14]; the Bastrop case carries the 2025 margins. The squeeze came from labor: cost per occupied room for labor rose 2.9% while leisure and hospitality wage rates rose 4.2% [^14]. Revenue grew mostly outside the rooms department, with rooms revenue up 1.1%, food and beverage up 4.3%, other operated departments such as parking, retail, spa and golf up 7.8%, and miscellaneous income, which includes resort and destination fees, up 10.9% [^14]. Management fees rose 3.8%, pulled up by resort and luxury hotels [^14]. A rooms-only hotel captures little of that ancillary growth.

A second benchmarking provider shows how much the labor line depends on the hotel's labor model. HotStats measured payroll at 43.0% of revenue in unionized hotels in 2025, against a non-union ratio that the Bastrop case cites and then sets aside for a fixed payroll [^15], after payroll per available room rose 5.5% in the first quarter of 2025 [^16]. In food and beverage the dependence is starker: labor made up 59.4% of department expenses in the first half of 2025, by CBRE's count [^17]. A study that applies a national margin without first placing the subject in the right labor model, segment and service level will misstate coverage, and the error is rarely small.

The trade association's own figures put the industry's payroll in scale. AHLA estimates hotel wages and benefits at $128 billion in 2025 and projects $131 billion for 2026, against projected guest spending of $805 billion in 2026; it also puts 2025 gross operating profit per available room at roughly 90% of its 2019 level after adjusting for inflation [^18]. Those are national aggregates, useful for direction only.

Franchise costs

For a flagged hotel, the franchise agreement takes a share of room revenue before any other expense. Wyndham's 2025 annual report describes a royalty of about 5% of gross room revenue plus 2% to 4% for marketing and reservations [^19]. RLJ Lodging Trust reports its branded hotels paying royalties of 2.0% to 6.0% of room revenue plus 1.0% to 4.3% in other franchisor fees [^20]. A franchise disclosure document can also anchor the rate assumption for an economy flag: the 2025 Ramada by Wyndham disclosure reports an average ADR of $84.29 and a median of $76.40 across 340 hotels for 2024 operations [^21]. Our note on franchise and non-franchise economics tests a flag's fee load against the rate premium it buys.

Local labor rules

Wage ordinances can move a market's expense base faster than any national survey records. Los Angeles set a hotel minimum wage of $22.50 an hour in 2025 for hotels of 60 or more rooms, scheduled to rise to $25 in 2026, $27.50 in 2027 and $30 in 2028 [^22]. A referendum to stop it gathered 84,007 valid signatures against the 92,998 it needed, so the schedule stands [^22,23]. A hotel study in a city with a hotel-specific wage rule must model the schedule year by year, because the ordinance, not the labor market, sets the floor.

What the evidence does not give

No source in our evidence publishes 2025 to 2026 management fee norms, a total operating expense ratio for U.S. hotels, or a months-to-stabilization curve. A study takes the fee from the proposed management agreement, the expenses from the operator's or a comparable hotel's statements, and the ramp from the competitive set's history.

Lenders often ask how hotel loans have performed in SBA's programs. SBA itself publishes no table by industry. One SBA loan broker counted 699 hotel businesses with 7(a) loans totaling $1.8 billion in 2025, an average of $2.6 million, without saying whether the year is fiscal or calendar [^24].

The loan-level file itself is the better record. Over the ten fiscal years to 2025, SBA approved 8,388 7(a) loans to hotels and motels for $19,545.7 million, and 2,620 loans under 504 for $5,784.1 million, counted as approvals in NAICS 721110, hotels and motels other than casino hotels. Among loans approved in fiscal 2011 to 2020, cohorts old enough to have run their course, 2.0% of hotel 7(a) loans were charged off, counted loan by loan, against 6.7% for all 7(a) loans; by dollar amount the hotel rate was 1.0%. The 504 picture is the reverse: 2.1% of hotel loans under 504 were charged off by count, against 1.2% for all loans under 504, and 1.5% by amount.

CMBS: the stress sits at the top and at maturity

Hotel loans in commercial mortgage-backed securities are the most closely tracked hotel credit, and in 2025 to 2026 they show strain without collapse. S&P measured the lodging delinquency rate at 6.67% in April 2025, the highest since February 2022, with the lodging special servicing rate at 8.9% [^26]. Trepp's lodging rate was 5.81% in September 2025 [^27], 7.31% in March 2026 [^28], 5.35% in July 2026 [^29] and 6.18% in September 2026 [^3]. Morningstar DBRS put the hotel special servicing rate at 8.31% in July 2026 [^30]. Across all property types, Trepp's overall rate was 7.86% in July 2026 [^29], and KBRA measured overall 30-day delinquency at 7.7% and its broader distress rate at 10.3% in September 2026 [^31].

KBRA found that upscale and upper-upscale hotels made up 41.4% of outstanding lodging CMBS balances in March 2026 but 72% of the delinquent balance, $1.6 billion in the upscale class and $2.7 billion in upper upscale [^32]. Its overall lodging delinquency rate was 6.4%, against 6.5% a year earlier and 5.3% two years earlier [^32]. When CMBS loans fail, recovery is poor: liquidations in July 2026 totaled $661.6 million with $317.3 million of realized losses, an average loss severity of 47.96% across property types [^30]. A study that never tests the refinance at maturity answers half the lender's question.

SBA 504 and 7(a)

SBA lends on hotels as operating businesses, not as investment property, and applies a test written for lodging: the business must earn more than 50% of the prior year's revenue from transients who stay 30 days or less at a time; a start-up shows it in projections [^33]. That makes the study's segment mix an eligibility document as well as a market one. Hotels, motels and other lodging facilities appear on SBA's list of special purpose properties [^33], and that classification sets the equity. A 504 borrower contributes 10% of project cost in the standard case, 15% when the property is special purpose or the business is new, and 20% when both apply [^34], so a new-build hotel with a first-time operator starts at the top tier. SOP 50 10 8, the June 2025 edition, also treated hotels as special purpose property needing a going-concern appraisal [^35].

Size is the other limit. The 504 debenture tops out at $5 million for a hotel, since the higher $5.5 million limit applies only to manufacturing and energy projects [^36], and a 7(a) loan to $5 million [^33], on which the guaranty is capped at $3,750,000 [^37]. Since July 4, 2026 a borrower and its affiliates can hold up to $10 million across the two programs together, when the 7(a) loan is approved first and the 504 loan second; Policy Notice 5000-879058, effective July 4, 2026 [^38]. A start-up 7(a) projection must reach 1.15x coverage, and a 504 project is tested at 1.15x [^33].

Acquisitions are tested on the seller's record. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, a change of ownership must meet 1.25x on historical cash flow, and an independent quality of earnings review applies to acquisitions of $3 million or more, with an exemption for Owner Buyout, ESOP & Cooperative, and any acquisition of an owner-occupied Special Purpose Property [^33]. An owner-operated hotel purchase often falls inside that exemption, so the lender leans on the appraisal and on the study's reading of the seller's results against its competitive set. Whether a study goes into the file is first the lender's decision, and the regulation leaves room for SBA to ask: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^39]. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice covers how we prepare studies for SBA lenders.

USDA Business and Industry

USDA's Business and Industry guarantee lists tourist and recreation facilities, including hotels, motels and bed and breakfast establishments, as eligible purposes in rural areas only, and an owner's living quarters must be deducted from the loan proceeds [^40]. USDA's fiscal 2026 annual notice set the guarantee at 85% for loans under $5 million and 80% for loans of $5 million and above, for loans obligated through September 30, 2026 [^81]; no fiscal 2027 notice was found, so a lender should confirm the current guarantee percentage with the USDA state office; in June 2025 the OCC described it at 80% with an initial fee of 3% of the guaranteed amount [^41]. The application rules are explicit: an independent feasibility study is mandatory for a new business when the guarantee exceeds $1,000,000, a pro forma balance sheet and affiliate statements apply above $600,000, and a Department of Labor review applies when a guarantee over $1,000,000 funds a project adding more than 50 direct jobs [^42]. Our USDA Rural Development practice covers those studies.

Banks, debt funds and CMBS

Conventional lenders size hotel loans on coverage and debt yield before loan-to-value. For 2026 a trade publication expected bank loan-to-cost of about 65%, with leverage starting at 55%, rates around 6.75% and coverage from 1.25x [^43]. Debt-yield floors for conduit loans ran at 9% to 10% for limited-service hotels and 10% to 11% for full-service and resort hotels in the second quarter of 2026 [^44]. Newly underwritten lodging CMBS loans in 2026 carried a debt yield of 12.69% and coverage of 2.12x [^2]. A study for a construction loan should show the stabilized debt yield at the takeout, which is what the takeout lender sizes to. Our conventional and institutional practice covers those studies.

EB-5, C-PACE and franchisor capital

Large hotels sometimes carry EB-5 capital as equity or mezzanine debt; our EB-5 practice and the Nevada hotel case show how a study supports it. C-PACE tranches sit behind the senior loan on several financings described in the segment sections, and franchisors lend selectively to their developers: Wyndham's 2025 guidance included about $110 million of development advance notes [^45]. Each adds a claim on the same cash flow, and the coverage table has to show all of them.

What the evidence does not give

We found no named USDA Business and Industry hotel transactions with dollar amounts, no franchisor lending program for new hotels and no lender survey of recourse terms. A study leaves those terms to the lender's term sheet.

Transaction volume rose in 2025 [^46], as the Florida case also records. LW Hospitality Advisors' survey of sales above $10 million put the average at $243,000 per room in 2024 [^47]; the Bastrop case cites the same survey's 2025 average.

For a feasibility study the valuation question is narrower than a sale price. A lender wants to know what the stabilized hotel will be worth as a going concern at the date it must refinance, and whether that value supports the takeout at the leverage and debt yield of that day. The appraiser answers it with an income approach on the study's own projection, so the study's stabilized year, expense load and reserve for replacement flow straight into the collateral value. A study that inflates the stabilized year inflates the value, and the reviewing appraiser will test it against the comparable sales above.

Real estate investment trusts own the larger branded hotels and publish the best public operating data for them. The industry is large in employment and taxes. What the evidence does not give

We found no 2025 census of U.S. hotels and rooms from STR or CoStar, no published share of supply that is franchised against independent, and no unit counts for the operators ranked sixth to tenth. A study should not imply a national share for independents; the boutique and independent section below sets out what can be said.

A study divides that pool by segment, grows it and allocates the subject a fair share and a penetration path; our note on the benchmarking report a hotel study rests on sets out the index mechanics. Two steps of the method get particular attention on this page. The first is the set itself: hotels of similar chain scale, product and location, each named and justified. The second is the re-run for new supply: when a competitor opens, the market's room count rises, every fair share is recalculated, and the base-year penetration indices are held while the shares are normalized back to the whole market.

Rating agencies read hotel collateral the same way. KBRA's 2024 presale for a securitized hotel portfolio reported an ADR of $205.64 and RevPAR of $153.16 for the twelve months to September 2024, with penetration of 97.0% for rate and 98.4% for RevPAR and an occupancy index above parity, and sized the debt at a KBRA loan-to-value of 87.2% [^55]; the Bastrop case reads the portfolio's occupancy figures. Those hotels were filling slightly more than their share of rooms at slightly less than their share of rate. A new hotel's index path should start below 100 and reach the set's level only for a stated reason: a newer product, a stronger flag or a better location.

What drives 2026 demand

The national demand drivers in 2026 are uneven. JLL named the World Cup and America250 events in host cities as tailwinds, with weak international inbound travel, labor costs and tariffs as headwinds [^46]. Inbound international visitation fell 3.1% year over year in July 2025 by CBRE's count [^56], and international visits in June 2026 were close to flat on a year earlier, up only 0.2% despite the tournament [^12]. A study in a host city should model the event year as an event and show the year after.

What the evidence does not give

No primary publisher issues a national rooms-per-capita, visits-per-household or bed-need benchmark for hotels, and none publishes a capture rate a study could borrow. A study that sizes demand from population ratios is using a method lenders do not recognize for hotels. The comp set's own history is the evidence.

The pipeline is shrinking in total and moving up-market. Lodging Econometrics counted 5,975 projects at June 30, 2026, down 4.9% from a year earlier, with 1,081 under construction and 2,147 projects and 245,871 rooms scheduled to start within twelve months [^1,58]; the Bastrop case gives the pipeline's room counts. Yet new announcements and starts both rose in the second quarter, and conversions of existing hotels to new flags reached a record 1,567 projects with 152,044 rooms [^1]. Counting renovations with conversions, 2,097 projects and 255,834 rooms were in the works [^59]. A conversion adds no rooms but is new competition: a repositioned hotel down the road can take share as surely as a new build.

Openings: the trackers disagree

CoStar counted 749 hotels with 79,116 rooms opened in 2025 and, in January 2026, projected 891 hotels with 99,011 rooms for 2026 and 1,688 hotels with 191,926 rooms for 2027 [^62]. Lodging Econometrics counted 277 hotels with 31,416 rooms opened in the first half of 2026 and in July forecast 661 hotels for the full year, followed by 738 hotels in 2027 and 832 in 2028 [^1,58], or 78,909 and 88,321 rooms [^63]. The rooms and supply growth behind Lodging Econometrics' 2026 forecast are in the Bastrop case. CoStar's own supply forecast for 2026 is 0.4% [^12], and HVS projected 0.8% [^7]. The trackers count differently; a study names the one it uses and builds its own list of competitive openings from permits, franchise announcements and site visits.

Where the pipeline is deepest

By market, Dallas held 183 projects in the second quarter of 2026, Atlanta 157, Nashville 122, Austin 116 and Phoenix 115 [^58]. Commentary through 2025 and 2026 describes rate pressure in several of those markets, but no agency publishes a list of saturated hotel markets. A study in one of them should show the competitive openings year by year and re-run the fair-share allocation for each.

What the evidence does not give

We found no pipeline counts by Census region, no national count of hotel closures for 2025 to 2026, and no state lodging license counts that would capture owner-run properties without payroll.

A hotel study has to place its subject in a segment before it can choose its comparables, because benchmark data are published by chain scale and lenders price risk the same way. STR and CoStar sort branded hotels into six chain scales by average rate, from economy through luxury, with independent hotels as a seventh category. "Limited-service", "select-service" and "full-service" are service-level terms that map roughly onto those scales; extended-stay is a length-of-stay product that spans several scales; resort is a location; and boutique has no formal definition at all.

A limited-service hotel earns almost all its revenue from rooms: a breakfast area, a small market and perhaps a meeting room, but no restaurant. The brands that HVS found most built in 2025 were Hampton by Hilton, Holiday Inn Express, Tru by Hilton and Fairfield Inn and Suites [^6]. HVS reports their cost jointly with midscale extended-stay [^6]; the Bastrop, Texas case checks its budget against that median.

The economics were the weakest of any segment in 2025. In CBRE's sample, midscale hotels saw EBITDA fall 8.7% [^14], and the Bastrop case sets out the fall in gross operating profit and the rise in property taxes behind it. JLL measured midscale RevPAR down 2.8% and economy down 4.4% for the year [^46]. CoStar expects 2026 growth in these scales to come from demand rather than rate [^12], so a limited-service projection should lead with the room nights it can capture and keep rate growth modest. KBRA found that economy and midscale hotels saw the largest rise in CMBS delinquency since March 2024, though together they are only 6.3% of outstanding lodging loan balances [^32].

New limited-service projects are often financed as two flags on one site with layered debt. In 2025 a dual-brand Hampton Inn and Home2 Suites of 189 keys in the Nashville area closed $25.45 million of debt, a $15.55 million senior bank loan with a $9.9 million C-PACE tranche behind it [^64]. Upper-midscale projects alone made up 2,225 projects of the national pipeline at June 30, 2026 [^58], so the competitive openings list is rarely empty for this segment. The Bastrop, Texas case and the Florida case apply the method to new limited-service hotels under SBA 504.

No source publishes limited-service RevPAR, occupancy or margin as a property type, economy-segment occupancy, staffing per hotel or typical acreage. A study must take those from the competitive set's report and the franchisor's prototype.

Select-service hotels sit between limited-service and full-service: a lobby bar or bistro, some meeting space, and a rate at the upscale or upper-midscale tier. Their demand mixes business and leisure transient travel, and CoStar's 2026 forecast credits the segment's recovery to both [^12]. HVS reports a median cost of about $200,000 per room on 2025 budgets [^6], a figure HVS warns its survey is not built to compare with earlier years.

The best public benchmark is Apple Hospitality REIT, which owns rooms-focused select-service hotels. In the third quarter of 2025 its comparable hotels ran about 76% occupancy at an ADR of about $163, a RevPAR of about $124, and an adjusted hotel EBITDA margin of 35.2% [^65]. For the full year, comparable RevPAR was $118, down 1.6%, and adjusted hotel EBITDA was $474 million at a margin of 34.3%, with 2026 margin guidance of 32.4% to 33.4% [^66]. A study that projects a new select-service hotel at a margin above an established portfolio's guidance has to explain the difference.

Supply is the segment's main risk. Upscale projects alone numbered 1,282 in the pipeline at June 30, 2026, and upscale and upper-midscale projects together made up 59% of all pipeline projects and 53% of its rooms [^58]. Capital is available but expensive: one debt fund that focuses on limited and select service lends from $15 million at up to 85% leverage and rates of 7.50% to 10% [^43]. Recent projects pair two flags: a Home2 Suites and Hampton Inn in Nashville closed a $67 million construction loan in 2025 [^67], and a newly delivered 208-key Homewood Suites and Hampton Inn pair in Southern California, 116 and 92 keys, refinanced for $38.6 million [^68]. In CMBS, Courtyard carried the largest delinquent balance of any upscale brand in March 2026, $659.1 million, a delinquency rate of 23.3% [^32]. The Georgia case and the South Carolina case test new select-service hotels under SBA 504.

Extended-stay hotels sell rooms by the week as well as the night, to project workers, relocating employees, medical visitors and travelers on long assignments. Their suites carry kitchens and their housekeeping is lighter.

HVS puts the median cost of an upscale extended-stay room at about $265,000 on 2025 budgets [^6]; midscale extended-stay is reported jointly with limited-service.

The financing is familiar. A 122-key economy extended-stay hotel in North Carolina closed $10,389,471 of construction financing in August 2026 [^69], and a trade publication expects extended-stay to be among the segments with the most available capital in 2026 [^43]. The underwriting trap is eligibility. SBA's transient test counts revenue from guests staying 30 days or less [^33], so a hotel whose plan relies on monthly corporate or crew contracts has to show the mix still passes. The study's segment table is the place to show it. No source publishes an extended-stay margin, a separate midscale cost per room, staffing levels or closures.

A full-service hotel is several businesses under one roof: rooms, restaurants and bars, banquets and meetings, and often parking and retail. Its pro forma has to model each department with its own revenue driver and labor cost, because CBRE's 2025 data show non-room revenue growing faster than rooms [^14]. HVS puts the median full-service cost at $467,000 per room on 2025 budgets [^6].

Urban full-service markets diverged widely in 2025. New York City held an ADR of $333.71, with the occupancy and RevPAR the Florida case cites, while Houston's occupancy fell 8.6% to 58.9% [^4]. Host Hotels & Resorts, a REIT that owns full-service and luxury hotels, reported comparable RevPAR of $229.24 for 2025, total RevPAR of $382.83 and a comparable hotel EBITDA margin of 28.9% [^70], and guided 2026 RevPAR to $234 to $235 with margins of 29.6% to 29.7% [^71]. Those are portfolio results for urban and resort hotels of scale, a ceiling for a smaller full-service project rather than a target.

Labor is the risk lenders watch. In CBRE's sample labor exceeded 50% of operated-department expenses, and large convention and full-service hotels with labor-intensive formats face heightened scrutiny from investors [^14]. Group business has also become harder to forecast: CoStar reports group booking windows compressing from 30 to 60 days to two to three weeks [^12], which makes a group-heavy forecast less certain at the time of underwriting. KBRA measured upscale CMBS delinquency at 11.3% in March 2026, against a lodging average of 6.4% [^32]. The Nevada case applies the method to a full-service hotel financed with EB-5 capital. No source publishes a full-service margin as a property type, typical equity or staffing.

At the top of the market, the figures change scale. HVS put the median luxury development cost at more than $1,600,000 per room on 2025 budgets, and attributes rising luxury cost to projects with residential and other mixed-use components [^6]. HVS does not survey upper upscale separately; the full-service median is the nearest substitute. Luxury and upper upscale were the only chain scales with profit growth in CBRE's 2025 sample [^14], and luxury RevPAR rose 3% in 2025 by JLL's count [^46]. In June 2026 CoStar forecast luxury RevPAR growth of 5.3%, the highest of any chain scale [^72]; its August forecast puts luxury ADR growth at 5.9% and notes that households earning $200,000 or more account for 25% of travel spending while making up 11% of households [^12].

That demand has drawn supply. Lodging Econometrics counted record pipelines in both tiers at June 30, 2026: 367 upper-upscale projects with 65,021 rooms, and 103 luxury projects with 25,496 rooms, up 12% in projects and 21% in rooms on a year earlier [^1]. A newly opened 155-room luxury hotel on the California coast refinanced for $115 million in June 2026 on a five-year floating-rate non-recourse first mortgage [^73], the kind of structure this tier uses.

The credit record is the tier's warning. KBRA measured upper-upscale CMBS delinquency at 10.9% in March 2026; Hilton-brand upper-upscale collateral carried a delinquent balance of $1.8 billion, a rate of 30.1%, and Hilton Worldwide brands together $2.5 billion, a rate of 15.7% [^32]. The pattern is not new: in February 2023 upper upscale was already the worst class, at 8% with $806 million delinquent, and Sheraton collateral ran at 21.7% [^74]. A study in this tier must test the debt against a downturn in group and international demand, not only the base case. No source publishes a luxury margin, an upper-upscale cost per room, typical equity or loan-to-cost for these hotels.

A resort hotel earns from its setting: beach, mountain, golf, lake or destination city. Rates are seasonal and ancillary spending on food, recreation, spa and fees carries more of the revenue. In CBRE's 2025 sample, resort and all-suite hotels were the only property types to grow profits, on rate strength and ancillary spending [^14]. Host Hotels & Resorts reported fourth-quarter 2025 RevPAR growth of 4.6% driven by leisure transient demand [^75]. Destination markets can also turn quickly: Las Vegas ADR fell 4.3% in 2025 and RevPAR 10.9% [^4]; the Nevada case works from the market's levels. A resort study must show the demand seasons separately, because a strong peak can hide a shoulder that does not cover debt service.

Pricing rules now apply to the fees resorts rely on. The FTC rule on unfair or deceptive fees, effective May 12, 2025, requires mandatory fees to be included in the displayed total price; the FTC's own example shows a $199 room with a $39 resort fee that must be advertised as one total [^76]. A pro forma should treat the resort fee as part of the price guests compare.

Resort capital stacks are large and often mixed-use. A lakeside resort development in Missouri with a total cost of $495 million closed a $220 million senior loan with a $65 million C-PACE tranche in November 2025 [^77]. A luxury resort project near Orlando, built on 70 acres with 460 residential units sold and 277 condo-hotel units, closed a $101 million construction loan in June 2026 [^78]. A ski resort project in Colorado with 59 hotel rooms and 95 residences closed $482.5 million of financing in September 2026 [^79]. In each, residential sales or condo-hotel units carry part of the cost, and a study has to keep the hotel's own cash flow separate from the real estate sales that support the stack.

Resort is not the same intent as wellness. Properties whose income rests on spa treatments, retreats and wellness programming are studied on the wellness and retreat hub. USDA's Business and Industry rules include tourist and recreation facilities in rural areas among eligible purposes [^40], which is the one route by which a rural resort hotel may reach a federal guarantee. No source publishes a resort RevPAR, margin, pipeline count, cost per room or typical equity.

An independent hotel has no franchise system to supply reservations, loyalty members or a standard of performance a lender can look up. A branded hotel's study can lean on the flag's record in comparable markets; an independent's study has to prove distribution, rate and occupancy from the property's own history or, for a new hotel, from the competitive set and the operator's record. Boutique and lifestyle hotels add a design and service premium that has to be shown in rate, not asserted. STR places all non-affiliated hotels in its independent category, whatever their price. Soft brands sit between the two: a collection flag supplies distribution while the hotel keeps its name.

New boutique supply arrives mostly through conversion and renovation rather than new construction, which is why the record conversion pipeline in the supply section matters for independents too. A lifestyle hotel of 248 rooms in Minneapolis completed its renovation in October 2026 under a hotel management company [^80], a common path for an older building being repositioned. In rural areas USDA is open to independents: its Business and Industry rules list bed and breakfast establishments with hotels and motels and require an owner's living quarters to be deducted from the loan [^40].

The evidence for this segment is the thinnest on the page. We found no primary definition of boutique, no independent cost per room, RevPAR, ADR or margin, no typical equity, and no dated count of independent openings or closures. A study for an independent hotel should therefore carry its own operating history, three years where it exists, its channel mix and commission costs, and a competitive set that includes both branded and independent hotels. The Johnson City, Texas lodging retreat case shows how an unbranded lodging property is underwritten under SBA 7(a).

  1. A competitive set chosen to flatter the subject

    The comp set fixes the demand pool, the rate and the penetration path. Leaving out the closest competitor, or adding weaker hotels to lower the bar, produces a fair share the market will not deliver. A reviewer rebuilds the set from the map and the chain scale, so the study should explain each inclusion.

  2. Penetration above parity from opening day

    A new hotel ramps. An index above 100 in the first year, without a product, flag or location reason, overstates income when coverage is thinnest, and rating agencies read the same indices [^55].

  3. An event year treated as a new base

    World Cup and America250 demand is real in 2026 host cities [^46]. A study that capitalizes an event year's RevPAR into the stabilized year lends against a one-time spike.

  4. A national margin applied to the wrong labor model

    Payroll took 43.0% of revenue in unionized hotels in 2025, by HotStats' count, a ratio that depends on the labor model [^15], and midscale profits fell while luxury profits rose [^14]. A margin borrowed from a sample unlike the subject misstates the cash available for debt service.

  5. A supply list that stops at "under construction"

    Conversions and renovations, a record in 2026 [^1,59], add competition without adding rooms, and two national trackers disagree on 2026 openings [^1,62]. Listing only hotels under construction misses the competitor that reflags down the street.

  6. The transient test left unanswered

    An SBA hotel loan depends on the business earning more than 50% of the prior year's revenue from transients who stay 30 days or less at a time; a start-up shows it in projections [^33]. Extended-stay and contract-heavy projections that never show the revenue split leave the lender unable to confirm eligibility.

  7. Franchise and capital costs left out of the pro forma

    Royalty, marketing and reservation fees can take several points of room revenue [^19,20], and a franchise renewal or change of flag brings a property improvement plan. Fees left in a footnote overstate net operating income.

  8. No test of the refinance

    About $18.7 billion of hotel CMBS debt across 596 loans falls due in 2026, and lenders were underwriting new lodging loans at a debt yield of 12.69% [^2]. Five years of projections without the debt yield at maturity leave the largest risk unmeasured.

  9. Cost from a survey median, not from bids

    HVS says its medians move with the sample and should not value a specific project [^6], and the furniture and casework tariffs step up on January 1, 2027 [^9]. A budget needs dated bids and a contingency that names the tariff exposure.

The checklist falls into three groups. Eligibility documents decide whether a program can lend at all: the revenue split for SBA's transient test, the rural location for USDA and the zoning approval for both. Valuation documents set the collateral: the going-concern appraisal and the study whose stabilized year the appraiser capitalizes. Commercial documents hold the study's assumptions: the franchise and management agreements set the fee load, and the bids set the cost the loan must cover.

A file that holds the study without the benchmarking report behind it, or the franchise agreement without the improvement plan, is not ready for committee.
