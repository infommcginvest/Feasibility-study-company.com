A credit officer handed a mixed-use rent roll is looking at two or three businesses under one roof: apartments leased by the year, storefronts leased by the decade, perhaps an office floor or a parking deck. Each of those incomes answers to a different market, and every federal program and agency decides for itself how much of the secondary income may carry the loan. That decision, more than the building's form, sets the loan amount, the leverage and the lender that will make it.

The lines are specific. SBA's rules have the operating business occupy 51% of an existing building and 60% of a new one, and the 504 program may not fund speculation or investment in rental real estate [^5,20]. USDA's Business and Industry program accepts apartments only inside a mixed-use facility that passes a business-revenue test [^8]. Fannie Mae takes 10% off commercial income and then trims net commercial income to 20% of effective gross income wherever it runs higher [^6]. HUD's Section 221(d)(4) limits reach the market through lender term sheets that disagree with each other [^10,11]. A building can cross one of these lines when a lease expires or an owner grows into more space, so a projection that folds every income line into one net operating income cannot tell a lender which program the building fits, or for how long.

The category gives a study little else to lean on. The research behind this page found no NAICS code for mixed-use, no SBA, USDA or HUD loan category for it and no published demand ratio. The definition planners cite comes from the Urban Land Institute: three or more significant revenue-producing uses, physically and functionally integrated and built to a coherent plan, with projects that combine fewer uses classed as multi-use or duo-use [^21]. Most buildings that reach an SBA or USDA lender, a storefront or an office with apartments above, would not meet it. The evidence has to be assembled one use at a time.

The 2025 and 2026 record gives each half of a typical building its own evidence. Apartments absorbed 365,900 units in 2025 against a five-year average of 370,000 [^1], and RealPage counted 519,000 market-rate units under construction in the third quarter of 2025, which it called the fewest in more than ten years [^22]. Shopping centers absorbed 708,000 square feet in the second quarter of 2026 [^2], after first-quarter absorption that was negative for the third year running [^23]. Conversion adds supply of its own, with 193,900 apartment units in the adaptive-reuse pipeline at the start of 2026 [^3]. Securitized lenders keep meeting mixed-use in their distress reports: KBRA counted it at 13.6% of newly distressed loans in September 2026 [^24], and Trepp's overall delinquency rate counting performing matured balloon loans stood at 9.62% in July 2026 [^4].

The first segment is the apartment building with some commercial space on the ground floor. Agency lenders finance it as multifamily as long as the commercial share stays inside their limits, and while it does, the multifamily hub covers its underwriting; this page picks it up where the commercial income crosses the cap. Fannie Mae's guide, in the version effective August 3, 2026, subtracts 10% of actual commercial income and then reduces net commercial income to 20% of effective gross income where the result is higher [^6]; the version effective August 20, 2026 carries the same rule [^25]. A retail tenant whose rent pushes past that line still pays it, but none of the excess sizes the loan. The HUD evidence is weaker. HUD's commercial limits for Section 221(d)(4) reach the market through lender term sheets, and an undated sheet from one lender and a March 2026 sheet from another state the income share differently [^10,11]; the HUD MAP Guide text was not found in the research behind this page. A study for a HUD loan should quote the limit its lender applies and where that limit comes from, rather than treat either sheet as settled.

The second segment is commercial-led: a retail center, an office building or a downtown block where residential or hotel income supports the project without defining it. Banks, CMBS lenders and life companies finance these as retail or office, with no agency cap and with the lender's own coverage and leverage limits [^7]. This is the segment that keeps appearing in rating agency distress reports [^24], for reasons the review failures section takes apart, and it is where a study's commercial demand case does most of the work.

The third segment is owner-occupied, and it is the SBA segment: an operating business buys or builds premises and leases the rest, residential space included. SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates governs applications received by SBA on or after October 1, 2026 [^5]. The residential-space language the research quotes is from the June 2025 edition, SOP 50 10 8, and a study should check it against the 8.1 text its lender applies. That edition let the owner of an existing building zoned for both commercial and residential use occupy the residential space or rent it to a third party, applied the occupancy limits whether the leased space went to a commercial or a residential tenant, barred loan proceeds from improving space leased to others and allowed at most one year to reach the required occupancy [^9]. Residential space for the business itself was allowed where a resident owner or manager was required, within a ceiling of its own [^9]. Apartment buildings are not eligible, and a passive landlord can borrow only as an Eligible Passive Company that leases the whole property to an Operating Company, which must guarantee the loan or co-borrow [^9,26]. USDA's Business and Industry guarantee sits beside this segment for rural projects, with a revenue test in place of an occupancy test [^8].

The test that places a project in a segment is arithmetic on the subject's own rent roll and floor plans, and it has to be run for every year of the projection, not only the stabilized one. A ground-floor lease that expires in year three, apartments that lease up ahead of the storefronts, or an owner that grows into upstairs space can each move the share across a program line. When that happens after closing, the loan was sized under one set of rules and is serviced under circumstances another program would have treated differently. The study is the place to show the share year by year, with the square footage measured on the same basis as the appraisal.

For SBA 7(a) and 504 the mixed-use question is a measurement, and the two editions of the SOP frame it differently. SOP 50 10 8.1 sets the shares in the table [^5]; the residential-space language that tells a study how apartments upstairs are treated is the June 2025 text of SOP 50 10 8 [^9], and the study has to say which reading of that language its lender applies. Either way, the floor plan decides the program: a business that measures below the share on the appraisal's basis has no SBA route, however strong its cash flow. Once the share is met, the 504 contribution turns on the building as much as the business, at 15% for a new business or a limited or special purpose property and 20% when both apply [^5]. The regulation leaves the study itself to SBA: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^37].

In a rural town the same building can go to USDA instead, and there the test is revenue, not floor area. Apartments and residential housing are ineligible for Business and Industry guarantees except in mixed-use facilities that pass a business-revenue test, which the research found stated only in a USDA training deck that carries no date [^8]; the study quotes the test as the lender applies it under the rule in force, and shows the business share of projected revenue in every year, since apartments can fill faster than the storefront. The site must lie outside any city or town of more than 50,000 people [^15], and the guarantee and fee terms in the table come from the FY2026 notice [^16]. Our USDA Rural Development practice and the note on the five USDA components cover the rest of the program.

Residential-led projects go to the agencies or to banks. Agency execution works while the commercial share sits inside the cap and stops being efficient once it does not: the income above the line still carries operating risk but no longer carries debt. HUD's Mortgagee Letter 2025-02 gives the market-rate Section 221(d)(4) thresholds as 85% of cost and a coverage ratio of 1.176 [^35]. In the second quarter of 2026 CBRE reported multifamily loan-to-value at 63.3% and commercial loan-to-value at 59.6% [^7], so a building that tips from residential-led to commercial-led can lose leverage as well as agency terms. In the Federal Reserve's July 2026 survey a moderate net share of banks eased standards on nonfarm nonresidential loans and a modest net share on multifamily loans, while construction and land development standards were basically unchanged [^38]; a mixed-use construction loan sits in that last category. Our conventional and institutional practice covers agency and bank execution.

Historic tax credits are equity, not debt, and they come with their own timeline: the federal credit applies to certified historic structures rehabilitated for income-producing use, owner-occupied residential use does not qualify, and the State Historic Preservation Office and the National Park Service review the work against the Secretary of the Interior's Standards before certification [^36]. The main-street section covers how that shapes a study.

The appraisal of a building with shops below and apartments above starts by splitting the net operating income the way the lender will: the residential stream, the commercial stream, and any parking or other income. Each stream takes a rate from its own sector evidence. The research found no broker or appraiser survey that publishes a cap rate for mixed-use as a category or by quality tier, and the CBRE survey it records for the first half of 2026 found sector rates broadly flat while the ten-year Treasury yield peaked at 4.67%, with neighborhood retail among the sectors leading compression [^17]. Tables of retail and multifamily rates by class circulate online without a disclosed method, and this page does not print them.

A storefront whose rent Fannie Mae would trim [^6] counts only the capped part of that rent in the loan the agency sizes. The reconciliation then weighs the two streams against sales of comparable small buildings in the subject's own market, where any exist, and states how much of the value rests on each use.

The transactions that reach the news are a poor guide to that reconciliation. A 768,000-square-foot mixed-use center in Kansas City sold for $63.3 million in September 2026 [^39], and another Kansas City shopping district sold in 2024 for $175.6 million, having changed hands for $660 million in 2016, with a large redevelopment since proposed [^40]. Both are large district-scale centers, and a cap rate or price per square foot borrowed from them imports a different market into a small building's appraisal. For an SBA loan, Pursuit Lending, a CDC, advises that the appraisal state the proposed owner-occupancy percentage and that the application's square footage match it [^41].

Two shortcuts should be refused. The first is consumer preference surveys: NAR's 2026 survey found 63% of residents in the 50 largest metros would pay more to live within walking distance of parks, shops and restaurants [^43], which supports a rent premium for a walkable location but says nothing about how many units or square feet a trade area can absorb. The residents upstairs are a small share of a storefront's customers, and the shoppers on the street are not the renters; each use's trade area has to be drawn on its own evidence, as our note on why the trade area is the study argues. Once the cases are run, the program cap from the lender segments decides how much of the secondary income enters loan sizing.

Town center

A retail-led district with residential and office around it, underwritten as retail by banks and CMBS lenders.

The adaptive-reuse apartment pipeline held 193,900 units at the start of 2026, with office the largest former use in it [^3]. Our research note on office repurposing carries the national office-to-apartment count, the metros with the largest pipelines, the incentive programs and the geometry and basis tests that decide whether a conversion pencils. The same RentCafe count gives figures for metros the note names without one, such as Los Angeles at 4,340 units, and for others it does not cover: Denver at 2,991, Philadelphia at 2,697, Atlanta at 2,642 and Cincinnati at 1,770 [^46]. This section covers what a lender's study adds to the note.

Conversions are financed with bridge or private construction debt, not agency execution, until the building is converted and leased. The 2026 record shows where that debt gets stuck. In Boston, Bisnow reported in August 2026 that financing problems had left most proposed conversions stalled, and one source it quoted put 75% of a conversion's cost in doubt until the permit is issued [^47]. In Washington a planned conversion of an office building to about 500 apartments, approved by the zoning commission in March 2026, had a lender pursuing foreclosure by late September; the report gives no cause [^48]. In Phoenix a conversion of a former bank tower stalled almost finished and went into foreclosure before its lender bought it back at auction in 2023, and another developer completed it, opening in October 2026 [^49]. None of the three reports blames a shortage of renters: Boston's turns on financing and cost certainty, Washington's on a foreclosure without a stated cause, and Phoenix's on a stall that took years and a new developer to finish.

That shifts the weight of the study. The building comes first. Developers prefer older buildings with narrower floor plates because more of each floor gets daylight [^50], and plumbing risers, structure and facade condition decide cost. The research found no published lender rule requiring a structural engineering report for conversions, but a lender that does not hold one before closing is pricing a structure it has not seen. Cost evidence is deal-level only: no source publishes a conversion cost per unit, so the study rests on the subject's own engineering and bids, with a contingency set after the structural survey.

The market case is the familiar apartment case with two adjustments. The converted units lease against new construction and the concessions it offers [^12], and the carry during conversion has to be funded until the takeout lender's coverage test is met. Where the conversion keeps retail on the ground floor, the stabilized building becomes a residential-led mixed-use building, and the agency commercial cap applies at takeout [^6]. A study should show the takeout coverage with the capped commercial income, not the full rent roll, and our note on coverage-constrained takeout covers that test.

The typical project is the rehabilitation of a historic income-producing building with ground-floor commercial space and apartments on the upper stories [^51]. The federal historic tax credit applies to qualified rehabilitation costs for certified historic structures; owner-occupied residential use does not qualify, and the State Historic Preservation Office and the National Park Service review the work against the Secretary of the Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation [^36]. Certification comes in three parts, covering the building's significance, the proposed rehabilitation and the completed work [^52], and the third comes only after construction, so a lender should expect the last certification after completion and size any bridge to that date.

The program's annual report shows how much of it is housing. In fiscal 2025 the National Park Service approved 1,170 proposed projects and certified 939 completed ones representing about $9.6 billion of investment, and it counted 11,544 new housing units and 10,390 rehabilitated ones, 53% of them for low- and moderate-income households [^53]. No source in the research publishes a per-project cost for main-street rehabilitations, a credit pricing benchmark or any lender underwriting or failure data specific to them; those are stated gaps, and the study fills them from the subject's own bids, its credit investor's letter of intent and local rent evidence.

The financing route depends on who occupies the storefront. If the owner's own business takes the ground floor, the SBA rules apply, renting the apartments to third parties does not lower the share the business must occupy (see lender segments), and the study has to measure whether the business reaches the required percentage on the space it occupies. In a rural town, USDA's Business and Industry guarantee can work instead if business use leads the building's revenue [^8]. If the storefront is leased to others, the building is investment real estate and goes to a bank, with any credit equity alongside.

The study's particular concerns are timing and constraints. The Standards limit what can be done to storefronts, windows and interiors, which affects both cost and the rentable layout of the commercial space. And the residential rents should be tested against what the trade area pays for older units, not new construction, because the rehabilitated units compete on character and location rather than on amenities.

The International Building Code treats a live/work unit as a dwelling with a workspace inside it, classified in Group R-2. The unit may not exceed 3,000 square feet, the nonresidential area may not exceed half of it and must be on the first or main floor, no more than five nonresidential workers may be present, and storage may take no more than a tenth of the nonresidential area [^54]. Jurisdictions amend these limits: Victoria, Texas sets its own size ceiling and residential-area limit and moves the unit into Group R-3 [^55]. A study should quote the local code as adopted, not the model code, because the classification drives fire separation, accessibility and cost.

The research found no agency or SBA guidance specific to live/work units and no lending benchmark for them. The nearest treatment is residential: the units are leased as dwellings, so an agency lender would read their rent as residential income inside the building's commercial cap, but that is an assumption the study has to confirm with the lender, not a published rule. For an SBA borrower the question is harder. A business operating from one live/work unit in a larger building may not occupy enough of the property to qualify, and the June 2025 SOP allowed residential space for a business only where a resident owner or manager was required [^9].

Demand evidence is equally thin. No source counts live/work units or measures their absorption separately from apartments. The study therefore runs the residential case for the units as apartments, tests whether the trade area supports the premium a workspace commands, and presents the work space as optional value rather than as a separate commercial income line. The mixed occupancy itself has to be resolved in design: the code allows accessory, nonseparated, separated and live/work approaches, alone or combined [^56], and the choice changes cost.

The ULI definition of mixed-use development lists a hotel among the revenue-producing uses a project can combine [^21]. In lending, the hotel is a different animal. In the August 3, 2026 version of its guide, Fannie Mae counts income from short-term rental units as commercial income inside its 20% cap [^6], and full hotel components otherwise fall outside agency multifamily execution, so a residential-led project with a hotel may need separate hotel financing or a commercial loan for the whole. Trepp put CMBS lodging delinquency at 5.35% in July 2026 [^4], a rate for lodging loans in securitized pools rather than for hotel floors inside small mixed buildings.

The research found no published requirements specific to the hospitality component of a mixed-use project: no agency rule on franchise agreements, management agreements or competitive-set reports for it, and no benchmark for how shared parking, lobbies and amenities are allocated. The study therefore treats the hotel as an operating business with its own demand case (room nights by segment, a named competitive set, occupancy and average rate ramp, franchise and management fees) and its own debt test, and it documents the shared-use agreement that governs common areas, which Fannie Mae's guide already requires where a property shares facilities [^57].

The hotel hub covers lodging demand and hotel finance in full, and our note on reading a hospitality STR report covers the data a competitive-set analysis uses. What the mixed-use study adds is the interaction: whether the hotel's guests support the ground-floor retail, whether residents and guests share amenities without one use bearing the other's costs, and whether the lender sizing the residential or retail loan has excluded hotel income or capped it.

ICSC publishes no town-center class. Its nearest formats are the lifestyle center and the community center, defined by leasable area and site acreage, and for the lifestyle center a trade-area radius, in a classification standard that carries no date [^58]. A town center adds residential, office or hotel uses to that format, sometimes on separate parcels, sometimes in vertical buildings around a retail core.

Banks, CMBS lenders and life companies underwrite town centers as retail or horizontal mixed-use [^18], and their record sits in the CMBS mixed-use series that the review failures below take apart, a series that moves with a small number of large urban loans. The Kansas City shopping district described in the valuation section, sold in 2024 and since proposed for redevelopment [^40], is a district-scale example.

A study for a town center has to work at district scale. The retail trade area is measured in miles, not blocks, and anchor and junior anchor commitments decide the in-line leasing. Residential phases can lease ahead of the retail core and change the income mix the lender sees in each year, so the study should present the mix by phase. Where parcels are financed separately, each loan should be tested on its own parcel's income, with reciprocal easement and shared parking agreements in the file. The retail hub covers shopping-center demand and tenancy in depth.

  1. Blended income sized past the program cap

    The study projects one net operating income for the whole building and sizes the loan on it. Fannie Mae counts net commercial income only to 20% of effective gross income after a 10% deduction [^6], and SBA and USDA test occupancy and business revenue [^5,8], so the loan the study implies can exceed the one the program allows. The remedy is coverage shown twice, once on the capped income and once on the full rent roll.

  2. Occupancy asserted, not measured

    An SBA file states an occupancy share without floor plans or a measurement basis, or measures it on a different footing from the appraisal [^41]. The Inspector General's 2010 audit of 504 lenders found a file that did not substantiate the occupancy requirement [^59], and in March 2026 it reported that SBA agreed to flag 7(a) loans with incomplete eligibility screening for review at guaranty purchase [^60]: an occupancy share nobody measured can be questioned years after closing. A study that measures occupied and leased space by year, on the appraisal's basis, closes this.

  3. A national distress rate read as a default probability

    Mixed-use delinquency figures swing with single loans. KBRA put mixed-use CMBS delinquency at 9.8% in March 2025, up from 7.9% [^61]. Its mixed-use distress rate fell 419 basis points in June 2025 after one portfolio loan was modified, a month after rising 292 basis points [^62]; in August 2023 mixed-use delinquency rose 82 basis points after a 201-basis-point jump in July, with one Manhattan loan of $215 million newly 30 days delinquent [^63]. Trepp's mixed-use special servicing rate was 11.90% in June 2026 [^18], and Fitch's overall rate was 3.37% at the end of 2025 [^19]; the three publishers measure differently and on different dates. These rates describe large urban securitized loans. A study that applies one to a small building, in either direction, has misread it.

  4. Refinance risk at maturity ignored

    In June 2026 the five largest newly delinquent CMBS loans, one of them a Minneapolis mixed-use tower, accounted for $998.9 million of the $2.64 billion that went delinquent that month [^64]. A study that tests coverage only at origination never meets the lender who has to take the loan out at maturity. The exit should be tested at the rates and the commercial cap a refinancing lender would apply.

  5. Comparables from the wrong market

    The appraisal or study borrows cap rates or prices from large district-scale centers, such as the Kansas City sales described above [^39,40], to value a small building. Those sales priced large districts. Small-building value has to come from small-building sales and income capitalized line by line.

  6. A one-year residential lease-up

    The pro forma fills the apartments in twelve months without concessions, when nearly three in four recent lease-up units were offering at least a month free [^12]. When the storefronts are also still leasing, an optimistic residential ramp overstates coverage in exactly the years when the commercial income is lowest.

  7. Ground-floor space with no named demand

    The commercial income is projected at a market rent with no tenant, no letter of intent and no evidence of how long similar space took to lease, and no published benchmark for ground-floor stabilization exists to fall back on. A vacant storefront is not a rounding error in a small building: it can be the difference between the program it qualifies for and the one it does not.

  8. Conversion cost set before the structure is known

    The 2026 conversion reports from Boston, Washington and Phoenix describe stalled financing, cost left open until the permit, a foreclosure and a years-long stall, none of them a shortage of renters [^47,48,49]. A conversion study without a structural survey, a post-survey contingency and a funded carry period is underwriting the market and skipping the building. Our note on why feasibility studies fail review covers the general patterns.

  • Floor-area schedule by use and an income-share or occupancy schedule for every projection year, measured on the appraisal's basis; for SBA, an appraisal stating the owner-occupancy percentage and an application whose square footage matches it, as Pursuit Lending, a CDC, advises [^41].
  • For SBA real estate held by a separate entity, the lease from the Eligible Passive Company to the Operating Company and the guaranties the regulation requires [^26].
  • All material commercial leases with the lender's review, tenant estoppel certificates and subordination, non-disturbance and attornment agreements [^57].
  • Shared-use agreements where uses share facilities, and condominium documents where the building is divided into a mixed-use regime [^57].
  • A market study and an appraisal [^57], with the market study running a separate demand case for each significant use, as this page recommends.
  • A property condition assessment, a Phase I environmental site assessment with an environmental indemnity, a seismic risk assessment where the hazard applies, and flood insurance evidence [^57].
  • Zoning evidence, including legal non-conforming status where relevant, a survey, title and certificates of occupancy [^57], and the building's code classification for its mixed occupancies [^56].
  • The property management agreement, and a ground lease estoppel where the building sits on leased land [^57].
  • A contractor's bid dated after mid-2026, broken out by use and by shared structure, with tenant improvement and leasing budgets for the commercial space (this page recommends the date because of the cost movement above).
  • For a conversion, a structural engineering report and a contingency set after it; no published lender rule requires the report, so its absence should be a stated condition rather than an oversight.
  • For a historic rehabilitation, the National Park Service Part 1, Part 2 and, at completion, Part 3 certifications, with the credit investor's commitment [^52].
  • For a hotel component, the franchise and management agreements and a competitive-set report; the research found no agency rule requiring them for mixed-use, so they are lender requests.
