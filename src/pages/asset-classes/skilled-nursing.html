Feasibility Study Company prepares independent skilled nursing feasibility studies, with the market study inside, for owners buying or refinancing a facility, developers adding or replacing beds, and the lenders that will rely on the result. The study is written for the lender's credit committee and for the sponsor who must meet its conditions, and it is built to the same standard whichever of them asks for it. The firm is an independent analyst, not a lender, broker or investment advisor, and the study states the determination the evidence supports, including a negative one.

What you receive

  • A written study that ends in a stated determination for the project as proposed and, where the numbers fail, as restructured.
  • Demand sized the way the state sizes it: the planning area's bed-need formula, then the occupancy, star rating and operator of each named competitor, and the hospitals that refer patients.
  • Revenue built payer by payer, with Medicaid days at the current state rate, Medicare days at the federal per diem, managed care at contract rates and private pay at local prices, and staffing built from scheduled hours and local wages.
  • Debt service coverage in a base case and under stress through the ramp, not only at stabilization.

A study covers the purchase or refinance of an operating facility, a change of operator, an expansion, a replacement building, new beds where the state allows them, short-stay rehabilitation and rural and critical access facilities. Private-pay assisted living and memory care, and independent living and life plan communities, are studied on their own hubs. The study is not an appraisal. HUD's draft valuation chapter puts a complete market study inside the appraisal for new construction, written by the appraiser, and accepts a certificate of need or a market need and feasibility study acceptable to its healthcare office as the need test [^40,41]. This study is the sponsor's own independent analysis, which the appraiser and the lender can read alongside those documents.

Use the briefing request on the home page: request a methodology briefing and name the facility's state, the project (purchase, refinance, expansion, replacement or new beds) and the lender or program. The reply sets out the analytical framework, the deliverable that program requires and the next steps. If the facility's records are sensitive, generate a confidentiality agreement first.

A skilled nursing facility is a licensed health care provider that happens to sit in a building. Most of its revenue is a rate set by a government: Medicare for short post-hospital stays, Medicaid for the long-stay residents who fill most beds, and managed care plans that negotiate off both. That makes it a different asset from its neighbors on this site. Assisted living and memory care is private-pay residential care, priced against what families can afford. Independent living and life plan communities sell housing and services to residents who mostly do not need nursing. A nursing facility study answers a narrower question: can this license, with this payer mix, under these state and federal rules, carry this debt?

The national cost reports show why that question is hard. In 2024 freestanding facilities earned 24.4% on fee-for-service Medicare, yet their margin on all other business combined was negative and their all-payer total margin was about 2% [^1]. Medicaid paid for 64% of days at the median facility, while traditional Medicare covered 8% of days and 13% of revenue [^1]. The profitable payer is the small one. A pro forma that grows the Medicare share without referrals to support it overstates coverage before the building is discussed.

The second fact is that new nursing homes are rarely built. MedPAC writes that capital in this sector is less likely to finance new construction than to update or buy existing facilities, because of state certificate of need laws [^1], and HUD's Section 232 program, the largest long-term lender, made 329 loans in fiscal 2025 that were refinances or acquisitions [^36]. Most studies therefore test an operating facility: a purchase, a refinance, a change of operator, an expansion or the replacement of an old building whose beds already exist.

The third is that the documented losses in this sector come from operators and payers, not from oversupply. The largest recent failure was an operator bankruptcy, real estate trusts have written off rent from tenants that raised going-concern doubts, and HUD's own Inspector General is auditing how the agency watches its nursing home book [^56,57,44]. This page sets out the October 2026 evidence for each part of a lender-grade study, from reimbursement and licensure and capital routes to three project types and review failures.

What is not published, and how the study fills it

No primary source gives a 2025 to 2026 skilled nursing cost per bed by U.S. region, a land share, an equipment share or the project size SBA and USDA lenders typically finance. A study therefore builds the budget from the subject: the land contract or appraisal, a contractor's bid priced to the current Weitz city index or a local equivalent, equipment quotes, and soft costs itemized from the design, licensing and financing steps. The median freestanding facility has 100 beds and the largest tenth have 176 or more [^1], while Alabama's state plan bars any new freestanding home under 50 beds [^18], so the bed count itself may be fixed by rule before any market analysis begins.

Development cost matters less in this asset class than in most, for a structural reason. In the 34 states plus the District of Columbia that run certificate of need programs for nursing homes, and the 3 more with bed moratoria [^1], a developer cannot simply add supply. New buildings usually carry beds bought or transferred from another license in the same planning area. Cost therefore enters most files as a replacement-cost test (would a buyer pay more than it costs to build?) or as the budget of a replacement facility, not as the opening line of a new market entry.

MedPAC's March 2026 report is the starting point for every operating line because it reads the cost reports facilities file with Medicare. It describes a sector that is profitable on one payer and loss-making on the rest. Freestanding facilities earned an aggregate 24.4% on fee-for-service Medicare in 2024, a quarter of them above 33.9%, for-profit facilities 27.2% and facilities of 100 to 199 beds 26.5% [^1]. Yet the all-payer total margin was only about 2%, two in five facilities lost money overall, and the margin on everything except traditional Medicare was negative [^1]. The Medicare margin is a margin on a minority of the business.

Payer mix decides the pro forma

At the median facility Medicaid paid for 64% of patient days and fee-for-service Medicare for 8% [^1]. The study's revenue line is therefore built payer by payer: Medicaid days at the state's current rate, supported by the rate letter or the published methodology; Medicare days at the federal per diem as adjusted for the facility's case mix and area wage index; managed care days at the contracted rates; and private-pay days at local prices. Private pay is small but visible. A 2025 survey put the median semi-private room at $314 a day, $9,555 a month or $114,665 a year, and a private room at $361 a day or $10,965 a month [^4]. NIC's market data show a higher average of $420 a day across freestanding nursing care in its 99 markets in the first quarter of 2026, up 27.5% since the first quarter of 2019 [^5]. One is a consumer survey, the other a metro operator series; they should not be blended.

Quality is a revenue line

Occupancy tracks the federal star rating. Across fiscal 2025 cost reports, 1-star facilities averaged 77.2% occupancy and 5-star facilities 87.6%, and operating margins rose with the rating as well [^6]. A star rating also gates capital: HUD's Express Lane adds a Five-Star screen for nursing facilities [^37]. A study of an acquisition should show the subject's inspection history and rating trend and how the forecast census depends on them, bearing in mind that the HHS Inspector General found widespread underreporting of serious falls [^59].

What the public operators show

The two largest listed operators give scale benchmarks, not ratios to copy. PACS Group reported fiscal 2025 adjusted EBITDA of $505 million on revenue of $5.29 billion, with occupancy of 89.1% across its facilities and 94.9% at mature ones [^9]. Ensign Group drew 95.6% of revenue from skilled nursing [^8]. Mature-facility occupancy near 95% describes a turnaround operator's seasoned portfolio, not a stabilized occupancy for a single facility.

Labor and related parties

No public source gives a payroll share of revenue, an operating expense ratio or a management fee norm for nursing facilities. The nearest published proxy is the labor-related share of the federal per diem, which CMS set at 72.0% for fiscal 2027 [^11]; it describes how the rate is wage-adjusted, not what a facility spends. MedPAC reports a median nursing staff turnover of 46% in 2024 and sector wage growth of 2.8% that year [^1], so the study builds staffing from scheduled hours by position, local wages and agency use. MedPAC also warns that reported margins may be understated because over three-quarters of facilities pay related parties such as management companies and landlords, and it cites an Illinois study that found reported profits were only 32% of actual profits [^1]. A lender should see every related-party agreement and expenses restated at market terms.

The license and the certificate of need

A nursing facility operates under a state license and, to bill Medicare and Medicaid, a federal certification and provider agreement; almost all freestanding facilities are dually certified [^1]. In certificate of need states the license comes with a fixed number of authorized beds, and adding, moving or replacing beds requires the state's approval. The study should name the planning area, its bed inventory and approved projects, and the state official who confirmed them, as HUD's draft valuation chapter asks of the appraiser [^40]. Beds from a transfer or purchase are a loan condition, not background.

Ownership changes add a second gate. When a nursing facility is sold or its operator changes, the provider agreement and the license follow state and CMS change-of-ownership procedures. No SBA guidance on the Medicare change of ownership or provider agreement assignment was found, so the study sets out the timetable and its cash flow gap. CMS also suspended indefinitely the nursing facility ownership revalidation deadline in December 2025 [^1], which leaves less public ownership data for the study to check.

Medicare: a per diem that rises each October

Medicare pays a skilled nursing facility a daily rate under its prospective payment system, adjusted for each patient's clinical characteristics and the local wage index. For fiscal 2027 CMS finalized a net update of 2.4%, a 3.3% market basket less a 0.9 point productivity adjustment, effective October 1, 2026 [^12]. MedPAC had recommended a 4% cut and CMS did not adopt it [^1,12]. The fiscal 2026 update was 3.2%, worth about $1.16 billion [^13]. A pro forma applies the update in force and treats later years as assumptions.

Medicaid: the payer that sets the floor

States spent $46.3 billion on fee-for-service Medicaid nursing home care in 2024, up 9.0% [^1], and MedPAC reports that 45 states raised rates in fiscal 2024 [^1]. The 2025 budget law changes the outlook. It cuts federal Medicaid spending by about $1 trillion over 10 years by the Congressional Budget Office's estimate and lowers the provider tax cap in expansion states from 6% to 3.5% by fiscal 2032, with nursing facilities exempt from that phase-down [^15]. State budgets are already moving: Idaho imposed a 4% across-the-board provider rate cut for fiscal 2026 that was likely to extend into 2027, and Colorado reversed a 1.6% increase [^16]. A study shows the rate methodology, the current rate letter and coverage with the Medicaid rate held flat.

Staffing as a cost, not a federal minimum

CMS repealed the federal minimum staffing standard, including the 24-hour registered nurse requirement and 3.48 hours per resident day, by an interim final rule of December 2, 2025, effective February 2, 2026; the 2025 budget law separately barred enforcement until 2034, and the pre-2024 rule requiring a registered nurse for eight consecutive hours a day returned [^14]. The facility assessment requirement stayed in place [^14]. That removes a federal cost floor but not state minimums or the market wage; the study models staffing to state rules and the subject's acuity, with agency labor shown.

Physical plant

CMS requires an emergency generator for nursing homes that provide life support, with no waiver, and the essential electrical system must meet the highest level of the generator standard [^17]. No published national norm covers parking, acreage, frontage, stormwater or utility loads for nursing facilities, and we found no published plan-review durations, so a replacement or expansion schedule should be built from the state's own review record.

HUD Section 232: the dominant long-term lender

Every source agrees that HUD's Section 232 program had a record fiscal 2025, but they count it differently, and a study should cite one count and say which. MedPAC, citing HUD, reports $6.0 billion insured across 337 projects, up from $3.2 billion and 220 projects in fiscal 2024 [^1]. A trade publication counts 337 loans [^36]. The counts come from different tabulations; HUD's own production report was not in the evidence.

The mix is the more useful fact. Of the 337 loans in fiscal 2025, 329 loans were 232/223(f) acquisitions or refinances and only seven were new construction or other types [^36], and in the first half of the year all but three of 228 applications were 223(f) [^37]. The largest lender closed $1.38 billion [^36]. HUD therefore mostly retires earlier debt on operating facilities. The research found no published 2026 bank or bridge loan terms for nursing facilities, so the debt HUD refinances is undocumented; a study for a bridge lender should show the coverage the facility needs to reach HUD's terms.

HUD has also made the program cheaper and faster. It cut the annual mortgage insurance premium to 0.25% for all FHA multifamily insurance programs in a notice of September 23, 2025 [^39]. In June 2025 it opened an Express Lane for low-risk 232/223(f) refinances: at most 70% loan-to-value, coverage of at least 2.0x on trailing twelve-month NOI for the skilled nursing portion and 1.6x for any other portion, an operator in place for at least two years, special uses no more than 20% of revenue, no defaults by controlling participants, and a mortgage of no more than $50 million, or $70 million in the New York City area [^38].

The standard 223(f) loan-to-value and coverage terms for nursing facilities were not found in a primary HUD document; undated lender websites publish them, and the study should take them from the lender's current HUD term sheet.

What HUD asks of the market study

For new beds HUD's need test is a state certificate of need, or a state-commissioned or alternative market need and feasibility study acceptable to its healthcare office [^41]. Its valuation chapter, in a Revision 2 draft whose final wording may differ, puts a complete market study inside the appraisal for new construction, substantial rehabilitation and 241(a) loans, written by the appraiser [^40]. The draft expects a defined primary market area, a map of all seniors and nursing properties existing or planned, referral sources, Medicaid rate letters and monthly cash flows to stabilization [^40]. It sets no minimum occupancy threshold [^40].

The program is also under review. HUD's Inspector General reported in April 2026 that HUD did not always address the risks reported in its borrowers' audited financial statements, with defaults measured as of June 2024 [^43]. A separate ongoing audit covers the nursing home book [^44].

SBA 7(a) and 504 under the October 2026 rules

SBA lending is governed by SOP 50 10 8.1 with Technical Policy Updates, effective October 1, 2026 [^46]. Its predecessor, in force from June 1, 2025, made businesses licensed as nursing homes or assisted living facilities that provide healthcare or medical services eligible, made unlicensed residential facilities without healthcare services ineligible, and told the lender to consider the terms of the license [^45]. It also treated a management agreement that gives the manager sole discretion as making the applicant an ineligible passive business: the applicant must approve the operating budget and significant spending, control the bank accounts and employ the staff [^45]. The research did not confirm that wording in the October 2026 SOP, so the lender should check the current text; our note on what SOP 50 10 8.1 changed covers the transition.

A nursing home fits SBA's definition of special purpose property, a limited-market property with a unique physical design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts its utility to the use for which it was built [^46], and lender and CDC guides list nursing homes among SBA's examples; the SOP page itself was not part of this evidence. The classification sets the 504 contribution at 15%, or 20% for a new business, against 10% in the standard case [^46]. For a 7(a) purchase, equity is 10% and coverage of 1.25x is measured on historical EBITDA over total post-transaction debt service, on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two [^46]; for an owner-occupied special purpose purchase whose price the appraisal fully supports, the floor may be met on projections [^46]. Combined 7(a) and 504 borrowing can reach $10 million [^46], still below the average HUD loan. On the study, the regulation is unchanged: 13 CFR 120.160(b): SBA may require professional appraisals, a survey, or a feasibility study [^47]. Our SBA 7(a) and 504 practice area has the full rules.

Who owns the buildings: two estimates

Investor ownership is where the evidence disagrees. MedPAC cites a 2023 study that found real estate investment trusts owned about 9% of facilities and private equity about 5%, and a 2024 study that put REITs at 16% and private equity at 13% [^1]. The studies used different years and definitions, and owning real estate leased to an operator is easy to count differently from owning the operator. A study should cite both and not average them. For underwriting, the point is structural: many facilities separate the property owner from the licensed operator, so the lender may be financing a landlord whose income is a lease, or an operator whose largest cost is rent. Each needs its own coverage test.

The competitor list in a study should therefore name each facility's operator, not only its building.

A nursing facility study sizes demand the way the state regulator does, then checks it against the market. The basic formula is the population of each age cohort times a bed-need ratio per thousand residents, or a use rate in patient days, divided by a target occupancy, less the licensed and approved beds in the planning area. Lenders and HUD then test that result against current occupancy and payer mix. No published lender benchmark exists for a capture rate, fair share or penetration rate for nursing facilities; the state ratio and occupancy are the tools.

The state ratios vary widely

Mississippi calculates need by planning district with a separate ratio for each age cohort, rising steeply with age [^19]. Arkansas projects most of the calculated need and qualifies a county only where existing facilities are nearly full [^20]. Tennessee projects two years forward and subtracts licensed and approved beds [^21], Alabama uses one ratio for everyone aged 65 and over on a three-year horizon [^18], and Michigan's planners use utilization rates by age group and planning area instead [^22]. Florida's 16.1 benchmark is older and applies only to its retirement community exception [^23]. A study applies the subject state's own rule, with its cohorts and horizon, and never imports another state's ratio.

Cohort populations come from the Census Bureau's population estimates and state projections, beds from the state inventory, and occupancy and payer mix from cost reports.

Occupancy: three bases, one decision

Three national occupancy figures circulate and they differ because they measure different things. NIC's market data put freestanding nursing care at 86.7% in its 99 markets in the first quarter of 2026, the highest since 2016, against a series low in the first quarter of 2021 [^5]. A cost-report survey of about 11,800 facilities found a fiscal 2025 median of 84.8%, with nearly half at or above 85% and about a third below 75% [^6]. MedPAC's 2024 median was 83%, with the top quarter above 91% and the bottom quarter at or below 70% [^1]. NIC covers large metropolitan markets, the cost reports cover every filer, and MedPAC's figure is a year older. A study should pick the base that matches its subject's market, show the local facilities' occupancy from cost reports, and never average the three.

Regional spreads are wide. NIC's Pacific region ran at 91.9% in the first quarter of 2026 and the Northeast, Southeast and Mid-Atlantic near 89%, while the Mountain region was at 86.6%, the East North Central at 83.3%, the West North Central at 82.8% and the Southwest lowest at 76.0%, with several Texas and Oklahoma markets reporting lower occupancy [^5]. The recovery is also slowing: national occupancy rose 1.9 percentage points in 2025, after 6.3 points in 2024 and 10.3 percentage points in 2023 [^6]. A forecast that extends the recent gain is extrapolating a slowing rebound.

Age, payer and referral

The population aged 85 and over is projected to grow about 18%, or 1.22 million people, over five years, from 8% in Hawaii to 33.5% in Nevada [^6]. Growth of that cohort drives long-stay Medicaid demand. Short-stay demand follows hospital discharges and the managed care contracts that steer them, which the short-stay section covers.

Inventory is shrinking slowly

Freestanding nursing care inventory in NIC's 99 markets fell by 1,704 beds in the year to the first quarter of 2026, a decline of 0.2%, against 5,512 beds a year earlier, while 7,738 beds were newly occupied, down from 20,692 beds in the year to the first quarter of 2023; absorption has been positive for 18 consecutive quarters [^5]. A broker report put units under construction at a record low in early 2026, with occupancy above 87% and rent growth above 5% [^26]. A trade association counted 774 closures and 62,567 fewer beds between February 2020 and July 2024 and 40 more counties without a nursing home [^25]; it is an advocacy source and its figures should be read as such. Beds under construction by region are not public, so a study names its planning area's projects from the state's certificate of need log.

Request a methodology briefing

The categories in this asset class overlap. CMS divides facilities by setting, location and ownership, swing beds are a separate payment class, and NIC and HUD each use their own splits. A post-acute focus and a secured dementia unit are service lines inside the same license, and a replacement facility is a type of project. The study scope follows the project: short-stay rehabilitation turns on Medicare and managed care referrals, rural and critical access facilities on small volumes and special payment rules, and a replacement facility on bed authority, construction cost and the census it can keep through the move.

Memory care inside a nursing home is a fourth line that this page treats as part of the facility, not as a subtype. A secured dementia unit in a nursing facility operates under the nursing facility license, is mostly paid by Medicaid at the long-stay rate, and adds security, programming and staff training costs. No published source gives the incremental cost, rate or occupancy of a memory care unit inside a skilled nursing facility, so a study of one has to build those lines from the operator's own unit history and local private-pay rates, and say that no benchmark exists. Free-standing memory care, which is private-pay residential care, belongs on the assisted living and memory care hub, and memory care inside a life plan community on the independent living and CCRC hub.

How it earns. A short-stay unit admits patients after a hospital stay of at least three days, for up to 100 days of Medicare coverage, with the patient paying $217 a day from day 21 in 2026 [^1]. It earns the highest daily rate in the building because the federal per diem is weighted toward therapy and nursing needs. Traditional Medicare spent $31 billion on skilled nursing care in 2024, and covered days ran at 1,396 per 1,000 fee-for-service beneficiaries, with about 45 admissions per 1,000 [^1]. Those volumes are falling on a per-beneficiary basis: covered admissions per 1,000 fell in 2024 while days per admission rose [^1]. The unit's revenue is therefore a referral business. It depends on which hospitals discharge to it, how many of their patients the facility accepts, and which insurers pay for them.

The managed care shift. Managed care plans accounted for 42.3% of skilled nursing admissions in 2025, mostly Medicare Advantage, with Medicaid managed care the rest [^6]. The median Medicare Advantage stay was 25 days against 41 days under traditional Medicare, so a bed filled by Advantage patients must turn over far more often, roughly one and a half Advantage admissions for every traditional one, to hold the same census [^6]. Advantage plans also negotiate their own rates, which the research found no reliable public source for.

How lenders underwrite it. The programs are the same as for any nursing facility: HUD Section 232, SBA when the operator owns the business, and banks. The study adds three exhibits. The first is a referral analysis: hospital discharges to post-acute care in the trade area, the subject's share by hospital, and the concentration of that share, since a facility with one dominant hospital referrer carries that hospital's decisions. The second is the payer contract schedule, listing each Advantage plan, its rate basis and its term. The third is the payer-mix sensitivity: coverage with the Medicare share at the trailing twelve months, and with a larger Advantage share at the plan rates. Because the Medicare margin is the only strongly profitable payer, as the operating section shows, a modest shift in mix moves coverage more than a modest shift in occupancy.

Where it fails review. Short-stay files fail when the Medicare share in the pro forma is higher than the facility's history with no new referral agreement behind it; when length of stay is projected at the traditional Medicare median for patients who will mostly be covered by Advantage plans; when the copayment is ignored for patients without secondary coverage; and when quality is assumed rather than shown, since hospitals and plans steer patients by star rating and readmission performance. With no published short-stay benchmark, these files rest on the operator's own unit history.

How it earns. MedPAC counts about 27% of skilled nursing facilities as rural, and rural freestanding facilities provided about 14% of fee-for-service Medicare stays in 2024 [^1]. Rural facilities are smaller and lower in volume, and MedPAC finds that low-volume facilities have lower margins, with its smallest size band far below the facilities of 100 to 199 beds shown in the operating section [^1]. Their payer mix leans further toward Medicaid, which puts the state rate at the center of the file.

The swing bed competitor. A critical access hospital, capped at 25 inpatient beds, can use any of them as a swing bed providing skilled nursing level care, and Medicare pays those days at 101% of reasonable cost [^54]. MedPAC estimates the swing bed payment at about $2,800 a day in 2024 against about $540 paid for swing-bed days at the skilled nursing rate in short-term acute hospitals [^1]. Swing beds handle about 4% of skilled nursing care, mostly in critical access hospitals [^1], but in a rural county they can be the main short-stay competitor, and they are paid on a different basis. A rural study should name every critical access hospital within the trade area, its swing bed volume and its discharge pattern, and should not assume that short-stay patients will move from the hospital to the nursing facility.

How lenders underwrite it. Rural facilities have the widest set of lenders. HUD's Section 232 program applies on the same terms as anywhere else. SBA lends when the operator owns the business, under the eligibility and passive-business tests in the capital section. USDA's Business and Industry guarantee applies where the site is outside any city or town of more than 50,000 people [^53], and Part 5001 requires an independent feasibility study for a guarantee over $1,000,000 to a new business [^52]. USDA's Community Facilities guarantee requires a financial feasibility report for every guarantee, prepared by a firm acceptable to the Agency, and sets its own occupancy ceiling for skilled nursing in that report [^52]; for fiscal 2026 the Community Facilities guarantee is 80% [^51].

Where it fails review. Rural files fail on four points. They count county population without the competing swing beds, home health and assisted living; budget staff at wages the local market cannot supply; assume a Medicaid rate increase the state has not made; or forget that residents of a closed facility usually find another one a short drive away [^1], so census gained from a neighbor's closure can leave again.

How it earns. A replacement facility moves an existing license and its beds out of an obsolete building into a new one, usually in the same planning area, sometimes downsizing and sometimes adding a few beds; one listed operator's 2026 California replacement added 15 beds to the old count [^55]. The earnings case is not new demand. It is that a new building with private rooms and modern systems can hold or raise census, lift the payer mix toward short-stay and private-pay patients, and improve the inspection record that drives referrals. The cost-report survey shows the stakes of quality: average occupancy ran from 77.2% at 1-star facilities to 87.6% at 5-star ones in fiscal 2025, with operating margins rising alongside [^6]. A replacement study should show where the subject sits on that range today and what evidence supports its move.

What it costs. No published source gives a replacement cost per bed. The hard cost evidence is the Weitz per square foot series in the development section, which excludes land, sitework, equipment and soft costs. A replacement budget adds land, demolition or reuse of the old building, the cost of moving residents, any bed purchases, and an operating deficit for the period between opening and stabilized census. HUD's Section 223(d) operating loss loans cover losses in the first two years after an insured first mortgage [^42], which gives a reasonable outer bound for the ramp a lender may be asked to fund. Months to stabilization for nursing facilities are not published.

How lenders underwrite it. HUD is the main long-term lender for new nursing construction, and its volume is small: of the 337 Section 232 loans closed in fiscal 2025, only seven were new construction or other types [^36]. A new construction or substantial rehabilitation loan needs the state certificate of need or an accepted need study [^41] and a complete market study inside the appraisal, with monthly cash flows to stabilization [^40]. The certificate of need is the core document: in a state with a surplus, such as Michigan with 65 planning areas in surplus [^22], a replacement can relocate beds but cannot add them. SBA can finance a smaller replacement for an owner-operator under the special purpose equity tiers in the capital section, but the combined SBA ceiling limits it to small projects.

Where it fails review. Replacement files fail when the bed transfer or relocation approval is assumed rather than in hand; when the census forecast ignores the residents who will not move to a new location; when the ramp is funded only by the borrower's projections and not by an operating reserve; when construction cost comes from a dated quote; and when the old building's disposition is left out, so the sponsor carries two buildings longer than the budget allows.

  1. The Medicare margin read as the facility margin

    Fee-for-service Medicare earned freestanding facilities 24.4% in 2024, but on a minority of days, and all other business together lost money [^1]. A pro forma that raises the Medicare share without a referral agreement, or capitalizes a Medicare-heavy year, overstates coverage.

  2. One occupancy figure taken from the wrong base

    NIC's metro figure, the cost-report median and MedPAC's median measure different samples [^5,6,1]. A study that borrows the highest one for a rural or low-rated facility, or extends the 2023 rebound across the loan term, has not shown its census.

  3. Beds assumed, not authorized

    In certificate of need states the beds are a license condition, and HUD's need test asks for the certificate or an accepted study [^1,41]. A new or replacement project without the bed approval in the file has not shown that it can open.

  4. A Medicaid rate held up by hope

    States have cut and reversed rate increases for fiscal 2026, and the 2025 budget law reduces federal Medicaid spending [^15,16]. A pro forma that grows the Medicaid rate without the state's methodology and rate letter leaves the largest payer unpriced.

  5. The building underwritten, the operator ignored

    The sector's losses came from operators: a Chapter 11 filing with $708.5 million of secured debt, and a REIT's $15.5 million write-off after a tenant raised going-concern doubt [^56,57], even while that landlord's core portfolio covered rent 1.57x at September 30, 2025 [^58]. A file without the operator's audited statements, related-party agreements and rent coverage misses the main risk.

  6. A passive structure presented as an operating business

    SBA treated a management agreement giving the manager sole discretion as making the borrower an ineligible passive business [^45]. A sponsor who owns the building and hires a manager to run everything needs a structure that keeps budget, bank account and staff control with the borrower.

  7. The change of ownership left off the timetable

    No SBA guidance addresses the Medicare change of ownership or provider agreement assignment, and the research found none. A purchase that closes before the state license and CMS approvals are scheduled can lose billing for weeks; the study should show the timetable and fund the gap.

  8. Value at the record price

    The average reached $124,200 per bed in mid-2026 [^32]. Sizing a loan to a per-bed average, or to a survey cap rate without a higher-rate case, lends against the top of a cycle.

  9. Quality data taken at face value

    Star ratings drive census and Express Lane eligibility, but the HHS Inspector General found widespread underreporting of falls with major injury [^59]. A study should read the inspection reports themselves, not only the rating.
